## 1. Latin square design
- Title: Latin square design · slug: latin-square-design · STANDALONE, topic "Statistical inference". Lead: "a design that removes two crossed nuisance classifications by placing each of $g$ treatments once in every row and once in every column of a $g\times g$ grid". Aliases: "Latin square", "Latin squares", "Graeco-Latin square".
- Why: backlog #25. STAT 503 L4 (scoping only); autocomplete gives "latin square design example / in statistics / formula / psychology / in bioequivalence". No slug exists, only 6 passing mentions.
- Key subtopics: blocking in two directions as two RCBs at once; $g^2$ units; the additive model $y_{ijk}=\mu+\alpha_i+\beta_j+\gamma_k+\varepsilon_{ijk}$ and why the usual formulas still work (balance makes row, column and treatment contrasts orthogonal); the error df $(g-1)(g-2)$, derived by counting, and why it is small for small $g$; randomisation (a standard square at random, then permute rows, columns and letters) and why a systematic cyclic square is not enough; no row/column×treatment interaction as the key assumption; replicated squares and reusing row or column blocks (with the df); Graeco-Latin squares as a pointer only; the equivalence to a $4^{3-1}$ fraction.
- Motivating example: Fisher's field with fertility gradients in both directions (Fisher §31) or Oehlert's goose eggs (site × age). Write it in your own words.
- Worked example (verified, latin_square.py): Oehlert Table 13.2, a 3×3 bioequivalence square (rows = period, columns = subject; A solution, B tablet, C capsule; AUC 1799/2075/1396, 1846/1156/868, 2147/1777/2291 with letters ACB/CBA/BAC). Treatment means A 1481.33, B 1566.33, C 2070.67. SS periods 928005.56, SS subjects 261114.89, SS treatments 608890.89, SS error 8989.56 on 2 df, MSE 4494.78, $F=67.73$ on (2, 2), $p=0.0145$; Tukey MSD 322.46 ($q=8.331$). These exactly match Oehlert Listing 13.1, p. 329. Our own extension, labelled as ours: ignore the period blocking and analyse by subject only, and MSE becomes 234248.8, $F=1.30$, $p=0.37$. Removing period variation is what makes the comparison possible. Also point out that this square is a crossover design and link to crossover-design.
- Your turn (different numbers, your_turn.py): a cyclic 4×4 square (rows = day, columns = operator; rows ABCD/BCDA/CDAB/DABC) with data 57 49 52 50 / 50 50 47 53 / 49 46 53 48 / 48 51 48 49. Answer: treatment means A 53.5, B 48.75, C 50.0, D 47.75; SS rows 24, SS columns 8, SS treatments 75.5, SSE 4.5 on 6 df; MSE 0.75; $F=33.56$ on (3, 6), $p\approx0.0004$.
- Check yourself: (1) Give the df for a 5×5 square. Answer: rows, columns and treatments 4 each, error 12. (2) An operator is unusually good with one treatment. Which assumption fails, and where does that effect end up? Answer: additivity (no block×treatment interaction); it is absorbed into error or distorts the treatment means. (3) Why choose a square at random instead of the cyclic one? Answer: it gives every plot the same chance of every treatment, which is what justifies the error estimate (Fisher §31).
- Citations (≥5, 3 sources):
  - fisher1935_doe §31, pp. 70–72: double restriction; for $g$ from 4 to 8 "singularly reliable"; a 6×6 square's rows and columns can each be rearranged in 720 ways; random choice from the transformation set; each pair of plots not in the same row or column has probability 1/5 of the same treatment (6×6).
  - fisher1935_doe §32, p. 73: the 6×6 ANOVA df 5/5/5/20 and $(s-1)(s-2)$ in general (Table 8).
  - oehlert2010 §13.3, p. 325 (definition, $g^2$ units, rows and columns each form RCBs); p. 326 (drawbacks: exactly $g^2$ units, few error df, hard to find units that block both ways); p. 327 (Table 13.2; Fisher–Yates randomisation; 1, 1, 4, 56 and 9408 standard squares for $g=2$–6); p. 328 (model, orthogonality, ANOVA table, error df); p. 329 (Listing 13.1; don't test the row/column blocks); pp. 330–331 (replicated squares, reused blocks, df).
  - nist_latin_square2002 §5.3.3.2.1: two nuisance factors; the advantages and disadvantages (levels must equal the number of treatments; the model assumes no interactions among blocks or between treatment and blocks; randomisation is more complex); the 4×4 Latin square is equivalent to a $4^{3-1}$ fractional factorial; the model and estimates.
- History: Fisher's agricultural motivation (double elimination of soil heterogeneity, §31), and the Fisher–Yates randomisation as described by Oehlert p. 327. Do not cite Fisher & Yates' tables directly (not opened).

## 2. Crossover design
- Title: Crossover design · slug: crossover-design · STANDALONE, topic "Statistical inference". Lead: "a design in which each subject receives a sequence of treatments in successive periods, so that treatments are compared within subjects while period effects are balanced by randomising the sequences". Aliases: "cross-over design", "crossover trial", "AB/BA design", "two-period crossover", "carryover effect", "washout period".
- Why: backlog #26. STAT 503 §4.6 (scoping only); autocomplete gives "crossover design clinical trial / in research / statistics / study". 0 hits for crossover, carryover or washout in research/content.
- Key subtopics: each subject is their own control, and the gain is the removal of between-subject variance; the AB/BA layout with sequence groups; the period effect and why a plain paired t-test is wrong (W&B p. 276); the model and the analysis through within-subject period differences $D$ (unpaired $t$ between sequences, $\hat\tau=(\bar D_{AB}-\bar D_{BA})/2$) and through sums $C$ (carryover pre-test); washout; why the two-stage "pre-test, then first-period analysis" procedure is anticonservative (W&B p. 280; cite W&B for Freeman's result, not Freeman); efficiency against a parallel design (derive $N_{\text{par}}/N_{\text{cross}}=2(1+\sigma_b^2/\sigma_e^2)$, which gives 6 when $\sigma_b^2=2\sigma_e^2$, matching W&B p. 277); multi-period crossovers as Latin squares; designs balanced for residual effects (each treatment follows every other equally often; $g$ subjects when $g$ is even, $2g$ when odd); the residual-effects model; a Wilcoxon rank-sum version as a pointer.
- Motivating example: two inhalers given to the same asthmatic children in two periods (W&B Box 2 context), or the milk-feed crossover (Oehlert Example 13.12).
- Worked example (verified, crossover.py): W&B Box 2, p. 279 (formoterol A vs salbutamol B, PEF; AB: 310/270, 310/260, 370/300, 410/390, 250/210, 380/350, 330/365; BA: 370/385, 310/400, 380/410, 290/320, 260/340, 90/220). $\bar D_{AB}=30.71$, $\bar D_{BA}=-62.50$; $\hat\tau_{A-B}=46.61$ (PEF units; Box 2 gives no unit, so state none); pooled $s^2_D=1500.81$, SE($\hat\tau$) = 10.78, $t=4.32$ on 11 df, $p=0.0012$, 95% CI (22.9, 70.3); estimated period 1 − period 2 = −15.89. Carryover pre-test on the sums: $t=0.18$, $p=0.86$. This matches W&B's printed conclusion (formoterol better, no evidence of carryover). Their test statistics are images that I couldn't extract, so Peer Review should treat these values as ours.
- Your turn (different data, your_turn.py): AB group (50,62), (48,58), (55,65), (47,59); BA group (52,61), (45,56), (50,59), (53,64). Answer: $\bar D_{AB}=-11$, $\bar D_{BA}=-10$, so $\hat\tau_{A-B}=-0.5$ and period 1 − period 2 = −10.5. The treatment difference is negligible and the period effect is large. Unpaired $t$ on the $D$s: $t=-1.22$, $p=0.27$. Ignoring sequence gives the same −0.5 because the groups are balanced, but the period effect inflates its variance ($p=0.90$).
- Check yourself: (1) With unequal sequence groups, what does a paired t-test on all A−B differences estimate? Answer: τ plus a multiple of the period effect, because the period effect enters the two groups with opposite signs (W&B p. 277). (2) Why do sums test for carryover and differences test for treatment? Answer: carryover appears only in period 2 for one sequence, so it shifts the sequence means of the sums, while the treatment and period effects cancel in the sums; derive this algebraically. (3) If $\sigma_b^2=\sigma_e^2$, how many more patients does a parallel trial need? Answer: 4 times as many.
- Citations (≥5, 2 sources):
  - wellek_blettner2012 p. 276: the paired-t error and the washout requirement; p. 277: periods, the period effect and treatment–period confounding, randomisation to sequence groups, the unpaired test on within-subject differences, the pre-test on sums, efficiency (the 6× example); p. 278: Box 1 (notation and test steps), the Wilcoxon rank-sum alternative, replicated four-period crossovers for bioequivalence, mixed models for multiperiod designs; p. 279: Box 2 data; p. 280: the two-stage first-period analysis is anticonservative (refs 19 and 23 = Grizzle 1965, Freeman 1989, cited only through W&B).
  - oehlert2010 p. 326: crossover as a Latin square with subject and period blocks; pp. 327–329: the bioequivalence square; p. 338: residual (carryover) effects, washout, designs balanced for residual effects, with $g$ even needing $g$ subjects and $g$ odd needing $2g$; p. 339: the residual-effects model, with residual effects not orthogonal to direct effects; pp. 340–341: milk example (Cochran, Autrey and Cannon 1941 via Oehlert), log scale, residual effects significant; p. 342: implementing $r_i$ terms and repeating the last treatment in an extra period; p. 441: crossover as repeated measures (order × period).
- History: Cochran, Autrey and Cannon's 1941 dairy crossover (via Oehlert p. 340), and the Grizzle/Freeman debate over two-stage analysis (via W&B p. 280). Mark anything beyond that as needing Jones & Kenward, which is not opened.
- Links to: paired-samples, two-sample-t-test, repeated-measures, randomised-controlled-trial, blinding-and-placebo, latin-square-design, randomised-block-design, linear-mixed-model, wilcoxon-rank-sum-test, power-function, effect-size; plus wilcoxon-signed-rank-test only once #78 merges. ## 3. Split-plot design
- Title: Split-plot design · slug: split-plot-design · STANDALONE, topic "Statistical inference". Lead: "a factorial experiment with two sizes of experimental unit and two randomisations, used when some factors are much harder to vary than others". Aliases: "split plot", "split-plot experiment", "whole plot", "subplot", "hard-to-change factor".
- Why: backlog #27. STAT 503 L14 (scoping only); autocomplete gives "split plot design example / anova table / degree of freedom / in agriculture". 0 hits in the repo.
- Key subtopics: two unit sizes and two randomisations (whole plots carry the hard-to-change factor; subplots are randomised within each whole plot); three equivalent views (restricted randomisation; whole-plot factor confounded with incomplete blocks; Yates' analogy to confounding); the model $y_{ijk}=\mu+\alpha_i+\eta_{k(i)}+\beta_j+(\alpha\beta)_{ij}+\varepsilon_{k(ij)}$ with two error terms; F denominators (A over WPE; B and AB over SPE); df $a(n-1)$ and $a(b-1)(n-1)$; why subplot comparisons are more precise; you cannot recognise a split plot from the data table, only from the randomisation; blocked whole plots; the cost argument; what goes wrong if a split plot is analysed as a CRD (random-run-order / not-reset); split-plot as a mixed model (link).
- Motivating example: furnace temperature (hard to change) × coating (easy) for steel bar corrosion (Jones & Nachtsheim Table 1, from Box et al. 2005), or Oehlert's irrigation × corn variety.
- Worked example (verified, split_plot.py): JN Table 1, p. 341, six whole plots (temperatures 360, 370, 380, 380, 370, 360), each with four coatings. Means: 360 → 56.63, 370 → 110.25, 380 → 136.50; C1 94.67, C2 90.17, C3 95.67, C4 124.00. SS: temperature 26519.25 (2 df), WPE 14439.63 (3 df, MS 4813.21), coating 4289.13 (3 df), T×C 3269.75 (6 df), SPE 1120.88 (9 df, MS 124.54). Split-plot $F$: temperature 2.75, $p=0.209$; coating 11.48, $p=0.0020$; T×C 4.38, $p=0.024$. The naive CRD analysis (pooled 12-df error) gives temperature $p=0.0026$, coating 0.386 and T×C 0.852. These reproduce JN's p-value table on p. 344 exactly, so the conclusions reverse.
- Your turn (different data, your_turn.py): four fields, with irrigation I1 on fields 1–2 and I2 on fields 3–4, and two fertilisers per field: (10, 14), (12, 18), (15, 19), (19, 25). Answer: irrigation means 13.5 and 19.5, $SS_I=72$, whole-plot error SS 34 on 2 df (MS 17), $F_I=4.24$ on (1, 2), $p=0.18$; fertiliser means 14 and 19, $SS_F=50$, I×F SS 0, subplot error SS 2 on 2 df (MS 1), $F_F=50$ on (1, 2), $p=0.019$. - Check yourself: (1) An oven's temperature is set once per bake and four cake recipes are baked in each run. Which factor is whole-plot and what is the whole plot? Answer: temperature, and the oven run. (2) With $a=3$ whole-plot levels, $n=4$ whole plots per level and $b=5$ subplot levels in a CRD at the whole-plot level, what are the WPE and SPE df? ## 4. Two-level factorial design
- Title: Two-level factorial design · slug: two-level-factorial-design · STANDALONE, topic "Statistical inference". Lead: "an experiment that runs every combination of $k$ factors at two levels each, so that every main effect and interaction is a single contrast among the $2^k$ treatment means". Aliases: "2^k factorial design", "two-series factorial", "full factorial design", "Yates' algorithm", "half-normal plot". I'd hold back a bare "factorial design" alias unless you want it here.
- Why: backlog #28. STAT 503 L5–6 (scoping only); autocomplete gives "factorial design example / in research / clinical trial / in biostatistics". There is no slug, and the existing pages cover only the two-factor concepts.
- Key subtopics: notation ((1), a, b, ab, …) and standard order; the ±1 table and interaction columns as elementwise products; each effect as one df; the effect conventions, which must be stated explicitly (Yates 1937 and NIST use high mean − low mean; Oehlert's $\hat\alpha_2$ is half of that, p. 238), because otherwise students mismatch software output; orthogonality and why each effect uses all the runs (Fisher §38: the precision is as if all 96 trials went to each factor); one-at-a-time versus factorial (Oehlert pp. 170–171); Yates' algorithm; unreplicated designs (pool high-order interactions, as in Yates §6; half-normal plot; Lenth's PSE; effect sparsity); the run count explodes, so fractional designs (NIST §5.3.3.3, a pointer only); blocking/confounding as a pointer.
- Motivating example: Fisher's four-ingredient 2^4 mixture with 6 tests per mixture (§38), retold in your own words.
- Worked example (verified, twok.py): Oehlert Table 10.4 pacemaker substrates (log-scale means in standard order 4.388, 2.831, 4.360, 3.107, 4.330, 3.268, 4.336, 2.471). Yates' algorithm column 3 is 29.091, −5.737, −0.543, −0.499, −0.281, −0.117, −1.039, −1.107; Oehlert prints 29.090, −5.735, −.544, −.500, −.282, −.117, −1.039, −1.108 from unrounded means. Effects (÷8): A −0.717, B −0.068, AB −0.062, C −0.035, AC −0.015, BC −0.130, ABC −0.138. Lenth: median |effect| 0.0679, $s_0=0.102$, only A exceeds $2.5s_0$, PSE = 1.5 × 0.0651 = 0.098, $t_A=-7.34$ on 7/3 df, $p\approx0.012$, which matches Oehlert's "about .01" (p. 242). The half-normal ordering puts A alone at the top. **Flag:** Oehlert's text (p. 241) defines the cutoff as $2.5s_0$, but his example (p. 241) uses 2.5 × median = .17. The conclusion is the same here, but the page should use $2.5s_0$ (Lenth's definition as Oehlert states it) and not reproduce the inconsistency. Lenth (1989) itself was not opened.
- Your turn (different data, your_turn.py): an unreplicated 2^3 in standard order: 20, 30, 22, 32, 21, 39, 23, 41. Answer (high mean − low mean): A 14, B 2, C 5, AC 4, AB = BC = ABC = 0. The A effect is 10 at low C and 18 at high C, so AC = (18 − 10)/2 = 4.
- Check yourself: (1) How many runs and three-factor interactions does a 2^5 have? Answer: 32 and 10. (2) What is the sign of the ABC column for combination ac? Answer: − (Oehlert Table 10.3). (3) A half-normal plot of an unreplicated 2^4 shows 13 effects on a line and 2 far above it. What assumption licenses reading the 2 as real, and when does it fail? Answer: effect sparsity; it fails when many effects are active.
- Citations (≥5, 4 sources):
  - oehlert2010 §10.4, p. 236: definition, $2^k$, notation; p. 237: Table 10.2, standard order; p. 238: effects as contrasts, SS, total effect = $2\hat\alpha_2$ with divisor $2^{k-1}$; p. 239: Table 10.3 and interaction columns as products; Yates' algorithm with $k2^k$ operations against $2^{2k}$; p. 240: Table 10.4; pp. 240–241: single replicates, effect sparsity, half-normal plot (Daniel 1959 via Oehlert), Lenth's PSE with $(2^k-1)/3$ df; p. 242: Figure 10.1 and $p\approx.01$; pp. 170–171: advantages of factorials over one-at-a-time.
  - fisher1935_doe §38, pp. 95–101: every trial informs each main question; interactions are symmetric; the 15 = 4 + 6 + 4 + 1 components; ANOVA with 15 treatment df and 80 error df.
  - yates1937_factorial §2d, pp. 11–12 (Table 4 sits on p. 12): effects as the mean of 4 minus the mean of the other 4, and the sign table built by products; §3, pp. 14–15: the systematic standard-order method (Yates' algorithm); §6, p. 27 (OCR page breaks are rough; confirm on the scan): single replication, with error estimated from high-order interactions (22 of the 63 df in a 2^6 involve four or more factors).
