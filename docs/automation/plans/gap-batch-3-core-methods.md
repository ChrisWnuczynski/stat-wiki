## 1. McNemar's test
- Title: McNemar's test · slug: mcnemar-s-test · STANDALONE, topic "Statistical inference". Lead: "a test of whether two proportions measured on the same subjects (or matched pairs) are equal, which conditions on the pairs that changed and compares the two directions of change". Aliases: "McNemar test", "McNemar's chi-squared test", "McNemar mid-p test", "test for correlated proportions". Don't use "paired proportions" or "matched pairs" as aliases: paired-samples already takes "for matched pairs".
- Why: backlog #5. Autocomplete gives "mcnemar test vs chi square / vs paired t test". UQ STAT1201 M11 (categorical data) is scoping only. It's the natural binary sequel to paired-samples and sign-test.
- Key subtopics: the paired 2×2 table and why $p_1-p_2=(b-c)/N$ depends only on the discordant cells; conditioning on $n=b+c$ gives $b\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,1/2)$ under $H_0$, so the exact test is a sign test on the changers (link sign-test, binomial-test); the asymptotic statistic $z=(b-c)/\sqrt{b+c}$ and $\chi^2=z^2$ on 1 df; Edwards' continuity correction $(|b-c|-1)^2/(b+c)$; the mid-p version (exact p minus the point probability, two-sided); why treating the margins as two independent samples is wrong (ignores the correlation; McNemar's standard error $\sqrt{(a+d)/N}$ in his notation); what the test does **not** say (the concordant pairs carry no information on the difference, but they matter for interpreting its size); small-sample guidance and how it changed (McNemar's own A+D thresholds vs Fagerland's simulation); Cochran's Q as the pointer for 3+ conditions; links to sensitivity-and-specificity (comparing two tests on the same patients is a paired design).
- Notation flag: McNemar labels the discordant cells A (yes→no) and D (no→yes); Fagerland uses $n_{12}$, $n_{21}$. Use one notation ($b$, $c$ for the discordant counts is fine) and say so once.
- Motivating example: McNemar's own movie-and-opinion setting (p. 56). The same people answer yes/no before and after a film; the question is whether the proportion of yeses changed, and the two answers from one person are not independent. Use your own words.
- Worked example (verified, mcnemar.py): Bentur et al. via Fagerland Table 1, 21 children; AHR before 2/21, after 8/21; discordant 1 (AHR→none) and 7 (none→AHR). $z=-2.121$, $\chi^2=4.5$; asymptotic $p=0.0339$; with CC $p=0.0771$; exact conditional $p=0.0703$; mid-p $p=0.0391$; exact unconditional $p=0.0353$. **All five match Fagerland Table 6 (p. 6) to 4 dp**, and the unconditional one comes from my own Suissa–Shuster maximisation. Teaching point: at $n=8$ discordant pairs the choice of version moves $p$ across 0.05; the page should say why (discreteness, conservatism) without picking a "winner" beyond what Fagerland's simulation supports.
- Second illustration (verified): McNemar's Table 28 (p. 225; Stanford–Binet items 1 and 2, $N=100$, pass rates 49% and 68%, discordant 10 and 29). $\chi^2=(29-10)^2/39=9.26$ and the critical ratio is 3.04 (printed p. 230; ours 9.256 and 3.042). Our own contrast, labelled as ours: analysing the margins as two independent samples gives $\chi^2=7.43$ ($z=2.73$). The paired analysis is more sensitive here because the item results are positively correlated.
- Your turn (different data): Cavo et al. via Fagerland Table 2 (p. 2), 161 patients, complete response 65/161 before and 75/161 after consolidation; discordant 6 and 16. Answer: $z=-2.132$, $\chi^2=4.55$, asymptotic $p=0.033$, CC $p=0.055$, exact $p=0.0525$, mid-p $p=0.0347$ (matches Table 6).
- Check yourself: (1) Only 3 discordant pairs, all in one direction. Can the exact two-sided test reach $p<0.05$? Answer: no; the smallest possible $p$ is $2\times(1/2)^3=0.25$. (2) Why do the 39 pupils who passed both items not appear in the statistic? Answer: under the null they tell us nothing about which direction of change is more likely; only changers can differ in direction. (3) A student runs a chi-squared test of independence on the paired 2×2 table. What hypothesis did they test instead? Answer: association between the first and second responses, not equality of the two margins.

## 2. Regression diagnostics
- Title: Regression diagnostics · slug: regression-diagnostics · STANDALONE, topic "Data analysis". Lead: "the checks of a fitted regression for what its summary statistics hide: residual plots for curvature and changing spread, leverage for unusual predictor positions, and influence measures such as Cook's distance for cases that move the fit". Aliases: "Cook's distance", "Cook's D", "high-leverage point", "influential observation", "influential point", "externally studentised residual", "residual plot". Do not alias bare "leverage", "hat matrix" or "outlier": they are used generally across the site, and outlier has its own page.
- Why: backlog #7. OpenIntro §8.3 and §9.3 TOC, Statology index and autocomplete ("residual plot patterns", "cook's distance threshold") are scoping only.
- Key subtopics: why $R^2$ and the fitted line can't validate a model (Anscombe); residuals vs fitted values as the primary plot, and what curvature or a fan shape means (pointer to a future heteroscedasticity page; no Breusch–Pagan claims); the hat matrix $H=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$ (Cook & Weisberg write $V$; say so), $\operatorname{tr}H=p'$, $1/n\le h_{ii}\le1$ with an intercept, and the simple-regression formula $h_{ii}=1/n+(x_i-\bar x)^2/S_{xx}$; link error-and-residual for $\operatorname{Var}(e_i)=\sigma^2(1-h_{ii})$ rather than re-deriving; why large leverage hides outliers (residual pulled to zero); internal $r_i$ vs external $t_i$ studentisation with $t_i\sim t_{n-p'-1}$; Cook's $D_i=r_i^2h_{ii}/[p'(1-h_{ii})]$ as residual × potential, its confidence-ellipsoid meaning and the "compare with $F(p',n-p')$ median / $D>1$" convention, with Cook & Weisberg's warning that $D$ is not $F$-distributed; diagnostics flag cases to investigate, not delete.
- Motivating example: Anscombe's quartet (p. 19). Four data sets with identical printed summaries (y = 3 + 0.5x, $R^2=0.667$, residual SS 13.75 on 9 df, SE of slope 0.118) and four different stories. Our code reproduces the printed summary for all four sets, and the set-3 line without the outlier, $y=4.006+0.345x$ against Anscombe's printed $4+0.346x$. Set 4: the $x=19$ case has $h=1$, so its residual is 0 and $D$ is undefined (0/0). The slope is determined by that one case (Anscombe p. 19; Cook & Weisberg p. 13).
- Worked example (verified, regression_diagnostics.py): Cook & Weisberg's rat data (Table 3.5.2, p. 119; 19 rats; body weight, liver weight, relative dose, proportion of dose retained). Reproduces Table 3.5.3(b) (p. 120): none of the three simple regressions has $|t|>1$ (0.63, 0.86, 0.96), but the full model gives $t=-2.66$ (printed −2.67, a data-rounding difference) for body weight and $2.74$ for dose. $D_3=0.93$ and $h_{33}=0.85$, and every other $D_i<0.27$ (ours 0.273, printed "bigger than 0.27"; flag the rounding). $r_3=0.81$, so case 3 is not an outlier in residual terms. Its influence is all potential. Deleting case 3 makes every $|t|<1$ for the slopes (ours −0.42, 0.48, 0.40). The explanation (p. 121): rat 3 (190 g) got the full dose 1.00 where the dosing rule implied less, against rat 8 (195 g) at 0.98. Mean leverage $p'/n=4/19=0.21$, so 0.85 is about four times the average. $F(0.5;4,15)=0.878$, putting $D_3$ near the 53rd percentile.
- Also verified: Cook & Weisberg Example 3.5.1 (p. 118, Table 3.5.1). With $p'=3$, the pairs $(h,r)=(0.90,1.000),(0.75,1.732),(0.25,5.196),(0.10,9.000)$ all give $D=3.0$: two cases are influential by potential and two by residual.
- Your turn (different numbers, your_turn.py): $x=1,2,3,4,5,10$; $y=1.2,1.9,3.2,3.8,5.1,7.0$. Answer: fit $\hat y=1.012+0.645x$; $h_{66}=1/6+(10-25/6)^2/S_{xx}=0.836$; $r_6=-1.91$; $D_6=9.33$ (all other $D_i\le0.28$). Without case 6 the slope is 0.970 and the prediction at $x=10$ is 9.83, against the observed 7.0.
- Check yourself: (1) Two cases have the same Cook's distance, one with $h=0.9$ and $r=1$, the other with $h=0.1$ and $r=9$. Are they influential for the same reason? Answer: no; the first through its position in predictor space, the second through its residual. (2) Why can't the residual of a case with $h_{ii}=1$ reveal anything? Answer: the fit passes through it exactly, so its residual is 0 whatever its response. (3) A case has $D_i=0.93$. Should it be deleted? Answer: not on that basis; it should be investigated (data error, different regime), and the conclusions reported with and without it.

## 3. 68–95–99.7 rule
- Title: 68–95–99.7 rule · slug: 68-95-99-7-rule (build_wiki.slug output) · STANDALONE, topic "Probability distributions" (or DISTRIBUTION_CONCEPTS; your call). Lead: "the statement that a normal random variable falls within one, two and three standard deviations of its mean with probabilities of about 0.68, 0.95 and 0.997". Aliases: "empirical rule", "68-95-99.7 rule", "68–95–99.7 rule", "68 95 99.7 rule". Don't alias "three-sigma rule": the phrase is also used for other bounds (e.g. unimodal-distribution inequalities), and statistical-process-control uses "three-sigma limits".
- Why: backlog #6. Penn State STAT 500 tag "Empirical rule", USyd DATA1001 normal model and autocomplete ("empirical rule", "68 95 99.7 rule") are scoping only.
- Key subtopics: $P(|X-\mu|<k\sigma)=2\Phi(k)-1$ for any $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, which follows from standardisation (link standard-score), giving 0.6827, 0.9545, 0.9973; 1.96 against 2; tails beyond 4, 5 and 6 SD; it is a property of the normal model, not of data. Counterexamples (ours): Exponential(1) gives 0.8647, 0.9502, 0.9817; Chebyshev's distribution-free floors are 0, 3/4 and 8/9 (link chebyshev-s-inequality), and the 3/4 floor is attained. Using the rule on a sample (McNemar's "usual symmetrical bell-shaped distribution" wording) as an approximation that needs checking (link normal-quantile-plot, shapiro-wilk-test once #78 merges). Discrete counts: binomial with continuity correction (link central-limit-theorem, limit-theorems).
- Motivating example: OpenIntro Guided Practice 4.21 (§4.1.5). SAT scores roughly $N(1100,200)$: about 95% score between 700 and 1500, and about 47.5% between 1100 and 1500, with no table needed. Use your own words and numbers if preferred.
- Worked example (verified, empirical_rule.py): de Moivre's 3600 fair-coin trials (1756, pp. 246–248). $\sigma=\sqrt{3600}/2=30$. His printed probabilities are 0.682688 for 1770–1830 (±1σ), 0.95428 for ±2σ and 0.99874 for ±3σ. Exact binomial gives 0.690688, 0.956285 and 0.997449; the plain normal values are 0.682689, 0.954500 and 0.997300; with continuity correction 0.690688, 0.956270 and 0.997444. Points for the page: de Moivre's ±1σ value is the normal value to 6 dp; his 3σ decimal 0.99874 is off by 0.0014. His printed odds of "369 to 1" (p. 248) correspond to 0.9973, so the decimal and the odds disagree on the page itself. I confirmed both on the scan (dm-270.png). State that carefully as a historical observation.
- Your turn (different numbers): Uniform(0,1), $\sigma=1/\sqrt{12}=0.2887$. What fractions lie within 1, 2 and 3 SD? Answer: 0.5774, 1, 1. Fewer than 68% lie within 1 SD, and none fall outside 2 SD.
- Check yourself: (1) Adult heights $N(170,10)$ cm: roughly what fraction exceed 190 cm by the rule, and what is the exact value? Answer: about 2.5%; exactly 0.0228. (2) Chebyshev promises at least 75% within 2 SD. Can a distribution have exactly 75%? ## 4. Analysis of covariance
- Title: Analysis of covariance · slug: analysis-of-covariance · STANDALONE, topic "Statistical inference" (field "experimental design", like batch 2). Lead: "an analysis of variance that adds a measured covariate to the model, so that treatments are compared at a common covariate value and the error is reduced by the within-treatment regression on the covariate". Aliases: "ANCOVA", "covariance analysis", "covariate-adjusted means", "parallel-lines model". Don't alias bare "covariate" or "adjusted means".
- Why: backlog #10 (swap; reasons above). Autocomplete "ancova vs anova", "ancova assumptions"; UQ STAT1201 M9 (multiple predictors) is scoping only.
- Key subtopics: covariates as predictive responses measured with, not before, the outcome; the parallel-lines model $y_{ij}=\mu+\alpha_i+\beta x_{ij}+\varepsilon_{ij}$, $\sum\alpha_i=0$; ANCOVA as the single-line vs parallel-lines comparison on $g-1$ and $N-g-1$ df; Fisher's computation, slope = error sum of products / error sum of squares of $x$, and the adjusted error SS (show it equals the regression SSE: 411.0 both ways); adjusted means $\hat\mu+\hat\alpha_i+\hat\beta\bar x_{\cdot\cdot}$ and the adjustment $\hat\beta(\bar x_{i\cdot}-\bar x_{\cdot\cdot})$; two benefits (precision; comparison at a common $x$) kept distinct; the key assumption that treatments don't affect the covariate (Fisher's plant-number example; Oehlert's awkward keyboard) and the $\tilde x$ remedy; linearity and common slope, and the lattice of models with the separate-slopes test; randomisation makes covariate imbalance small (Fisher's $1:\sqrt{10}$ remark); adjusted means have unequal SEs; extrapolation (Oehlert's "−34 hours of pain"); the Type II/III point for factorials. Avoid observational-data "adjustment fixes confounding" claims; none of the opened sources supports them.
- Motivating example: Fisher's feeding experiment (§55, pp. 163–165). Pigs of the same age enter at different weights. Randomisation keeps the test valid, but measuring the initial weight lets that variation be removed. Use your own words.
- Worked example (verified, ancova.py): Oehlert's keyboarding pain (Table 17.1, p. 456; 12 staff, 3 keyboards, $x$ = hours keyboarding, $y$ = hours of pain). This **exactly reproduces Listing 17.1 (p. 458)**. Covariate ANOVA $F=1.45$, $p=0.286$. ANCOVA: treatments adjusted for $x$, SS 1195.8, $F=11.64$ on (2, 8), $p=0.004$; $x$ adjusted SS 1273.5, $F=24.79$; MSE 51.4; slope 1.8199 (SE 0.3655); effects $-48.21$, $14.399$, $-4.671$; adjusted means 73.57, 54.50, 49.44 (SE 3.641, 3.722, 3.943); Tukey 2−1 interval $(-33.59,-4.55)$, centre −19.07. Ignoring $x$: MSE 187.2 ("about 3.6 times", p. 459; ours 3.64), $F=6.74$, $p=0.016$; raw means 76.75, 59.50, 41.25. The adjustments $\hat\beta(\bar x_{i\cdot}-\bar x)$ are 3.185, 5.005 and −8.19, which is exactly raw − adjusted. Fisher's pooled slope $E_{xy}/E_{xx}=1.8199$. Also verified: the equal-slopes test $F=1.24$ on (2, 6), $p=0.354$, which matches Oehlert's Listing 17.3 (p. 464).
- Your turn (constructed numbers, label as such; your_turn.py): two teaching methods, pre-test $x$ and post-test $y$. Method A $x$ = 10, 12, 14, 16, $y$ = 20, 23, 25, 29; Method B $x$ = 14, 16, 18, 20, $y$ = 21, 24, 26, 29. Answer: raw means 24.25 and 25.00 (B − A = 0.75); pooled within slope 1.375; adjusted means 27.00 and 22.25 (B − A = −4.75, SE 0.45, $t=-10.56$ on 5 df). Ignoring $x$, $t=0.30$. The sign flips because B's pre-test mean is 4 points higher. A follow-up in the hidden answer: is the adjustment trustworthy here? Only if the methods could not have changed the pre-test and the groups overlap in $x$.
- Check yourself: (1) Keyboard 3's raw mean is 41.25 but its adjusted mean is 49.44. Why did it go up? Answer: its staff typed less ($\bar x_3=54.5<59$), so at the common 59 hours the model predicts more pain: $1.82\times4.5\approx8.19$. (2) A treatment makes people type less. What does comparing at equal keyboarding time do to its effect? Answer: it hides the part of the effect that works through the covariate. (3) Four treatments, five units each, one covariate: what are the error df? Answer: $20-4-1=15$.
