# StatWiki Editorial Style Guide (from Chris, 19 Nov 2026)

## 1. Editorial philosophy
StatWiki is a rigorous, accessible encyclopedia of statistics, probability and related mathematical disciplines. Its purpose is not merely to communicate facts, definitions and formulas, but to develop a coherent understanding of the mathematical principles underlying them. Writing should be intellectually curious, analytical and questioning: establish not only what is true, but why, under what assumptions, and how it relates to broader statistical ideas.
Central principle: Explain the mathematics without obscuring the reasoning. Combine the precision of a mathematical textbook with the conceptual accessibility of an excellent university lecturer.

## 2. Tone and voice
Formal, authoritative, intellectually engaging academic voice. Precise; Explanatory (understanding over results); Investigative; Accessible (progressive, without sacrificing rigour); Sceptical (assumptions, limitations, misinterpretations); Direct (clear economical sentences); Intellectually honest (distinguish established results, conditional conclusions, approximations, interpretations).
Avoid slang, profanity, excessive informality, conversational filler, unnecessary rhetorical flourishes. Personality comes through clarity, insight and engagement, not humour or colloquialism.

## 3. Explanation through first principles
Where appropriate, introduce a concept via the problem it addresses: 1 Motivation, 2 Definition, 3 Mechanism, 4 Derivation, 5 Interpretation, 6 Limitations, 7 Connections. Integrate these naturally rather than imposing an identical template on every article. Don't present formulas as isolated facts when their reasoning can be explained.

## 4. Mathematical rigour
Correctness over simplicity. Define symbols before use; state assumptions; consistent notation. For theorems/results: give conditions; distinguish definitions from propositions needing proof; explain nontrivial steps; derive when it helps understanding; distinguish exact from approximate; clarify interpretation and applicability. Don't drop essential qualifications to simplify. Intuition illuminates the mathematics, never substitutes for it.

## 5. Conceptual intuition
Pair formal explanation with careful intuition (concrete examples, analogies, geometric/probabilistic interpretations), clearly distinguished from formal justification. E.g. the LLN is not just "averaging out": state the convergence statement, assumptions, and the meaning of increasing n.

## 6. Critical examination of assumptions
Assumptions are integral, not footnotes. Explain which conclusions depend on which assumptions; explore relaxations/violations where useful. Distinguish: correlation vs causation; statistical vs practical significance; estimation vs prediction; probability about data vs about parameters; necessary vs sufficient; mathematical properties vs empirical claims; model-based conclusions vs reality. No procedure is universally valid.

## 7. Anticipating misconceptions
Explain why a misinterpretation is wrong, contrast with the correct statement, use counterexamples. Aim: independent statistical reasoning.

## 8. Progressive depth
Start with the central concept and motivation; increase sophistication gradually. Accessibility doesn't mean avoiding advanced maths; provide foundations. Introductory treatment must be consistent with the general one; no oversimplifications that must be unlearned.

## 9. Connections between concepts
Present statistics as connected: probability foundations, inference/estimation, alternative approaches, computation, applications, general structures (e.g. MLE -> likelihood, optimisation, Fisher information, asymptotics, Bayes). Connections should clarify, not distract.

## 10. Examples and applications
Worked examples: state the problem, assumptions, calculations, interpretation. Where useful, show how changing an assumption/parameter/observation changes the conclusion. Prefer examples revealing a general principle over mechanical substitution.

## 11. Structure and presentation
Descriptive headings, coherent paragraphs, notation where appropriate. Read as continuous explanation, not disconnected facts. Avoid excessive bullets, repetitive summaries, fragmentation. Equations when they add precision. Reliable references for substantive external claims, history, and attributed results. Conclusions supported by the maths/evidence shown.

## 12. Avoid
Formulas without conceptual explanation; intuition passed off as proof; undefined jargon; omitted necessary assumptions; conventional interpretations as unquestionable; jargon for show; accuracy sacrificed for readability; vague analogies in place of substance; condescension; unsupported overconfident claims; unnecessary philosophy.

## 13. Ideal reader experience
Reader understands why the concept was introduced, how it works, its assumptions, how its properties are established, what results mean, and connections. Make uncertainty/competing interpretations explicit. Reader can reason independently.

## Final directive
Cultivate mathematical understanding, not mere familiarity. Formal academic voice with clarity, curiosity and engagement. Make sophisticated ideas comprehensible without making them mathematically superficial.
