# Odds ratio — source notes

- Checked: Dodge (2008), *The Concise Encyclopedia of Statistics*, printed p. 390, “Odds and Odds Ratio”: odds are $p/(1-p)$; the worked treatment/reference example gives death probabilities 5% and 10%, odds about 0.053 and 0.111, and odds ratio about 0.47. Dodge contrasts odds ratio with relative risk and describes the rare-outcome approximation.
- Checked: Agresti, Tarantola and Varriale (2023), “Simple Ways to Interpret Effects in Modeling Binary Data”, author-hosted chapter at https://users.stat.ufl.edu/~aa/articles/Challenges.pdf, §5.1, pp. 155–156: logistic regression exponentiated coefficient is a conditional odds ratio, not a risk ratio; §5.2, pp. 157–158: marginal and conditional odds ratios need not coincide even when the additional predictor is uncorrelated with the exposure.
- Checked: Cox (1958), “The Regression Analysis of Binary Sequences”, original JRSS B publisher-hosted full paper via the Oxford Academic DOI page. Section 4.2, printed p. 221, identifies $e^\beta$ with the two-by-two-table odds ratio for a two-level predictor; the introduction, pp. 215–216, relates binary responses to explanatory variables. The History citation now points to §4.2 rather than the abstract.
- Checked: Agresti (2002), *Categorical Data Analysis*, ch. 3 publisher DOI page confirms its treatment of association in two-way tables. The full chapter and Penn State's online lesson were unavailable in this session, so new claims will be supported by the opened Agresti et al. (2023) author chapter and Dodge.
- Checked: For a 2-by-2 table with exposed outcomes 30 yes/70 no and unexposed 10 yes/90 no, the odds ratio is $(30\cdot90)/(70\cdot10)=27/7\approx3.8571$, whereas the risk ratio is $0.30/0.10=3$ and risk difference is $0.20$. Reversing the outcome or exposure takes the reciprocal; reversing both leaves the odds ratio unchanged.
- Assumed: Counts are from comparable groups; without assignment or adequate adjustment an observed odds ratio is an association rather than a causal effect.
- Disputed: An odds ratio of three does not generally mean three times the probability. Its interpretation changes with which outcome and reference group are chosen. A large odds ratio can coexist with a small absolute risk difference when events are rare.
