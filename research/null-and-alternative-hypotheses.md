# Null and alternative hypotheses — source notes

- Checked: Neyman and Pearson (1933), *On the Problem of the Most Efficient Tests of Statistical Hypotheses*, original Royal Society paper, pp. 293–298, especially the definition of sample space, simple versus composite hypotheses (p. 294), critical region and false-rejection constraint (pp. 293–295), and choice of critical region for a specified alternative (p. 298). A public scan was opened at https://stats.org.uk/statistical-inference/NeymanPearson1933.pdf; DOI metadata are in the existing bibliography entry `neyman_pearson1933`.
- Checked: Dodge (2008), *The Concise Encyclopedia of Statistics*, printed pp. 249–250, “Hypothesis” and “Hypothesis Testing”: a hypothesis is about a distribution or its parameters; Fisher's 1935 use of “null” and the development of Neyman–Pearson testing are described. The history paragraph will cite Dodge only for these historical statements.
- Checked: OpenStax, *Introductory Statistics 2e*, §§9.1–9.2, publisher-hosted pages: the opposing statements, one-sided versus two-sided alternatives, the reject/do-not-reject wording, and the four combinations of decision and truth. Its rule of thumb that a null “always” contains equality is an elementary convention, not a general theorem; the page will use parameter sets to handle composite hypotheses.
- Checked: `sources/stat2004/chapters/inf-04-hypothesis-tests.qmd`, “The ingredients” and “Errors and power”: pre-existing blood-pressure and tennis examples, images, and citations are part of the baseline and must remain.
- Checked: The new twelve-trial binomial worked example was computed directly with Python's `math.comb`; an exact arithmetic check will cover every printed value.
- Assumed: The twelve trials are independent Bernoulli trials with common success probability; this is a model assumption, not established by the count.
- Disputed: “Fail to reject” is not proof that the null is true; the outcome depends on power and the specified alternative. A null need not be a point equality in full generality, even though many introductory examples are.
