# Jacobian — source notes

- Checked: Cambridge Statistics Laboratory lecture notes (2016), author-hosted full lecture set at https://www.statslab.cam.ac.uk/Dept/People/djsteaching/S1B-16-all-lectures.pdf, lecture 1 §1.13, pp. 21–22: in a differentiable one-to-one two-dimensional change of variables, the transformed joint density is the original density evaluated at the inverse map times the absolute inverse Jacobian determinant. The domain must be transformed too.
- Checked: Existing `sources/stat2003/chapters/prob-05-transforms-and-limits.qmd`, around its sum/ratio of exponentials example, already shows the determinant $u$ and a Gamma–Uniform factorization. Preserve that example, so the new worked example will use a different linear map of two independent uniforms.
- Checked: MacTutor History of Mathematics, “Carl Jacobi (1804–1851)” at https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Jacobi/, biography paragraphs discussing functional determinants: Cauchy used a functional determinant earlier, while Jacobi's 1841 memoir developed the subject; this is the source for named history.
- Checked: Dodge (2008), *The Concise Encyclopedia of Statistics*, printed p. 540, “Transformation”, discusses why statisticians transform variables, but does not state the Jacobian density formula. Use only for context, not for the determinant theorem.
- Checked: If $X,Y$ are independent Uniform(0,1), $S=X+Y$ and $D=X-Y$ have inverse $(X,Y)=((S+D)/2,(S-D)/2)$ and absolute inverse determinant $1/2$. Their joint density is $1/2$ on the image diamond of area $2$; the marginal density of $S$ is $s$ for $0<s<1$ and $2-s$ for $1<s<2$, giving $\Pr(S<1)=1/2$. If $U=2X,V=3Y$, the inverse determinant is $1/6$ on a rectangle of area $6$.
- Checked: A many-to-one counterexample is $X\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(-1,1)$ with $Y=X^2$. Each positive $y<1$ has two inverse branches and density $f_Y(y)=1/(2\sqrt y)$; keeping only the positive branch gives half the correct density and integrates to $1/2$.
- Assumed: The worked map is one-to-one on the stated support and densities are with respect to two-dimensional Lebesgue measure; boundaries have probability zero.
- Disputed: A Jacobian matrix is not its determinant, and a density change uses the absolute inverse determinant, not the signed forward determinant. Omitting the transformed support or using a one-to-one formula for a many-to-one map can produce a density that does not integrate to one.
