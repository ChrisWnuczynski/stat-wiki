## Lead context
A probability space names the outcomes, the events that receive chances, and a rule that adds those chances over countable disjoint unions.

## Motivating example
A single coin toss needs only four events. An endless sequence of tosses is different: the event that a head appears eventually, and the event that the proportion of heads settles at one half, each depend on the whole sequence. Those events are countable combinations of the finite strings one can actually list. A probability space is the setting in which such combinations are events and have probabilities.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.1, pp. 1–2)

On a fair coin, the chance that the first head arrives on toss $k$ is $(1/2)^k$. The chance that it arrives by toss three is
$$
\frac12+\frac14+\frac18=\frac78.
$$
The complementary chance of three tails in a row is $1/8$. Adding the countable list of first-head chances gives $1$, so a head appears at some finite toss with probability $1$. Finite additivity of the first three terms would not have settled the infinite tail.{{cite:durrett2019}} (Thm. 1.1.1, continuity from below)

## Intuition
Think of the sample space as the set of complete stories the experiment can tell. An event is a question about the story that has a yes-or-no answer, and the probability is the weight of the stories for which the answer is yes. The weights of disjoint stories add. Countable additivity extends that bookkeeping from “two or three alternatives” to “one alternative for each positive integer”, which is what a limit along a sequence of events requires.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.1, pp. 1–2)

A sigma-algebra is the family of questions one has agreed to answer. On a finite set every subset can be a question. On the real line, or on the set of infinite coin sequences, some subsets are too wild to carry a probability that is countably additive and agrees with length or with fair-coin frequencies. Restricting attention to a sigma-algebra is the way the theory stays consistent.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.1, generated $\sigma$-fields, pp. 2–3)

## Definition
The opening definition already records the three axioms. A few consequences are used so often that they are worth seeing as part of the same definition. The empty set has probability $0$, because it is the disjoint union of itself with itself, and the only number equal to its double is zero. If $A\subset B$, then $B$ splits into $A$ and the part of $B$ outside $A$, so $\mathrm{P}(A)\le\mathrm{P}(B)$. Finite additivity is countable additivity with the unused sets empty. The same splitting argument gives the inclusion–exclusion identity
$$
\mathrm{P}(A\cup B)=\mathrm{P}(A)+\mathrm{P}(B)-\mathrm{P}(A\cap B).
$$
{{cite:durrett2019}} (Thm. 1.1.1, monotonicity)

On a countable sample space the most general probability is a list of nonnegative weights $p(\omega)$ that sum to $1$, with $\mathrm{P}(A)=\sum_{\omega\in A}p(\omega)$. When the space is finite and the weights are equal, this is the familiar count of favourable outcomes over the number of outcomes. Nothing in the definition forces the weights to be equal.{{cite:durrett2019}} (Ex. 1.1.2, discrete probability spaces)

## Interpretation — What is and is not an event
Calling a subset an event means it belongs to $\mathcal{F}$, so that $\mathrm{P}$ is allowed to see it. On $\mathbb{R}$ the Borel sigma-algebra is the smallest one containing the open intervals. It contains the closed intervals, the singletons, and every countable set. It does not contain every subset of the line. A random variable is a function that pulls Borel sets back into $\mathcal{F}$, so questions such as $\{X\le x\}$ are events even when $\Omega$ itself is complicated.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.2, distributions, p. 10)

Two sets $A$ and $B$ generate a sigma-algebra whose atoms are the four pieces $A\cap B$, $A\cap B^c$, $A^c\cap B$, and $A^c\cap B^c$. Every set built from $A$ and $B$ by complements and unions is a union of some of those atoms. If all four atoms are nonempty there are $2^4=16$ such unions, counting the empty union. If an atom is empty the count is smaller. The number $16$ is a ceiling, not a promise.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.1, $\sigma$-field generated by a collection, p. 3)

## Properties
Countable additivity is stronger than finite additivity, and the extra strength is exactly what passes a limit inside $\mathrm{P}$.

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Let $\mathrm{P}$ be countably additive on a sigma-algebra, with $\mathrm{P}(\Omega)=1$. The empty set equals the disjoint union of the sequence $\emptyset,\emptyset,\ldots$, so $\mathrm{P}(\emptyset)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{P}(\emptyset)$. The only solution in $[0,1]$ is $\mathrm{P}(\emptyset)=0$.

If $A\subset B$, write $B=A\cup(B\setminus A)$ as a disjoint union. Finite additivity, which is countable additivity with the later sets empty, gives $\mathrm{P}(B)=\mathrm{P}(A)+\mathrm{P}(B\setminus A)\ge\mathrm{P}(A)$.{{cite:durrett2019}} (Thm. 1.1.1(i))

For a countable cover, set $B_1=A_1$ and $B_n=A_n\setminus(A_1\cup\cdots\cup A_{n-1})$. The $B_n$ are disjoint, their union equals $\bigcup_n A_n$, and $B_n\subset A_n$. Countable additivity and monotonicity give
$$
\mathrm{P}\Bigl(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n\Bigr)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{P}(B_n)\le\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{P}(A_n).
$$
{{cite:durrett2019}} (Thm. 1.1.1(ii))

If $A_n\uparrow A$, the increments $C_1=A_1$ and $C_n=A_n\setminus A_{n-1}$ are disjoint and union to $A$, while the first $n$ of them union to $A_n$. Therefore $\mathrm{P}(A_n)\uparrow\mathrm{P}(A)$. If $A_n\downarrow A$, the complements increase to the complement, and subtracting from $1$ reverses the inequality. A probability automatically gives the first set finite measure, which is the extra hypothesis the same argument needs for a general measure.{{cite:durrett2019}} (Thm. 1.1.1(iii)–(iv))
:::
:::

## Statistical use — Building later results on one space
Almost every later limit is a statement about one probability space. The strong law says that the set of sequences whose sample averages converge has probability $1$. Markov's inequality and the central limit theorem are statements about the same space, read at a fixed time or in the limit. A model that only assigns probabilities to finitely many sets cannot even name the set of sequences on which a limit holds.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§2.4, Thm. 2.4.1)

Conditional probability and independence are defined inside the space, once the events have positive probability or the product formula is imposed. They are not extra axioms. Changing the sigma-algebra changes which conditional questions are legal, which is why a statistic, a stopping time, and a tail event are all described by the information they make measurable.

## Worked example
Two fair dice give $\Omega=\{1,\ldots,6\}^2$ with $36$ equally likely outcomes, so each outcome has probability $1/36$. Let $A$ be the event that the sum is at least $10$, and let $B$ be the event that the first die shows $6$.

The outcomes in $A$ are $(4,6)$, $(5,5)$, $(6,4)$, $(5,6)$, $(6,5)$, and $(6,6)$, so $|A|=6$ and $\mathrm{P}(A)=6/36=1/6$. The outcomes in $B$ are the six pairs with first coordinate $6$, so $\mathrm{P}(B)=1/6$. The intersection needs a first coordinate $6$ and a sum of at least $10$, hence a second coordinate of at least $4$: $(6,4)$, $(6,5)$, $(6,6)$. Thus $|A\cap B|=3$ and $\mathrm{P}(A\cap B)=3/36=1/12$. Inclusion–exclusion gives
$$
\mathrm{P}(A\cup B)=\frac{6}{36}+\frac{6}{36}-\frac{3}{36}=\frac{9}{36}=\frac14.
$$
Counting the union directly agrees: the six outcomes in $A$ together with $(6,1)$, $(6,2)$, and $(6,3)$, which lie in $B$ but not in $A$, make nine outcomes.{{cite:durrett2019}} (Ex. 1.1.2; Thm. 1.1.1)

**Your turn.** On the same two dice, let $C$ be the event that both faces are even. Find $\mathrm{P}(C)$, and find $\mathrm{P}(A\cap C)$.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
Each die has three even faces, so $|C|=3\times 3=9$ and $\mathrm{P}(C)=9/36=1/4$. The outcomes in $A\cap C$ are the even pairs whose sum is at least $10$: $(4,6)$, $(6,4)$, and $(6,6)$. So $|A\cap C|=3$ and $\mathrm{P}(A\cap C)=3/36=1/12$. The pair $(5,5)$ is in $A$ but not in $C$, which is why the intersection is smaller than $A$.
:::

## Common misconceptions
Equal likelihood is a special discrete model, not part of the definition. The ankle-bone die described by Durrett has four faces, scored $1$, $3$, $4$, and $6$, and there is no reason for those four faces to share the probability $1/4$. The space still consists of a finite set, the collection of all its subsets, and four nonnegative weights that sum to $1$.{{cite:durrett2019}} (Ex. 1.1.2, p. 3)

A second confusion is to treat every subset of an uncountable space as an event. Countable additivity plus agreement with ordinary length cannot be kept for every subset of the line. A third is to replace a countable union by a finite one and then pass to the limit without continuity from below. The partial sum $7/8$ is the probability that the first head arrives by toss three. The probability that a head arrives at some toss is the limit $1$, and that step uses the continuity theorem rather than a fresh axiom.

The union bound is an upper bound, not a probability. Three events of probability $0.4$ have union bound $1.2$, which says nothing once it exceeds $1$. If the three events coincide, the union has probability $0.4$.

## Assumptions and limits — Where the axioms stop
The axioms do not say which sigma-algebra to use. A coin-toss sequence can carry the sigma-algebra generated by the finite strings, which is large enough for the strong law and too small to decide arbitrary subsets of sequence space. They also do not force $\mathrm{P}$ to be defined on a power set. A finitely additive set function that fails countable additivity can still satisfy the finite axioms and still give the wrong probability to a limit event. Durrett's example of an algebra of subsets of the integers that is not a sigma-algebra makes the distinction concrete: the sets that are finite or have finite complement form an algebra, but the even integers are a countable union of singletons and neither the set nor its complement is finite.{{cite:durrett2019}} (Ex. 1.1.6, p. 4)

Conditional probability is undefined when the conditioning event has probability $0$. The product rule for a long intersection stops at the first factor whose conditioning event has probability $0$, even if a different description of the same experiment would have avoided that null set.

## Connections
An [event](event.qmd) is a member of $\mathcal{F}$. A [probability measure](probability-measure.qmd) is the set function $\mathrm{P}$, and a [sigma-algebra](sigma-algebra.qmd) is the family it is defined on. The [axioms of probability](axioms-of-probability.qmd) are this definition read on the events a first course names explicitly. The [strong law](strong-law.qmd) is a probability-$1$ statement about one sequence of sample averages, which is why it lives on a space closed under countable operations.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.1; Thm. 2.4.1)

## Check yourself
Three short calculations. Open an answer only after you have a number or a yes-or-no.

### A union from three pieces
Suppose $\mathrm{P}(A)=0.2$, $\mathrm{P}(B)=0.5$, and $\mathrm{P}(A\cap B)=0.1$. Find $\mathrm{P}(A\cup B)$ and $\mathrm{P}(A^c)$, and decide whether $A\subset B$ is possible.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
Inclusion–exclusion gives $\mathrm{P}(A\cup B)=0.2+0.5-0.1=0.6$. The complement has probability $1-0.2=0.8$. If $A$ were contained in $B$, the intersection would equal $A$ and would have probability $0.2$, not $0.1$. So $A$ is not a subset of $B$.
:::

### A countable sum
Independent trials succeed with probability $0.2$ and otherwise fail. The chance that the first success occurs on trial $k$ is $(0.8)^{k-1}(0.2)$. Find the chance that the first success occurs on trial $3$, and the chance that it occurs on trial $1$, $2$, or $3$.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
Trial $3$ has probability $(0.8)^2(0.2)=0.128$. The three-trial total is $0.2+0.16+0.128=0.488$. The remaining probability $0.512$ is the chance that the first three trials all fail, which is $(0.8)^3$.
:::

### Finite versus countable
On the positive integers, let $\mathcal{A}$ contain every set that is finite or has finite complement. Explain why $\mathcal{A}$ is an algebra and why the set of even integers shows that it is not a sigma-algebra.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The complement of a finite set has finite complement only in the trivial sense that the original set is finite, and the complement of a cofinite set is finite, so $\mathcal{A}$ is closed under complements. The union of two finite sets is finite, and a union that involves a cofinite set is cofinite. Each even singleton is finite, hence in $\mathcal{A}$. The even integers are a countable union of those singletons, and both that set and its complement are infinite, so the union is not in $\mathcal{A}$.
:::

## History
The triple $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathrm{P})$ is the starting point of the measure-theoretic development in Durrett: outcomes, a sigma-field of events, and a countably additive probability. The same chapter points out that equal weights are optional. The four faces of an ancient astragalus, scored $1$, $3$, $4$, and $6$, need four probabilities, not a single count of faces.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.1, pp. 1–3) Later limit theorems, including the strong law under a finite absolute mean, are proved inside this framework rather than by a separate set of axioms.{{cite:durrett2019}} (Thm. 2.4.1, p. 76)
