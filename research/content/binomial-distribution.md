## Motivating example
A laboratory screens a fixed batch of devices for a rare flaw. If each device independently has the same chance of failing, the number of failures has a binomial distribution: the identity of the devices is irrelevant once we ask only for the count. That reduction is useful because there are many possible sequences of pass and fail outcomes but only one count for each total. If devices share a faulty production run, however, their failures may cluster, and the same binomial calculation can understate the chance of a batch with many failures.{{cite:siegrist2022_binomial_distribution}} (§11.2, Basic Concepts and Examples)

## Intuition
For exactly $k$ successes in $n$ trials, any one specified sequence has probability $p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$. There are $\binom nk$ sequences with that count, producing the mass function in the definition. Its centre is $np$, the number expected from repeating the trial $n$ times; its variance $np(1-p)$ records both the number of opportunities and the uncertainty in each one. The distribution is discrete, so probabilities attach to integer counts, not areas under a density curve. The normal and Poisson curves discussed below are approximations for different regimes, not replacements for the exact finite-trial model.{{cite:siegrist2022_binomial_distribution}} (§11.2, The Probability Mass Function; Moments; Approximations)

## Definition
The trial count $n$ is fixed before observing the outcomes and the success probability $p$ is the same at each trial. Each Bernoulli indicator $B_i$ has $\Pr(B_i=1)=p$, and $X=\sum_{i=1}^nB_i\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)$ when the indicators are independent. The endpoints $p=0$ and $p=1$ give degenerate counts at $0$ and $n$; formulas involving division by $p(1-p)$ require the interior case. The mass $\binom nkp^k(1-p)^{n-k}$ sums to one by the binomial theorem.{{cite:siegrist2022_binomial_distribution}} (§11.2, Basic Concepts and the Probability Mass Function)

## Properties
The probability-generating function $G_X(t)=\mathbb E(t^X)=(1-p+pt)^n$ encodes both the mass function and its moments. If two independent binomial counts share $p$, their generating functions multiply, so their sum is binomial with the sum of their trial counts. If their success probabilities differ, that simplification fails: the result is a Poisson–binomial count. Likewise, drawing without replacement from a small finite population produces dependent indicators and a hypergeometric count.{{cite:siegrist2022_binomial_distribution}} (§11.2, Moments; Transformations; Sampling Without Replacement)

The two common approximations answer different questions. When $n$ is large and $p$ small while $np$ stays moderate, the count approaches a Poisson law with mean $np$. When both $np$ and $n(1-p)$ grow, the standardised count approaches a normal law. A continuity correction helps when using a smooth normal curve to approximate a staircase of discrete probabilities. At small $n$ or near the boundary, exact binomial tails remain available and avoid avoidable approximation error.{{cite:siegrist2022_binomial_distribution}} (§11.2, The Poisson Approximation; The Normal Approximation)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
For one Bernoulli trial, $\mathbb E(t^{B_i})=(1-p)+pt$. Independence gives $G_X(t)=\prod_i\mathbb E(t^{B_i})=(1-p+pt)^n$. Expanding by the binomial theorem yields $\Pr(X=k)=\binom nkp^k(1-p)^{n-k}$ and confirms that its masses sum to $G_X(1)=1$. Differentiating at $t=1$ gives $\mathbb E X=G_X'(1)=np$ and $\mathbb E\{X(X-1)\}=G_X''(1)=n(n-1)p^2$; subtracting $(np)^2$ from $\mathbb E X^2$ gives $\operatorname{Var}(X)=np(1-p)$. The product of two such generating functions with the same $p$ has exponent equal to the combined number of trials, proving the addition property.{{cite:siegrist2022_binomial_distribution}} (§11.2, Probability Mass Function; Moments; Transformations)
:::
:::

## Related distributions
A [Bernoulli distribution](bernoulli-distribution.qmd) describes one trial; the binomial describes its sum across independent repetitions. A [hypergeometric distribution](hypergeometric-distribution.qmd) counts successes when items are drawn without replacement from a finite population, so the trials are dependent. A [Poisson distribution](poisson-distribution.qmd) approximates a rare-success binomial count, whereas [beta–binomial prediction](beta-binomial-posterior-prediction.qmd) arises when the common $p$ itself varies between batches according to a beta law, creating dependence within each batch.{{cite:siegrist2022_binomial_distribution}} (§11.2, Sampling Without Replacement; Poisson Approximation){{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §2.1)

## Statistical inference
The likelihood of a fixed-$n$ count is proportional to $p^x(1-p)^{n-x}$, so the observed proportion $x/n$ is the maximum likelihood estimate for interior counts. Exact tests and confidence intervals use binomial tail sums rather than a normal approximation when the sample is small or the estimate is near an endpoint. Under a beta prior, the same likelihood updates the two beta parameters by successes and failures; the resulting posterior predictive count is beta–binomial rather than binomial at a plugged-in probability. These uses share a likelihood, but they answer different questions about $p$ and future counts.{{cite:siegrist2022_binomial_distribution}} (§11.2, Probability Mass Function){{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §2.1)

## Generating random samples
To simulate a binomial count, generate $n$ independent uniforms on $(0,1)$ and count how many are below $p$; equivalently, draw $n$ Bernoulli indicators and sum them. For a large number of repeated binomial counts, a dedicated binomial generator is faster, but it represents the same independent-trial model. Drawing a single uniform and comparing it with $p$ only produces one Bernoulli trial, not a general $\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)$ count.{{cite:siegrist2022_binomial_distribution}} (§11.2, Bernoulli trials representation)

## Worked example
Suppose $100$ independent units each have flaw probability $0.02$. For the flaw count $X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(100,0.02)$, the expected count is $100(0.02)=2$ and the variance is $100(0.02)(0.98)=1.96$. The chance of no flaws is $0.98^{100}\approx0.13262$; the chance of exactly one is $100(0.02)(0.98)^{99}\approx0.27065$. Thus $\Pr(X\leq1)\approx0.40327$. A Poisson approximation with mean $2$ gives $\Pr(X\leq1)\approx e^{-2}(1+2)=0.40601$, close but not identical. Exact binomial arithmetic is easy here, so the approximation is mainly a way to understand the rare-event shape.{{cite:siegrist2022_binomial_distribution}} (§11.2, The Poisson Approximation)

**Your turn.** For $200$ independent units with flaw probability $0.01$, calculate the exact chance of no flaws and its Poisson approximation. Give the exact mean and variance, and identify the condition that would be broken if flaws came in shared production clusters.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
For $X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(200,0.01)$, $\Pr(X=0)=0.99^{200}\approx0.13398$. The Poisson mean is $200(0.01)=2$, so the approximation is $e^{-2}\approx0.13534$. The exact mean is $2$ and variance is $200(0.01)(0.99)=1.98$. Shared production clusters would make unit outcomes dependent, violating the independent-trial assumption even if each unit's marginal flaw probability stayed $0.01$.{{cite:siegrist2022_binomial_distribution}} (§11.2, Bernoulli trials and the Poisson approximation)
:::

## Common misconceptions
An observed count of three out of ten does not mean that a future ten-trial count must equal three: the count itself is random even at a fixed $p$. The binomial model does not apply merely because each outcome is binary; trials must also be independent with one common success probability and fixed $n$. Variance is $np(1-p)$, not $np$, except as an approximation when $p$ is small. Finally, a beta–binomial predictive count is not binomial at the posterior mean of $p$: mixing over a shared uncertain $p$ makes future trials dependent.{{cite:siegrist2022_binomial_distribution}} (§11.2, Basic Concepts and Moments){{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §2.1)

## Assumptions and limits — When trials cease to match
If ten items are sampled without replacement from a box containing only ten red and ten blue items, the colour probabilities change after each draw; a hypergeometric model applies. If a software fault makes all units on one production line fail together, independence fails and extreme counts become more common than the binomial model predicts. If each trial has a different fixed success probability, the sum is Poisson–binomial rather than $\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)$. And if sampling stops at the first success, the number of trials is random and a geometric model describes the waiting time instead.{{cite:siegrist2022_binomial_distribution}} (§11.2, Bernoulli trials and Sampling Without Replacement)

## Connections
The sample proportion $X/n$ is a rescaled binomial count, so its variance is $p(1-p)/n$. A [binomial likelihood](maximum-likelihood-for-a-binomial-proportion.qmd) underlies inference about one probability, while [binary logistic regression](binary-logistic-regression.qmd) allows that probability to change with covariates. The [central limit theorem](central-limit-theorem.qmd) explains the large-sample normal approximation; the [Poisson limit](poisson-limit-of-the-binomial.qmd) explains the rare-event approximation. Each follows from a different way of changing $n$ and $p$.{{cite:siegrist2022_binomial_distribution}} (§11.2, Proportion of Successes; Approximations)

## History
Bernoulli's *Ars Conjectandi* appeared posthumously in 1713 and includes the repeated-trial calculation now associated with the binomial distribution. Its publication history is subtler than a single attribution: Edwards's historical study notes that De Moivre published the general-chances form in 1712, before Bernoulli's manuscript appeared, although Bernoulli may have derived it much earlier. Bernoulli's book also connected repeated binary trials with the law of large numbers, which made observed proportions central to inference.{{cite:edwards2013_ars}} (§§“Part I” and “Part IV”){{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 44–45, Binomial Distribution)
