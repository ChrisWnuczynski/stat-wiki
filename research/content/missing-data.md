## Lead context
Missing data are values the study meant to record and does not have.

## Motivating example
Four measurements were planned: $10$, a missing value, $14$ and $18$. The three observed numbers average $14$. Filling the hole with $14$ leaves that mean; filling it with $6$ moves the four-number mean to $12$.

## Intuition — An empty cell is not the same thing as a zero
A destroyed record, a plot that failed, or a coordinate that was never written down leaves a hole where a number was intended. Filling that hole by repeating the experiment is the ideal repair, and it is often impossible. A latent label in a mixture model is a different hole: the statistical story includes a component indicator that the sampler never kept. The arithmetic that replaces a dropped measurement is not the arithmetic that replaces that indicator.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 350, missing outcomes)

## Definition — Intended observations that are absent
Missing data occur when certain intended outcomes are not present, for example because a record was lost or a unit left the study. In a designed experiment the historical repairs estimate the missing yield rather than collect it again. Allan and Wishart treated a missing field plot in 1930, Yates in 1933, Bartlett by a covariance adjustment in 1937, and Rubin in 1972. Those dates locate early designed-experiment repairs; later missingness assumptions are a separate subject.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 350, definition and history)

## Worked example
Four measurements were planned: $10$, a missing value, $14$ and $18$. The mean of the three observed numbers is $(10+14+18)/3=14$. If the missing value had been $14$, the mean of all four would still be $14$. If it had been $6$, the complete mean would be $(10+6+14+18)/4=12$. The two fill-ins move the reported mean by $2$. The observed mean alone does not say which fill-in is right.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 350, absent outcomes)

**Your turn.** The same three numbers are observed, and the missing value is filled by $22$. What is the mean of the four numbers, and by how much does it exceed the observed mean $14$?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The four-number mean is $(10+22+14+18)/4=16$. It exceeds the observed mean by $2$. A high fill-in pulls the mean up by the same amount that the fill-in $6$ pulled it down.
:::

## Connections
A mixture's missing component label is handled by the [E step](e-step.qmd) of the [EM algorithm](em-algorithm.qmd), which replaces the label by its conditional probability under a model for that label. A dropped measurement is a different gap. Direct likelihood inference for the data parameter can ignore the process that caused the gap under two sufficient conditions. Write $\theta$ for the parameter of the intended measurements and $\phi$ for the parameter of the missingness process. Rubin's missing-at-random condition constrains only the pattern that was actually observed: for each $\phi$, the probability of that realised pattern does not depend on the missing values, and the condition says nothing about any other pattern. The parameters are distinct when their joint parameter space is a product of a $\theta$-space and a $\phi$-space. When both hold, the likelihood ratio for $\theta$ computed from the observed values alone equals the joint-model ratio at that same admissible $\phi$, provided the probability of the realised pattern is positive and the ratio is defined.{{cite:rubin1976_missing}} (§5, Def. 1, p. 584; §5, Def. 3, p. 585; §7, Thm. 7.1, p. 586) These conditions are sufficient for the cancellation. They are not necessary. If the mechanism depends on the unobserved values, the pointwise factorisation need not hold, and ignoring the mechanism can change likelihood ratios. The factor obtained by integrating the mechanism over the missing values can nevertheless be constant in $\theta$. Let an always-observed measurement $X$ carry $\theta$, and let an independent $Y$ be Bernoulli with probability $1/2$. Dropout probabilities $1/4$ at $Y=0$ and $3/4$ at $Y=1$ depend on the missing $Y$, yet the marginal probability of that pattern is $1/2$ and does not depend on $\theta$.{{cite:rubin1976_missing}} (§7, Thm. 7.2, pp. 586–587) A sampling distribution of a statistic can still depend on the mechanism, because the distribution of the observed values given the realised pattern uses the missingness model.{{cite:rubin1976_missing}} (§6, p. 585) Repeating the experiment, when it can be done, can supply a new measurement. It need not recover the original missing value.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 350, repeating the experiment)
