## Motivating example
A hospital compares two models for daily admissions. Adding predictors raises the best achievable training likelihood, even when the extra terms mostly track chance fluctuations. AIC subtracts the attraction of that in-sample gain by charging a penalty for fitted parameters. The result is a score for comparing predictive density models fitted to the same observations: the candidate with the smaller score has the better AIC ranking, subject to the model and likelihood assumptions.{{cite:akaike1974}} (§§IV–V, pp. 718–719)

## Intuition
The fitted log-likelihood rewards a model for explaining the observations it has already seen. The same observations also chose its parameters, so this score is optimistic for future data. AIC's $2d$ term estimates the cost of that reuse in regular likelihood models with $d$ fitted parameters. It does not require an actual test set, and it does not report an absolute goodness-of-fit probability. A lower AIC identifies the better score among the candidates presented, not a guarantee that any candidate represents the data well.{{cite:akaike1974}} (§§IV–V, pp. 718–719)

## Definition
For candidate model $M_j$, let $\hat\theta_j$ maximize the likelihood of the **same observed response data**, and let $d_j$ count its freely fitted parameters, including a variance parameter when estimated. Define $\mathrm{AIC}_j=-2\ell_j(\hat\theta_j)+2d_j$ and prefer the smallest value. The natural logarithm is used in $\ell_j$; AIC is dimensionless. Constants in a log-likelihood may be omitted only if the same constant is omitted for every candidate being compared. The absolute score then changes, while the differences do not.{{cite:akaike1974}} (§§IV–V, pp. 718–719)

The parameter count must match the fitted likelihood. In a Gaussian regression with an estimated residual variance, the residual variance counts as a parameter in addition to the coefficients. In an ARIMA model the estimated innovation variance likewise enters the count. A model fitted on fewer rows because of missing data or on a transformed response has a different likelihood target; comparing its raw AIC with one fitted on the original observations can be meaningless.{{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§§7.5, 9.6, parameter counts)

## Properties
For nested candidates with maximized log-likelihood improvement $\Delta\ell=\ell_{\rm larger}-\ell_{\rm smaller}$ and $r$ extra parameters, the AIC difference, larger minus smaller, is $-2\Delta\ell+2r$. Thus the larger model wins by AIC only when $\Delta\ell>r$. This is a comparison of fitted scores, not a hypothesis test with a fixed significance level. If the maximized log-likelihoods are equal, the simpler model wins by exactly two AIC units per extra parameter.{{cite:akaike1974}} (§§IV–V, pp. 718–719)

For $n>e^2\approx7.389$, BIC's $d\log n$ penalty exceeds AIC's $2d$ penalty for each added parameter. The two criteria need not choose different candidates: a sufficiently large likelihood improvement can pay both penalties. AICc adds a small-sample correction in common regular models; in the forecasting regression convention with $T$ observations and $k$ predictors, the extra term is $2(k+2)(k+3)/(T-k-3)$, where $k+2$ includes intercept and residual variance. This correction grows as the sample becomes small relative to the parameter count.{{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§7.5, AIC and corrected AIC)

The nested-model rule follows by subtraction: $\mathrm{AIC}_{\rm larger}-\mathrm{AIC}_{\rm smaller}=-2(\ell_{\rm larger}-\ell_{\rm smaller})+2(d_{\rm larger}-d_{\rm smaller})=-2\Delta\ell+2r$. Also, $\log n>2$ exactly when $n>e^2$, which proves the stated per-parameter penalty comparison.{{cite:akaike1974}} (§§IV–V, pp. 718–719){{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§7.5, BIC comparison)

The correction has an exact interpretation in a narrow Gaussian case. Let $Y=X\beta+\varepsilon$ with fixed full-rank design matrix $X$, $d$ regression coefficients, known variance $\sigma^2$, and independent $N(0,\sigma^2)$ errors. Put $H=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$, so $H^2=H$ and $\operatorname{tr}H=d$. The fitted mean is $HY$. Apart from the common $n\log(2\pi\sigma^2)$ term, in-sample negative twice log-likelihood is $\|Y-HY\|^2/\sigma^2$, with expectation $\operatorname{tr}(I-H)=n-d$. For an independent replicate $Y^*=X\beta+\varepsilon^*$ at the same design points, $Y^*-HY=\varepsilon^*-H\varepsilon$, whose expected squared norm divided by $\sigma^2$ is $n+\operatorname{tr}H=n+d$. The new-sample expected deviance therefore exceeds the training expected deviance by $2d$. Adding $2d$ to the observed training deviance is exactly unbiased for this new-sample expectation. This calculation proves the correction for this stated model; the general asymptotic Kullback–Leibler argument has additional regularity conditions.{{cite:akaike1974}} (§§IV–V, pp. 718–719)

## Statistical use — When AIC and BIC disagree
When AIC and BIC rank candidates differently, first list each model's fitted sample size, maximized log-likelihood, parameter count and scores on one sign convention. Compare AIC *differences*: subtract the minimum from each AIC score. A difference of zero marks the best-ranked candidate, while a positive difference says how much worse its penalized fit is by this criterion. Inspect residuals and predictive checks as well, because minimizing AIC cannot reveal a shared defect in every candidate. If prediction error itself is the target, a properly separated test set or cross-validation can assess it directly.{{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§7.5, AIC and cross-validation)

For the mixture table already shown, the best AIC is $2043.4$ at six components, while BIC and the listed cross-validation scores favor three. Finite mixtures can be singular and have unbounded unrestricted likelihoods when a covariance collapses onto one observation. The ordinary $2d$ bias argument does not automatically justify that component count; fitting constraints and the evaluation target matter. In time-series forecasting, the AICc is often preferred for smaller samples, and information criteria from ARIMA models with different differencing orders should not be compared casually because the likelihoods may be based on different transformed data.{{cite:fraley_raftery2002}} (§3, pp. 614–615, singular covariances){{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§9.6, information criteria and differencing)

## Worked example
Two admission-count models are fitted by maximum likelihood to the same 100 days. Model A has maximized log-likelihood $-140$ and two parameters, giving $\mathrm{AIC}_A=-2(-140)+2(2)=284$. Model B has log-likelihood $-136$ and five parameters, giving $\mathrm{AIC}_B=-2(-136)+2(5)=282$. Model B ranks better by two AIC units: its four-unit log-likelihood gain exceeds the three-unit gain needed to pay for three extra parameters. Its lower score does not establish that its forecasts are calibrated or that the five-parameter model is true.{{cite:akaike1974}} (§§IV–V, pp. 718–719)

**Your turn.** Model C and Model D fit the same observations. C has maximized log-likelihood $-50$ and three parameters; D has log-likelihood $-48$ and six parameters. Calculate both AIC values and name the preferred model.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
$\mathrm{AIC}_C=100+6=106$ and $\mathrm{AIC}_D=96+12=108$, so C has the smaller AIC. D gains two log-likelihood units but adds three parameters; it would need a gain greater than three for its AIC to be lower.{{cite:akaike1974}} (§§IV–V, pp. 718–719)
:::

## Common misconceptions
AIC is not a probability that a model is true, nor a conventional goodness-of-fit test. A lower score among two candidates does not say either model fits adequately. AIC scores from models fitted to different responses, transformed scales or different subsets of rows need not share a common likelihood basis. The penalty counts estimated parameters, including nuisance scales when estimated, rather than only the predictors an analyst cares about. Finally, adding a predictor always improves or preserves the maximum training likelihood for genuinely nested fits, but it can worsen AIC when its gain is too small.{{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§7.5, predictor and variance counts)

## Assumptions and limits — When the ordinary penalty fails
The predictive interpretation of the ordinary penalty uses regular, identified, interior likelihood fits and an appropriate comparison target. A two-component Gaussian mixture with one covariance shrinking around a single data point provides a concrete failure: its unrestricted maximized likelihood can diverge, so inserting a nominal parameter count into ordinary AIC does not repair the fit. For a quasi-Poisson model there is no full ordinary likelihood and hence no usual likelihood AIC. In a small Gaussian regression with nearly as many fitted terms as observations, the asymptotic $2d$ correction may be too small; AICc expresses a larger finite-sample adjustment where its derivation applies. For an ARIMA model, changing the differencing order can change which transformed observations enter the likelihood.{{cite:fraley_raftery2002}} (§3, pp. 614–615, singular covariances){{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§§7.5, 9.6, small sample and differencing)

## Connections
The [likelihood function](likelihood-function.qmd) supplies the fit term, and [Kullback–Leibler divergence](kullback-leibler-divergence.qmd) explains its predictive target up to a model-independent constant. [BIC](bayesian-information-criterion.qmd) uses a $\log n$ parameter penalty and pursues a different large-sample approximation. A [held-out test set](a-held-out-test-set.qmd) measures predictive performance on untouched cases; [cross-validation](cross-validation.qmd) repeatedly does this inside development data. [Deviance](deviance.qmd) also comes from likelihood comparisons, but one should check its reference model and constants before treating it as an AIC term.{{cite:akaike1974}} (§§IV–V, pp. 718–719)

## History
Akaike introduced the information criterion in his 1974 paper on statistical model identification. He contrasted a minimum information-theoretic criterion based on maximum likelihood with routine hypothesis-testing choices, especially for time-series models. The formula in §V is negative twice the maximized log-likelihood plus twice the number of independently adjusted parameters.{{cite:akaike1974}} (§§IV–V, pp. 718–719)

Later use extended the comparison to regression and forecasting. Hyndman and Athanasopoulos present AIC beside cross-validation and a finite-sample correction for selecting predictors and ARIMA orders. These applications keep the same central idea while making the likelihood basis, parameter count and sample size explicit.{{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§§7.5, 9.6, model selection)
