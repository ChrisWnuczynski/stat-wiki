## Motivating example
A larger statistical model can fit a sample better simply because it has more parameters. Analysis of deviance compares a sequence of fitted models by measuring how much likelihood improves when terms are added. A useful table states the models being compared, the decrease in deviance, the change in design rank and the reference distribution used for calibration. Sequential rows answer questions about a specified order of model enlargement.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§5.3.3, model selection and sequential tables)

## Definition — The saturated likelihood cancels in a difference
Let $\ell_0$ and $\ell_1$ be maximised log likelihoods in nested models, with model one containing model zero. For a common known dispersion $\phi$, write unscaled deviance as $D_j=2\phi(\ell_{\mathrm{sat}}-\ell_j)$. Then
$$\frac{D_0-D_1}{\phi}=2(\ell_1-\ell_0).$$
Thus the scaled deviance decrease is the likelihood-ratio statistic. It is nonnegative because the larger parameter space includes every fit allowed by the smaller model. This comparison requires the same response data and compatible likelihood conventions. Deviances from unrelated likelihoods are not automatically comparable by subtraction.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§5.3.2, nested-model likelihood-ratio test)

## Properties — Dispersion changes the calibration
For regular identifiable models with an interior null and enough information, the likelihood-ratio statistic has an asymptotic chi-squared reference law with degrees of freedom equal to the parameter-rank difference. Boundaries, singular designs or incidental parameters can invalidate that reference. Small individual Poisson means also make residual-deviance chi-squared approximations unreliable in settings where increasing sample size does not cure the approximation. A nested-model difference and the deviance against a growing saturated model are different asymptotic comparisons.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§5.3.1, residual deviance and dispersion; §5.3.2)

For Gaussian linear models with common unknown variance, unscaled deviance is residual sum of squares. With reduced and full design ranks $r_0<r_1$, the statistic
$$F=\frac{(D_0-D_1)/(r_1-r_0)}{D_1/(n-r_1)}$$
has the exact $F_{r_1-r_0,n-r_1}$ null law under independent normal equal-variance errors. The denominator estimates dispersion from the full model. Using an estimated dispersion to form an F-type comparison in another GLM requires a separate approximation argument.{{cite:nist_oneway_anova}} (§3.2.3.1, mean-square ratio and assumptions)

## Worked example — The same decrease on two scales
Suppose two nested Gaussian fits use $n=20$ observations, with design ranks two and four, and residual sums of squares $50$ and $32$. The deviance decreases by $18$ for two added coefficients. If the error variance is known to be $\phi=2$, the scaled likelihood-ratio statistic is $9$. If the variance is instead estimated from the full fit, the mean square error is $32/16=2$ and the exact F statistic is $(18/2)/2=4.5$. These statistics use different reference distributions despite being based on the same fitted sums of squares.{{cite:nist_oneway_anova}} (§3.2.3.1)

**Your turn.** If a fifth independent design column lowers the full-model SSE from $32$ to $30$, what is the F statistic for adding that one column?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The new residual degrees of freedom are $20-5=15$. The statistic is $(32-30)/(30/15)=1$.
:::

## Assumptions and limits — A selection path changes the question
When design columns are not orthogonal, the improvement from adding a term depends on the model already fitted. Tests of a block of related interactions differ from testing only the final interaction in a sequential table. Dummy columns for one categorical variable should be considered as a group when the question concerns that whole variable. Repeated stepwise choices also require caution in interpreting the nominal p-values as if the final model had been specified in advance.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§5.3.3, order-dependent selection)

## Intuition
Deviance measures how far a fitted model is from a saturated fit that gives every observation its own mean. A larger nested model is closer to that ceiling, so its deviance is smaller. The drop is the part of the likelihood that the added parameters bought. For a known dispersion $\phi$, dividing the drop by $\phi$ recovers the likelihood-ratio statistic $2(\ell_1-\ell_0)$. A drop of $18$ with $\phi=2$ is a likelihood-ratio statistic of $9$. A drop of $15$ with $\phi=5$ is a likelihood-ratio statistic of $3$. The two calculations use the same definition and different numbers. The saturated log likelihood cancels, which is why the comparison can be computed from the two model deviances alone, provided both deviances used the same saturated target and the same dispersion convention.

In a Gaussian linear model the unscaled deviance is the residual sum of squares. The worked fits on $n=20$ observations, with ranks $2$ and $4$ and residual sums $50$ and $32$, have a drop of $18$ on $4-2=2$ degrees of freedom. Known variance $\phi=2$ scales that drop to $9$. Estimating the variance from the larger model uses mean square $32/16=2$ and produces the $F$ statistic $(18/2)/2=4.5$. The residual sums are the same in both calculations. The reference distributions are not. One is chi-squared on $2$ degrees of freedom, after scaling by the known variance. The other is $F_{2,16}$.

### Properties — Rank difference is the degrees-of-freedom count
For regular models the asymptotic null law of the likelihood-ratio statistic uses the difference in parameter rank, not the difference in the number of column names. Two nested designs of ranks $5$ and $8$ have rank difference $3$ when the smaller design's column space sits inside the larger one and the usual smoothness conditions hold. A duplicated column does not increase the rank and should not increase the degrees of freedom. Boundary constraints, such as a variance component tested at zero, change the reference law even when a formal rank difference can still be computed. The chi-squared approximation is a statement about an interior null and a well-behaved information matrix.

The residual deviance against a saturated model is a different comparison from a nested drop. As the sample grows, a saturated multinomial or Poisson model acquires more cells or more means. The asymptotic behaviour of that residual deviance is not the nested-model theorem with a fixed rank difference. Small Poisson means make the residual-deviance approximation poor even in large samples of the same kind of mean. A nested comparison of two smooth parametric models can still be well calibrated in that setting. The table should say which of the two comparisons a row reports.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§5.3.1, residual deviance and dispersion; §5.3.2)

## Common misconceptions
A sequential analysis-of-deviance table depends on the order of entry when the columns are not orthogonal. The drop attributed to a factor is the improvement over the model already fitted, not the improvement over the intercept alone. Reading every row as if it were a test against the same reduced model double-counts or mis-assigns the shared explanatory power. Interactions are a special case: a block of interaction columns answers a question about that block. The last single interaction column, entered after the others, answers a narrower question.

Stepwise selection repeats these tests on models chosen because earlier tests were large. The nominal chi-squared or $F$ tail probability for the final comparison is the probability that would apply if that comparison had been specified in advance. Selection makes the statistic's null distribution stochastically larger than the reference distribution of a predeclared comparison. A held-out likelihood, or a penalty such as AIC computed on a predeclared list, is a different procedure and should be labelled as one.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§5.3.3, model selection and sequential tables)

## Connections
Adjusted $R^2$ ranks nested Gaussian fits by a degrees-of-freedom rescaling of residual sums of squares. The $F$ statistic from an analysis of deviance uses those same sums and asks whether the average drop per added rank exceeds the full-model mean square. The cutoff for an increase in adjusted $R^2$ is a partial $F$ of one. A level-$5\%$ test uses a larger cutoff. Information criteria compare maximised log likelihoods plus a penalty proportional to the number of parameters, which for a known-variance Gaussian model is a monotone function of the residual sum of squares plus a different penalty. The same pair of fits can be ordered differently by the three rules. Reporting the residual sums, the ranks, and the dispersion estimate makes the ordering recomputable under each rule.
