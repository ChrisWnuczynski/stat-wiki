## Lead context
For a covariance matrix, eigenvectors reveal directions in which a collection of measurements varies without cross-covariance, and eigenvalues give the variance along each direction. The same calculation controls the axes of a normal density and the variance retained by principal component analysis.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §1.1, “Eigenvalue decomposition of the covariance matrix”; Ch. 3, §3.5)

## Motivating example
Suppose a two-coordinate cloud is elongated along the line where both measurements rise together. The variances of the two original coordinates do not identify the direction of that elongation. Diagonalising their covariance matrix finds a new pair of perpendicular axes: one along the cloud's long direction and one across its short direction. The corresponding eigenvalues quantify how much the cloud varies along each axis. If a one-dimensional display is needed, the long direction is the principal component that retains more variance.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §1.3, covariance ellipsoids; Ch. 3, §3.5, PCA transformation)

## Intuition
An eigenvector is a direction that a matrix does not turn away from itself. Multiplication by the matrix only scales it by the eigenvalue; a negative eigenvalue reverses its direction as well as scaling it. For a symmetric matrix, perpendicular eigenvectors can be chosen to span the space. Covariance matrices are a special case: their eigenvalues cannot be negative, because variance along any direction is nonnegative. Zero eigenvalues mark directions with no random variation. This picture makes the matrix equation $S\boldsymbol a=\lambda\boldsymbol a$ a statement about directional variance, not just a computational trick.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §1.1, “Eigenvalue decomposition of the covariance matrix”)

## Definition
For a real square matrix $A$, a nonzero vector $\boldsymbol v$ is an eigenvector if $A\boldsymbol v=\lambda\boldsymbol v$ for some eigenvalue $\lambda$. Equivalently, $(A-\lambda I)\boldsymbol v=0$, so an eigenvalue solves $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$. Multiplying an eigenvector by any nonzero scalar gives another eigenvector for the same eigenvalue. Thus the vector's length and sign are not determined by the eigenvalue equation alone. We normally use unit vectors when comparing directions. The definition applies to general square matrices, but the real orthogonal decomposition used on this page requires a *real symmetric* matrix.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §1.1, “Eigenvalue decomposition of the covariance matrix”)

For a symmetric $S$, collect orthonormal eigenvectors as columns of $Q$ and their eigenvalues in $\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_p)$. Then $S=Q\Lambda Q^\top$. A covariance matrix is symmetric and positive semi-definite, so each $\lambda_j\geq0$. Positive definiteness means every eigenvalue is strictly positive. This decomposition does not assert that arbitrary observations are independent after rotation; it guarantees zero covariance of their rotated coordinates. For a jointly normal population, those zero covariances additionally imply independence.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §§1.1, 1.3; Ch. 3, §3.5)

## Properties
The trace and determinant are quick summaries of the spectrum: $\operatorname{tr}(S)=\sum_j\lambda_j$ and $\det(S)=\prod_j\lambda_j$. A zero eigenvalue makes $S$ singular, so a formula using $S^{-1}$ needs strictly positive eigenvalues. For positive-definite $S$, its inverse has the same eigenvectors and reciprocal eigenvalues, while $S^{1/2}=Q\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_j})Q^\top$ has the same directions. These facts explain the inverse covariance and square-root transformations in multivariate normal calculations.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §1.1, eigenvalue decomposition and inverse; Ch. 3, §3.1, Exs. 3.5–3.6)

For a unit vector $\boldsymbol a$, the variance of its projected data is $\boldsymbol a^\top S\boldsymbol a$. Writing $\boldsymbol a=Q\boldsymbol b$ gives $\sum_j\lambda_jb_j^2$, a weighted average of the eigenvalues because $\sum_jb_j^2=1$. Therefore the largest eigenvalue is the maximum variance obtainable by a unit direction, and its eigenvector attains it. Requiring later directions to be orthogonal yields the remaining principal components in decreasing eigenvalue order. The fraction of total variance in the first $k$ directions is $\sum_{j=1}^k\lambda_j/\sum_{j=1}^p\lambda_j$ when the denominator is positive.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 3, §3.5, PCA transformation and compression properties)

For a positive-definite covariance $\Sigma$, the same eigenvectors orient a multivariate normal density's ellipsoidal contours. At squared Mahalanobis radius $C$, the semi-axis along eigenvector $j$ has length $\sqrt{C\lambda_j}$. The density contour and a confidence region for an estimated mean answer different questions; the latter contracts as independent data accumulate, whereas the population density shape does not. An eigenvalue's unit is the square of the measurement unit of its associated projected score, so changing the scale of an original coordinate can alter the eigenvectors and eigenvalues.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §1.3, covariance ellipsoids; Ch. 3, §3.5, application to data)

**Proof idea.** Orthogonal diagonalisation reduces quadratic forms to sums of eigenvalues times squared coordinates. From that one calculation follow positivity, maximal projected variance, trace, determinant, inverse and contour axes.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §§1.1, 1.3; Ch. 3, §3.5)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
For real symmetric $S$, the spectral theorem supplies orthogonal $Q$ and diagonal $\Lambda$ with $S=Q\Lambda Q^\top$ and $Q^\top Q=I$. If $\boldsymbol a=Q\boldsymbol b$, then $\boldsymbol a^\top S\boldsymbol a=\boldsymbol b^\top\Lambda\boldsymbol b=\sum_j\lambda_j b_j^2$. For a covariance $S$, the left side is $\operatorname{Var}(\boldsymbol a^\top\boldsymbol X)\geq0$ for all $\boldsymbol a$. Taking $\boldsymbol b$ to be each coordinate basis vector proves every $\lambda_j\geq0$. It also proves that a positive-definite covariance has strictly positive eigenvalues, and conversely.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §1.1, “Eigenvalue decomposition of the covariance matrix”)

Because trace is unchanged by orthogonal similarity, $\operatorname{tr}(S)=\operatorname{tr}(Q^\top SQ)=\operatorname{tr}(\Lambda)=\sum_j\lambda_j$. Taking determinants gives $\det(S)=\det(Q)^2\det(\Lambda)=\prod_j\lambda_j$. If no eigenvalue is zero, multiplying $Q\Lambda^{-1}Q^\top$ by $S$ gives $I$, hence $S^{-1}=Q\operatorname{diag}(1/\lambda_j)Q^\top$. For nonnegative eigenvalues, multiplying $Q\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_j})Q^\top$ by itself gives $S$. A zero eigenvalue makes the determinant zero and prevents an ordinary inverse.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §1.1, eigenvalue decomposition and inverse; Ch. 3, §3.1, Ex. 3.6)

For $\|\boldsymbol a\|=1$, orthogonality gives $\sum_j b_j^2=1$, so $\boldsymbol a^\top S\boldsymbol a=\sum_j\lambda_jb_j^2\leq\lambda_1$ when $\lambda_1$ is the largest eigenvalue. Equality holds at a first eigenvector. Requiring $\boldsymbol a$ to be orthogonal to the first $k-1$ chosen eigenvectors sets the first $k-1$ coordinates of $\boldsymbol b$ to zero and makes the next maximum $\lambda_k$. The transformed scores $Q^\top\boldsymbol X$ have covariance $Q^\top SQ=\Lambda$, so their variances are the eigenvalues and their cross-covariances vanish. Summing their variances gives the stated total and explained-variance fraction.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 3, §3.5, PCA transformation and compression properties)

Finally put $\boldsymbol u=Q^\top(\boldsymbol x-\boldsymbol\mu)$. For positive-definite $\Sigma$, a normal density's equal-height condition is $(\boldsymbol x-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol x-\boldsymbol\mu)=C$. Substitution gives $\sum_j u_j^2/\lambda_j=C$. Setting all but one $u_j$ to zero shows the intercepts $\pm\sqrt{C\lambda_j}$ along the eigenvectors. The density contour is an ellipsoid whose long axes correspond to larger eigenvalues.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §1.3, covariance ellipsoids)

## Statistical use
In principal component analysis, diagonalising the sample covariance identifies uncorrelated score columns. An analyst can retain a few high-variance directions to display or compress the data, but retaining variance is a reconstruction goal, not a guarantee of predictive value for a separate response. Standardising coordinates before diagonalising a correlation matrix gives a different question: it gives each original coordinate unit variance first. Record which matrix was analysed and apply the same centring and scaling to future observations.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 3, §3.5, PCA transformation, application to data and iris example)

The eigenvalues also diagnose whether a proposed covariance is valid. Negative eigenvalues rule out a covariance matrix; zero eigenvalues reveal exact linear redundancy, and very small ones make inversion sensitive to perturbations. For a positive-definite covariance, the inverse weights deviations along a low-variance direction more heavily in a Mahalanobis distance. This is why a visually small displacement across a narrow data cloud may be less typical than a larger displacement along its long axis.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §§1.1, 1.3; Ch. 3, §3.1, Ex. 3.7)

## Worked example
Take $S=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$. Its characteristic equation is $\det(S-\lambda I)=(2-\lambda)^2-1=(\lambda-3)(\lambda-1)=0$, so the eigenvalues are $3$ and $1$. Unit eigenvectors are $\boldsymbol q_1=(1,1)^\top/\sqrt2$ and $\boldsymbol q_2=(1,-1)^\top/\sqrt2$. Projected scores along these directions have variances $3$ and $1$ and zero covariance. The first direction retains $3/(3+1)=3/4=75\%$ of the total variance. A normal density with covariance $S$ has, at squared radius $C=4$, semi-axis lengths $\sqrt{4(3)}=2\sqrt3$ and $\sqrt{4(1)}=2$ along those directions. The long axis follows the positive association between coordinates.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §1.3, covariance ellipsoids; Ch. 3, §3.5, PCA transformation)

**Your turn.** For $B=\begin{pmatrix}5&2\\2&5\end{pmatrix}$, find both eigenvalues and unit eigenvectors. What fraction of total variance does the leading direction retain if $B$ is a covariance matrix?{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §1.1; Ch. 3, §3.5)

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The characteristic polynomial is $(5-\lambda)^2-4=(\lambda-7)(\lambda-3)$, so the eigenvalues are $7$ and $3$. The unit eigenvectors are $(1,1)^\top/\sqrt2$ and $(1,-1)^\top/\sqrt2$, respectively. The first direction retains $7/(7+3)=7/10=70\%$ of total variance. Both eigenvalues are positive, so $B$ is a possible positive-definite covariance matrix.
:::

## Common misconceptions
An eigenvector's sign is arbitrary: $\boldsymbol q$ and $-\boldsymbol q$ name the same axis, so software can return either without changing a PCA fit. A repeated eigenvalue permits rotations within its eigenspace, so individual eigenvectors need not be unique even after their lengths are fixed. Eigenvalues of a *general* real matrix need not be real or nonnegative; those conclusions here use symmetry and, for nonnegativity, positive semi-definiteness. Finally, uncorrelated PCA scores are not necessarily independent unless an additional joint distribution such as a multivariate normal supplies that implication.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §§1.1, 1.3; Ch. 3, §§3.4–3.5)

## Assumptions and limits
The clean orthogonal axes, real eigenvalues and Rayleigh-quotient argument require a real symmetric matrix. A sample covariance has that structure, but finite samples can yield unstable eigenvectors when nearby eigenvalues are close, and a repeated eigenvalue identifies a subspace rather than a preferred basis within it. A covariance eigenvalue is nonnegative; if a numerical calculation reports a small negative value, check whether it reflects rounding or a genuinely invalid matrix. PCA based on covariance is sensitive to units, while PCA based on correlation intentionally changes the scale convention. Neither choice establishes that high-variance directions are the ones relevant to a prediction task.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §1.1; Ch. 3, §§3.4–3.5)

## Connections
The [positive definite matrix](positive-definite-matrix.qmd) criterion is an eigenvalue test. [Principal component directions](principal-component-directions.qmd) select eigenvectors of a covariance matrix, and their eigenvalues give component variances. The [singular value decomposition](singular-value-decomposition.qmd) of a centred data matrix supplies the same directions through its right singular vectors; squared singular values, divided by the covariance divisor, are the covariance eigenvalues. For a [multivariate normal distribution](multivariate-normal-distribution.qmd), those directions orient density ellipsoids. Whitening additionally divides transformed coordinates by the square roots of positive eigenvalues, producing identity covariance.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §§1.1, 1.3; Ch. 3, §§3.1, 3.5)

## History
In statistics, the eigenvalue problem became central to principal component analysis. Pearson's 1901 geometric work on orthogonal regression supplied earlier mathematics, and Hotelling's 1933 formulation treated principal components through covariance eigenvectors. Strimmer's modern account explicitly distinguishes the two contributions and derives the PCA rotation from the covariance decomposition.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 3, §3.5, “PCA transformation”)

The present use of eigenvalues extends beyond PCA: covariance positivity and normal-density contours are both described by the same orthogonal decomposition. The statistical meaning of an eigenvalue is consequently tied to the matrix being decomposed—variance for a covariance matrix, rather than a universal measure of importance.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §§1.1, 1.3; Ch. 3, §3.5)
