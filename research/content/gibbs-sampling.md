## Lead context
Gibbs sampling builds a dependent sequence of draws from a difficult joint distribution by repeatedly drawing from easier conditional distributions. Each update changes only one coordinate or block, conditional on the current values of everything else. This is useful in Bayesian models whose posterior normalising constant is awkward but whose full conditionals are recognisable sampling distributions. The method needs a proper joint target and valid conditional samplers; convenient algebra alone does not establish convergence or good exploration.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§10.1–10.6, pp. 27–30){{cite:gelfand_smith1990}} (§§1–2.3, pp. 398–401)

## Motivating example
Imagine a posterior for a mean $\mu$ and a precision $\tau$. Holding $\tau$ fixed may make the conditional law of $\mu$ normal; holding $\mu$ fixed may make the law of $\tau$ gamma. Alternating those draws avoids sampling directly from the coupled two-dimensional posterior. A pair recorded after the updates is a joint draw from a Markov chain, not two independent marginal draws. When the posterior has a narrow diagonal ridge, the chain can move in short horizontal and vertical steps even though every conditional draw is exact; the staircase figure shows this limitation.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§10.6, pp. 29–30){{cite:gelfand_smith1990}} (§2.3, pp. 400–401)

## Intuition
The joint target describes which combinations are plausible. A conditional update draws one coordinate among combinations compatible with the *current* other coordinates. After updating $X$, an update of $Y$ must condition on the new $X$; otherwise a different transition rule is being run. The sampler may stay at the same value after a draw, especially for a discrete coordinate. “Every proposal is accepted” describes the lack of a Metropolis rejection step, not a promise that the state changes at every update.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§10.1, 10.4–10.5, pp. 27–29)

The full conditional $\pi(x_i\mid x_{-i})$ is proportional to the joint density or mass as a function of $x_i$. Factors independent of $x_i$ cancel in its normalisation; factors containing $x_i$ must stay. This makes a posterior known only up to a global constant usable for Gibbs updates. The proportionality still has to be normalised for the coordinate draw, by a sum, integral or recognised distribution. A wrong support restriction or rate/scale convention produces a different chain even if the algebraic kernel resembles the desired family.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§6, 10.1, 10.6, pp. 11, 27–30)

## Definition
Let $\pi(x_1,\ldots,x_p)$ be a proper joint target. A Gibbs update of coordinate $i$ keeps $x_{-i}$ fixed and replaces $x_i$ with a draw from $\pi(\cdot\mid x_{-i})$. A *systematic scan* composes the coordinate updates in a fixed order, recording a completed state after each sweep. A *random scan* chooses an update index at random at each step; a *block Gibbs* update replaces several coordinates together from their joint conditional law given the remaining coordinates. These are different transition kernels that can share the same invariant target when built from its exact conditionals.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§8.1, 10.1–10.5, pp. 20–21, 27–29)

For two coordinates and an $X$-then-$Y$ scan, the transition from $(x,y)$ to $(x',y')$ is $K((x,y),(x',y'))=\pi(x'\mid y)\pi(y'\mid x')$. The second factor uses $x'$, not $x$. The reverse scan has $\pi(y'\mid x)\pi(x'\mid y')$ and need not give the same transition probability. These formulas describe one sweep, not the target mass $\pi(x',y')$ itself.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§8.1–8.2, 10.4–10.5, pp. 20–21, 28–29)

## Properties
One exact coordinate update leaves $\pi$ invariant, and any composition of such updates leaves it invariant. A mixture of coordinate kernels with fixed positive selection probabilities is invariant too. Invariance means a chain *started* at $\pi$ remains at $\pi$; it does not mean an arbitrary starting state has converged after one sweep. For a finite product state space with strictly positive mass at every state, a complete systematic sweep has a positive transition probability between every pair of states, so repeated sweeps converge to $\pi$. More general state spaces need their own irreducibility and convergence conditions.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§8.1, 10.4–10.5, pp. 20–21, 28–29){{cite:geman_geman1984}} (§XII, Theorem A, p. 733)

The conditional draws are usually serially dependent. If the target coordinates are highly correlated, updating one at a time can mix slowly because a coordinate is constrained by the other current values. Blocking related coordinates may help if the block conditional can be sampled. No universal number of warm-up or retained sweeps establishes convergence, and thinning usually reduces the amount of usable information when storage is not limiting.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§10.6, pp. 29–30){{cite:geyer1992}} (§§3.3–3.7, pp. 477–480)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
For a two-coordinate target, suppose the current pair has law $\pi$. Update $X$ conditional on the unchanged $Y$. The resulting joint law is $\pi_Y(y)\pi(x'\mid y)=\pi(x',y)$. Updating $Y$ conditional on the new $X$ similarly gives $\pi_X(x')\pi(y'\mid x')=\pi(x',y')$. Thus each kernel and their composition preserve $\pi$. The same argument works for any coordinate or block by replacing the first marginal with $\pi_{-i}$. A fixed mixture of invariant kernels is invariant by linearity.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§8.1, 10.4, pp. 20–21, 28–29)

In the finite strictly positive case, every full conditional assigns positive probability to every coordinate value. A complete sweep can therefore set the coordinates to any chosen target vector with positive probability, regardless of its starting vector. The sweep kernel has positive entries, including self-transitions, and is irreducible and aperiodic. The finite-state Markov-chain convergence theorem then yields convergence to its invariant law. Positivity is a sufficient condition here; without it, support and reachability require separate analysis.{{cite:geman_geman1984}} (§XII, Theorem A, p. 733){{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§5.1, 10.5, pp. 9–10, 29)
:::
:::

## Statistical use
Derive each full conditional from the *same* posterior and check its support and normalisation. Choose an update schedule, an initial state and a recording unit; one stored row after a systematic sweep contains one value of every parameter. Run enough sweeps to examine trace plots, autocorrelation and effective sample size, preferably from dispersed starts when separated regions are plausible. Posterior means and probabilities can be estimated from retained states, but their Monte Carlo uncertainty must account for dependence. The linked normal-mean-and-variance example shows why an inverse-gamma marginal update and an inverse-gamma *full conditional* can have different shapes: they condition on different information.{{cite:gelfand_smith1990}} (§§2–3, pp. 399–404){{cite:geyer1992}} (§§3.3–3.7, pp. 477–480){{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§10.6, pp. 29–30)

## Worked example
Suppose a binary target assigns $\pi(0,0)=3/10$, $\pi(0,1)=1/10$, $\pi(1,0)=1/5$ and $\pi(1,1)=2/5$. The four masses sum to one. Begin at $(X,Y)=(0,0)$ and update $X$ then $Y$. Holding $Y=0$, the chance of drawing $X'=1$ is $(1/5)/(3/10+1/5)=2/5$. If that happens, the chance of drawing $Y'=1$ is $(2/5)/(1/5+2/5)=2/3$. The one-sweep transition probability to $(1,1)$ is therefore $(2/5)(2/3)=4/15$. The target mass of $(1,1)$ is $2/5$, a different quantity. Repeating sweeps is what permits the chain to approach the joint target from this fixed start.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§10.1, 10.4–10.5, pp. 27–29)

**Your turn.** From the same target, begin at $(1,1)$ and use the same $X$-then-$Y$ update order. What is the one-sweep probability of ending at $(0,0)$? Show both conditional factors before multiplying.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§10.1, 10.4, pp. 27–28)

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
Given the old $Y=1$, $\Pr(X'=0\mid Y=1)=(1/10)/(1/10+2/5)=1/5$. Given the *new* $X'=0$, $\Pr(Y'=0\mid X'=0)=(3/10)/(3/10+1/10)=3/4$. The transition probability is $(1/5)(3/4)=3/20$. Using the old $X=1$ for the second factor would calculate a different transition rule.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§10.1, 10.4, pp. 27–28)
:::

## Common misconceptions
Sampling from full conditionals does not make consecutive states independent, and accepting every conditional draw does not guarantee fast mixing. The sampler's invariant law is not its distribution after one sweep from an arbitrary fixed start. A marginal posterior of $X_i$ integrates over the other coordinates; a full conditional fixes them. Updating each coordinate from its marginal instead would be a different algorithm, and independently sampling from separately chosen conditional-looking distributions may fail to target any claimed joint law.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§10.1–10.6, pp. 27–30)

## Assumptions and limits
The target must be proper and each used full conditional must be defined and sampleable on the chain's reachable support. Zero-probability regions can prevent the coordinate moves needed to connect parts of that support. In high dimensions, a valid stationary distribution alone is insufficient evidence that a finite run explored all important modes. Diagnostics can reveal problems but cannot prove adequate exploration in every case. If conditionals are supplied independently rather than derived from one joint density, their compatibility is an additional model question.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§5.1, 10.1–10.6, pp. 9–10, 27–30){{cite:chen_ip2015}} (§§1, 3, pp. 3266–3271)

## History
Geman and Geman used sequential local conditional updates for image restoration in 1984 and analysed convergence in their finite positive-state setting. Gelfand and Smith's 1990 paper brought conditional simulation into Bayesian marginal calculations. The name describes the Gibbs distributions used in the earlier image model; the present statistical method covers many other joint targets.{{cite:geman_geman1984}} (§§XI–XII, pp. 731–733){{cite:gelfand_smith1990}} (§§1–2.3, pp. 398–401)
