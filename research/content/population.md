## Lead context
A study names a collection it wants to learn about. That collection is the population. The observations in hand are evidence about it only when the study says how they were taken from it. {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 428) {{cite:abs_glossary2023}} (Glossary, “Population”)

## Motivating example
A ministry wants the mean rent paid by households in a city. The population is those households, not the 400 who answered a web form, and not the rents as an abstract set of numbers. The 400 are a sample, or they are a biased slice, depending on how they were reached. {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 428) {{cite:abs_glossary2023}} (Glossary, “Sample (partial enumeration)”) {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 469)

## Intuition
The population is the reference universe of the question. Change the question and the population changes. "Households in the city this year" and "households that answered the form" are different universes. A perfect description of the second is not a description of the first. {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 428) {{cite:abs_glossary2023}} (Glossary, “Scope”)

## Definition
A population is a collection of statistical units of the same nature, the units whose quantifiable information the study cares about. It is the reference universe of the problem. {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 428) In the same sense it is any complete group with at least one characteristic in common, the entire set of potential observations about which the study wants to learn. {{cite:abs_glossary2023}} (Glossary, “Population”) {{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, “population”)

Citizens of a state, trees in a forest, workers in a factory, and prices of goods are examples of discrete populations of units. {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 428)

## Properties
A parameter is a quantitative feature of a population, such as its mean. The parameter is usually unknown. A statistic computed from a sample estimates it. {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 412) {{cite:abs_glossary2023}} (Glossary, “Estimate”) {{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, “statistic”)

A census observes every unit in the population. A sample observes a subset. {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 64) {{cite:abs_glossary2023}} (Glossary, “Census (complete enumeration)”) The list from which the sample is actually drawn is the frame. It matches the population only when every unit of interest is on the list and nothing else is. {{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, “sample frame”) {{cite:abs_glossary2023}} (Glossary, “Coverage”)

## Statistical use
Every inferential statement is a statement about a named population. A confidence interval for a mean is an interval procedure aimed at the population mean, not at the sample mean, which is already known once the sample is in hand. {{cite:abs_glossary2023}} (Glossary, “Inferential statistics”) {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 518) {{cite:abs_glossary2023}} (Glossary, “Confidence interval”)

## Worked example
A population of 5 workshops has daily outputs 10, 12, 14, 16, and 18 tonnes. The population mean is (10+12+14+16+18)/5 = 70/5 = 14. A sample of the first and the last has mean (10+18)/2 = 14. A sample of the two smallest has mean (10+12)/2 = 11. Both are samples from the same population. They estimate the same parameter and they are not equal. {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 412) {{cite:abs_glossary2023}} (Glossary, “Sample (partial enumeration)”)

**Your turn.** A sixth workshop with output 20 is added to the population. What is the new population mean? If the sample of the two smallest is unchanged, what is its mean?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The population total is 70 + 20 = 90 over 6 workshops, so the mean is 15. The sample mean stays 11.
:::

## Common misconceptions
The population is not the sample, and it is not a probability distribution. A model may describe the population with a distribution. The population itself is the collection of units. {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 428) {{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, “population”) {{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, “model”)

"The population of numbers" is loose speech. The units come first. The numbers are measurements on the units. {{cite:abs_glossary2023}} (Glossary, “Data unit”) {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 428)

## Assumptions and limits
Some populations are real and finite, like the workshops. Others are hypothetical, like the outputs a process would produce if it kept running. The arithmetic of an average looks the same. The sentence about what the average refers to does not. {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 428) {{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, “population”)

## Connections
A [census](census.qmd) covers the population. A [sample](sample.qmd) studies part of it. A [parameter](parameter.qmd) describes the population. A [statistic](statistic.qmd) describes the sample. The step from the statistic to the parameter is an inference. {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 518) {{cite:abs_glossary2023}} (Glossary, “Statistic”)

## History
Counting a whole population is the older official practice. Using a part of the population as evidence about the rest became a disciplined method much later, and the disasters of unrepresentative lists are part of that history. {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 64) {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 468) {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 469)
