## Lead context

A life insurer that charges the same premium to every age will, if the price is an average across ages, collect too little from the ages that die sooner. Actuarial science is the mathematical practice of putting a number on uncertain future payments, especially the payments that depend on how long people live and on whether insured events occur. The number is an expected present value under an explicit probability of the event that triggers the payment, and under an explicit interest assumption that moves money through time.

## Definition

An actuary applies mathematical approaches to anticipate, measure, and manage risk: identify risks, document them, monitor them, and turn a recommendation into a premium, a reserve, or a benefit rule. The work rests on theory, data, and experience, and on professional judgment, because the same calculation affects people whose interests differ: the policyholder, the insurer, and the pension members.{{cite:soa_glossary2022}} (p. 3, "Actuary")

The risks are actuarial when occurrence, timing, or severity is uncertain. Mortality is the incidence of death in a population, often the probability that a person of a given age and place dies within a stated period. An annuity is a contract for a series of payments at regular intervals over a term of years or over a lifetime. Liabilities, in the actuarial sense, are the actuarial present value of the future expected payments to be paid from an insurer or a pension plan. A net premium, in a net-premium valuation, is the premium calculated on the valuation assumptions, payable under the same conditions as the office premium, that will provide the contractual benefits.{{cite:soa_glossary2022}} (p. 5, "Annuity"; p. 40, "Liabilities"; p. 45, "Mortality"; p. 46, "Net Premium")

Net-premium valuation is the reserve method in which those net premiums are the amounts necessary to pay benefits on stated mortality and interest assumptions. Company expenses are not in the net-premium reserve, and the reserve carries no explicit margin for profit or for adverse experience. In words the glossary uses, the reserve is the present value of the liability, net of the present value of the future theoretical premium, and net of expense and charges.{{cite:soa_glossary2022}} (p. 46, "Net Premium Valuation")

## Interpretation

Read a one-year term insurance as the smallest case of that definition. The benefit is a fixed sum paid if the insured dies during the year. The mortality assumption is a probability \(q\) of that death. The interest assumption is a rate \(i\) used to discount a payment made at the end of the year. The expected present value of the benefit is
\[
\frac{q \times \text{benefit}}{1+i}.
\]
That quantity is the net premium that, on these assumptions, provides the contractual benefit. Take a benefit of \(1040\), a mortality probability \(q=0.01\), and an interest rate \(i=0.04\). The discounted benefit is \(1040/1.04 = 1000\), and the expected present value is \(1000 \times 0.01 = 10\). The office premium charged to the policyholder is a different number once expenses and a margin are loaded on. The net premium is the benefit piece alone.{{cite:soa_glossary2022}} (p. 46, "Net Premium"; "Net Premium Valuation")

Two assumptions move the same premium in opposite directions that are easy to see in the formula. A higher \(q\) raises the expected payout in proportion. A higher \(i\) shrinks the present value of a payment that is a year away. Setting \(i=0\) recovers the pure expected payout \(q \times \text{benefit}\), which is \(10.40\) for the numbers above, not \(10\). The four-tenths of a unit is the interest discount on a one-year delay, and it is part of the valuation rather than a rounding of the mortality probability.

The same reading scales to a lifetime contract, with one change of bookkeeping. A life annuity pays while the annuitant is alive, so each future payment is multiplied by the probability of still being alive at that payment date, and then discounted. A whole-life insurance pays on death, so each possible year of death contributes the probability of dying in that year times the discounted benefit. Both are present values of expected payments. The life table is the schedule of those age-specific probabilities. Mortality, as the glossary defines it, is the probability that a person of a stated age dies within a stated period, and the table is that probability written out by age.{{cite:soa_glossary2022}} (p. 5, "Annuity"; p. 45, "Mortality")

A reserve is the liabilities that remain after the net premiums still to be collected have been subtracted, on the same mortality and interest assumptions. Early in a contract the present value of future benefits can exceed the present value of future net premiums, and the difference is a positive reserve the insurer holds. A negative reserve, in the glossary's terms, arises when the expected value of future claims is smaller than the expected value of future premium. The sign is a comparison of two present values, not a comment on whether the business is sound.{{cite:soa_glossary2022}} (p. 40, "Liabilities"; p. 46, "Net Premium Valuation")

## Statistical use

Pricing and reserving are expectations. The probability model supplies \(q\), or a schedule of \(q\) by age. The interest model supplies the discount. The contract supplies the payment. The actuarial present value multiplies them. Changing any one of the three changes the premium and the reserve. A best-estimate calculation uses assumptions that are not deliberately padded. A statutory reserve may use a different mortality or interest assumption from the one used to price the contract; the glossary's net-premium valuation is explicit that the reserve uses theoretical premiums with no link required to the premium actually charged.{{cite:soa_glossary2022}} (p. 46, "Net Premium Valuation")

The data behind \(q\) are durations: how long people lived, and whether they were still under observation when the study stopped. A death during the study is an exact lifetime. A person who leaves observation alive contributes a lifetime known only to exceed the time already seen, which is the censored record studied in [censoring](censoring-statistics.qmd). The [survival function](survival-function.qmd) is the probability of still being alive, and it is the factor that multiplies each future annuity payment. [Regression](least-squares.qmd) and [generalised linear models](generalised-linear-model.qmd) enter when the rate depends on rating factors such as age and smoking, so that \(q\) is not a single number for everyone of a given age.

The [law of large numbers](law-of-large-numbers.qmd) is why a pool of similar policies can be priced from an average that no single policy will match. One life either claims the benefit or does not. A large book of lives, each with probability \(q\) and each independent given the mortality assumption, has a total payout whose relative fluctuation shrinks as the number of policies grows. The premium \(10\) in the one-year example is not a prediction that this policyholder will cost \(10\). It is the expected cost that is fair across a portfolio of such policies on the stated assumptions.

Adverse selection is the statistical complication the glossary names directly: people with a more than average likelihood of loss are more likely to buy or to keep the insurance, and higher lapse rates can raise the mortality of those who remain. The \(q\) estimated from a population life table is then the wrong probability for the lives who actually hold the contract. The premium calculation is only as good as the match between the mortality assumption and the lives being insured.{{cite:soa_glossary2022}} (p. 4, "Adverse Selection")

## History

The first demographic studies, in the sense of counting human populations from lists of deaths, date from the middle of the seventeenth century. John Graunt analysed the London bills of mortality, the only population data of that kind then available, and in 1662 published a study of family size, migration, and the structure of the population. With William Petty he argued for more serious population study and for a centre that would gather the statistical data. Through the eighteenth century the same kinds of analysis were refined. In the nineteenth century several European countries and the United States undertook national censuses and began regular records of births, marriages, and deaths. Those records showed that regions differed in the chance of survival, and the gathered data made prediction possible.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 156)

Abraham de Moivre, whose *Doctrine of Chances* appeared in 1718, contributed to actuarial science as well as to the normal density later used for games of chance and for measurement. The profession that now combines a mortality table, an interest rate, and a contract grew up with life insurance and with pension promises, once the population records existed to support a schedule of \(q\). The glossary definitions used above are the modern statement of that combination: a probability of the insured event, a discount, and a present value of the resulting payments.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 356){{cite:soa_glossary2022}} (p. 46, "Net Premium Valuation")

## Connections

[Demography](demography.qmd) measures the same births, deaths, and migrations, and it stops at the population account. Actuarial work takes the mortality schedule from that account and prices a contract written on it. [Epidemiology](epidemiology.qmd) and [biostatistics](biostatistics.qmd) share the survival model and the incomplete follow-up, and they ask about disease rather than about a premium. [Econometrics](econometrics.qmd) shares the discounting of uncertain future streams when the stream is a market price rather than a life-contingent payment. The object common to the actuarial calculation is an expectation of a future payment under an explicit probability of the event that triggers it, discounted at an explicit interest rate.{{cite:soa_glossary2022}} (p. 40, "Liabilities"){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 156)
