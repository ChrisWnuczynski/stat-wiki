## Lead context

Three distributions can share a mean and a standard deviation and still look nothing alike: one with two peaks, one with a single peak, and one with a long right tail. A graph is the comparison that makes those shapes obvious. A mean and a standard deviation, printed alone, do not. OpenIntro draws three populations, each with mean 0 and standard deviation 1, whose histograms a reader would not confuse, and whose numerical summaries a reader would. The picture is doing the work the two numbers cannot do. That is the reason statistical graphics sit at the start of an analysis rather than at the end as decoration.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 2, §2.1.4)

## Intuition

A table of frequencies asks the reader to compare many cells. A graph turns the same comparison into position, length, or area, which the eye can rank. The purpose of the display is a rapid visualisation: the general behaviour of the phenomenon, and the important factors, made obvious. A graph can translate a frequency table or complete it. It does not replace the table when the reader needs the exact count, and the table does not replace the graph when the reader needs the shape. The choice between them is a choice about which comparison is the one the question asks. Interest rates in a sample of loans, drawn as a histogram, pile up under 15 percent and leave a thin right tail above 20 percent. The mean of 11.57 percent and the standard deviation of 5.05 percentage points are compatible with that shape and with other shapes. The histogram is what shows which shape occurred.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 236){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 2, §2.1.3, §2.1.4)

## Definition

A statistical graphic plots points, bars, lines, or other marks so that a data set can be read at a glance. Graphical representations, in Dodge's wording, cover techniques that clarify, interpret, and analyse data by plotting points and drawing line segments, surfaces, and other geometric forms or symbols. The graph is a form of data representation. Which picture is used depends on the kind of data, the nature of the variable, and the goal of the study.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 236–237)

Charts for categorical counts include the pie chart, the line chart, and the pictogram. Dodge calls this group quantitative graphics in the sense that they represent qualitative categorical variables. Frequency charts for a quantitative variable include the histogram and the stem-and-leaf diagram. A Cartesian plot uses perpendicular axes that meet at an origin. A [histogram](histogram.qmd) splits the range into bins and counts the observations in each bin. Its vertical axis is frequency and its horizontal axis is the variable. It is built to show centre, spread, skewness, outliers, and modes, which together suggest a distributional model that a probability plot or a goodness-of-fit test can then check. A [scatterplot](scatterplot.qmd) shows the joint behaviour of two numerical variables, one point per case, with the response usually on the vertical axis and a suspected related variable on the horizontal axis. A [line chart](line-chart.qmd), in the categorical group, places a frequency at each discrete value. [Summary statistics](summary-statistics.qmd) are the numerical companions of these pictures.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 237){{cite:nist_histogram}} (§1.3.3.14, “Purpose” and “Definition”){{cite:nist_scatterplot2002}} (§1.3.3.26, “Definition”){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 2, §2.1.1)

Exploratory analysis uses these displays heavily, and the displays are not the whole of that analysis. Exploratory data analysis is an approach that postpones the usual assumptions about the model and lets the data reveal structure. It is not identical to statistical graphics, although the two are often spoken of together. Graphics are the collection of techniques. The exploratory approach is the decision to look before choosing a model, and Tukey’s 1977 book is the work the handbook names as seminal for that decision.{{cite:nist_eda2002}} (§1.1.1, “Philosophy” and “History”)

## Statistical use

Use a histogram to ask what population shape is plausible, where the data sit, how spread out they are, whether they are symmetric, and whether a few points stand apart. The questions the histogram is built to answer are the ones in its purpose statement: centre, spread, skewness, outliers, and modes. Observations that fall on a bin boundary need a rule. A common convention puts them in the lower bin. The bin width is a choice, not a property of the data. Too few bins hide modes. Too many bins turn sampling noise into apparent peaks. Scott’s rules are one systematic answer. An arbitrary choice by the user is another, and it has to be stated, because a different width can change the number of modes a reader reports. The cumulative histogram is the variant whose vertical axis adds the counts of all bins up to the current one. Either form is a summary of one numerical variable.{{cite:nist_histogram}} (§1.3.3.14, “Questions” and “Definition”){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 2, §2.1.3)

Use a scatterplot to ask whether two variables move together, whether the relation is linear, whether it is curved, whether the vertical spread changes with the horizontal variable, and whether a point is isolated. The Alaska pipeline example in the handbook is a linear relationship that suggests a linear regression. Other examples in the same section are a quadratic, an exponential, a damped sine, a fan shape in which the spread of the response changes with the predictor, and a single outlier. The plot shows association. Association is not causation. The handbook’s heading is the claim: causality is not proved by association. A scatterplot is a diagnostic for structured association, linear or otherwise. If the association exists, the plot may or may not suggest a mechanism, and the mechanism is a subject-matter question. Several scatterplots on one page, or a scatterplot matrix, extend the same idea past two variables. A co-plot conditions the cloud on a third variable.{{cite:nist_scatterplot2002}} (§1.3.3.26, “Questions” and “Causality Is Not Proved By Association”){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 2, §2.1.1)

A pie chart can show how a whole breaks into categories, and a bar plot of the same counts makes the comparison of those categories easier to read. For a loan sample, the excess of one housing category over another is immediate in a bar plot of counts and slower in a pie chart, because angles are harder to compare than lengths. Row and column proportions are a further display of the same table. The printed proportions can fail to add to 1 in the last decimal, as when 0.851 and 0.150 add to 1.001, and the graph of those proportions inherits the rounding. The line chart belongs with the categorical displays. A stem-and-leaf diagram keeps the digits of a quantitative batch while showing the same shape a histogram would show, which is why Tukey added it to the exploratory toolkit in 1977, after the box plot of 1972.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 2, §2.2.2, §2.2.5){{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 236–237)

## Assumptions and limits

A graph is an argument about a scale. Omitting zero on a bar chart changes the visual ratio of two counts. Changing the bin width changes the number of modes. Plotting counts where rates were required draws a map of population size and calls it a map of risk. The educational goal attached to graphics is to say what the graph does not reveal, not only to name the kind of chart. A histogram does not prove a distributional model. It suggests one, and a probability plot or a goodness-of-fit procedure is the check. A scatterplot does not prove a cause. It shows an association, and the association can be produced by a third variable that was not drawn. A pie chart of a sample is a picture of the sample. It becomes a picture of a population only under the sampling design, which the chart does not contain.{{cite:nist_histogram}} (§1.3.3.14){{cite:nist_scatterplot2002}} (§1.3.3.26){{cite:gaise2016}} (p. 8, goal 3)

The same limits apply to the companions of the picture. A mean and a standard deviation attached to a histogram are useful when the shape is close to the shape those summaries assume. Three distributions with the same mean and the same standard deviation are the counterexample, and they are the reason the picture comes first. Exploratory analysis is not a licence to choose the display that flatters a conclusion. It is a discipline of looking before the model is fixed, and of reporting the display that would have changed the model. Tukey’s programme, and the handbook’s restatement of it, treats the graphic as a tool inside that discipline, not as a substitute for it.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 2, §2.1.4){{cite:nist_eda2002}} (§1.1.1)

## History

Plotting a point in a coordinate plane goes back to Greek geometry, and the mathematical development of that idea is Descartes’s. Crome used graphical statistical displays as a teaching device, including representations later read as pie charts, in publications of 1785 and 1820. Playfair’s *Commercial and Political Atlas* of 1786 used line charts for trade. Guerry’s 1833 essay on the moral statistics of France described frequencies of crimes and is one of the early frequency displays. Pearson later gave the histogram its name. Playfair’s interest in the balance of trade went with his use of line charts, histograms, and pie charts, and Guerry’s essay is an early use of a frequency distribution. Schmid’s 1954 handbook surveyed the types then in use. Tukey added the box plot in 1972 and the stem-and-leaf diagram in 1977, as part of exploratory analysis. The stem-and-leaf diagram is similar to the histogram, and the histogram itself dates from the eighteenth century, which is older than Pearson’s name for it. The handbook’s history of exploratory analysis starts from Tukey 1977 and from the books that followed, including Mosteller and Tukey, Hoaglin, and Velleman and Hoaglin. The modern default, a histogram for one variable and a scatterplot for two, is the pair those histories converge on.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 192, 236–237){{cite:nist_eda2002}} (§1.1.1, “History”)
