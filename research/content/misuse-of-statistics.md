## Lead context

A paper can be wrong in a way that no arithmetic error will reveal. The sample was whoever answered. The analysis was one of many that were tried. The published sentence treats a correlation as a cause, or a significant test as a large effect. Misuse of statistics is this family of failures: the numbers are real, and the conclusion drawn from them is not supported by the design or the analysis that produced them. Official agencies are explicitly entitled to comment when their figures are read that way. Scientific literatures have a parallel problem when the path from data to a reported finding is flexible.{{cite:un_res68261}} (Principle 4){{cite:ioannidis2005}} (p. 0696)

## Definition

Misuse of statistics is the drawing of a conclusion that the sampling, the experiment, or the analysis does not warrant. It includes erroneous interpretation of a published official figure. Principle 4 of the Fundamental Principles of Official Statistics names that case directly: statistical agencies are entitled to comment on erroneous interpretation and misuse of statistics. Principle 3 is the paired requirement that agencies present sources, methods, and procedures according to scientific standards, so that a reader can see what the figure is.{{cite:un_res68261}} (Principles 3 and 4)

The category also includes practices that manufacture a statistically significant claim. In Ioannidis's framework a research finding is any relationship that reaches formal statistical significance, and bias is the combination of design, data, analysis, and presentation choices that produce such a finding when one should not have been produced. Selective or distorted reporting is the typical form. Chance error is a different object. A study can be honest and still be wrong because of sampling variation. Misuse is the extra distortion that comes from how the study was built or how it was shown.{{cite:ioannidis2005}} (p. 0696)

The category is wider than fraud. Changing the definition of unemployment changes the official rate even when every form is counted correctly. Political power has repeatedly set the boundary of what official statistics study, expanding or limiting the contents, and definitions of poverty, wellness, and occupational class are places where that choice is routine. An observational comparison can be numerically exact and still be the wrong basis for a causal sentence. A tiny p-value on a tiny effect can be reported as if it were a large one. Each of these is a misuse when the stronger claim is stated as though the method had established it.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 394–395, Official statistics){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.4)

## Statistical use

The tools that detect misuse are the same tools that do the job properly, applied to the claim rather than only to the table. A [confidence interval](confidence-interval.qmd) shows whether an effect is precisely estimated or merely away from zero. A [p-value](p-value.qmd) is a tail probability under a stated model, not a probability that the claim is true. Where many relationships are tested, the positive predictive value of a significant claim depends on the prior odds of a true relationship and on the power. With a type I error α, power 1−β, and R the ratio of true relationships to false ones among those tested, the positive predictive value is (1−β)R / (R − βR + α). A finding is more likely true than false, before bias is added, only when that quantity exceeds one half, which rearranges to (1−β)R > α. At α = 0.05 the threshold is (1−β)R > 0.05. Flexibility in outcomes and analyses acts like a further bias that moves non-findings into the reported column.{{cite:ioannidis2005}} (pp. 0696–0697)

In official series, the safeguard is documentation. A reader who cannot see how unemployment was defined cannot tell whether a drop is a labour-market change or a definitional one. International comparisons fail for the same reason when two states collect the same word under different rules. Objectivity is a requirement of the official system, and it is also a reason agencies publish methods: a figure without a method cannot be checked against the claim that is being made about it.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 395, comparability and objectivity){{cite:un_res68261}} (Principle 3)

Observational studies support associations and hypotheses. They do not, by themselves, support the causal conclusion that would be reasonable from a randomised experiment. A [confounding variable](confounding-variable.qmd) is correlated with both the explanatory variable and the response. Sun exposure, in a comparison of sunscreen use and skin cancer, can produce an association that is not the effect of the sunscreen. Searching for confounders helps and does not finish the job, because an unmeasured one can remain. Treating the association as the effect is the misuse. Reporting the association as an association is the analysis the design supports.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §§1.2.5 and 1.3.4)

## Common misconceptions

A significant test is not a large effect, and it is not a proof that the claim is true. It is a tail probability under a model that was supposed to be fixed before the data were searched. A study with a flexible choice of outcome, of covariate, or of subgroup can report a tail probability that was computed as if the choice had been fixed. The printed p-value then describes a different experiment from the one that was performed.{{cite:ioannidis2005}} (pp. 0696–0697)

A large official percentage is not, by itself, a verdict. The percentage inherits the definition, the coverage, and the non-response of the collection. Principle 4 exists because a correct count can be paired with an incorrect sentence. Disagreeing with the sentence is part of the statistical agency's role, not a political extra.{{cite:un_res68261}} (Principle 4){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 394)

An observational association that survives a list of measured covariates is still an observational association. The list can be long and the coefficient can be stable, and an unmeasured common cause can still be the reason the groups differ. The misuse is the causal verb, not the arithmetic that produced the coefficient.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.4)

## Connections

Misuse is the reason [statistical literacy](statistical-literacy.qmd) is taught as more than formula-reading, and it is part of the background to the [replication crisis](replication-crisis.qmd). [Sampling bias](sampling-bias.qmd), a convenience sample, and non-response are design failures that no later regression will honestly repair. [Multiple testing](multiple-testing.qmd) and the [Benjamini–Hochberg](benjamini-hochberg.qmd) procedure are the formal answer when many hypotheses are examined on purpose. [Open science](open-science.qmd) is the institutional attempt to make the analysis plan and the data visible before the significant result is the only thing a reader can see. A corrected analysis is a refusal to let a significant test, a dramatic chart, or an official percentage carry a conclusion the study did not earn.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3){{cite:ioannidis2005}} (p. 0696){{cite:un_res68261}} (Principles 3 and 4)

## History

The temptation to treat a count as a verdict is as old as official numbers. Kings counted soldiers and stores, and later states defined unemployment, poverty, and occupational class in ways that moved the published level. The twentieth-century argument about false discoveries is more specific. It treats a significant result as a claim that may be false even when the arithmetic is correct, and it lists the conditions that make that outcome likely: small samples, small effects, many tested relationships, flexible analysis, and interests that reward a positive report. Selective outcome reporting has been documented even for randomised trials, so the problem is not confined to obviously weak designs. The 2014 General Assembly resolution that restated the 1994 Fundamental Principles put the right to comment on misuse into the public rules of official statistics, next to the duty to publish methods.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 393–395){{cite:ioannidis2005}} (pp. 0696–0698){{cite:un_res68261}} (Principles 3 and 4)
