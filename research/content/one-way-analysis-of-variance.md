## Motivating example
Five corn varieties grown on several plots can have different observed average yields even if their true means are equal. One-way analysis of variance asks whether the variation *between* variety means is large compared with the plot-to-plot variation *within* varieties. The comparison is useful because the same within-variety scatter supplies a scale for judging all groups, rather than inviting a collection of unplanned pairwise tests. The corn plot makes the two sources of variation visible before the sums of squares are calculated.{{cite:nist_oneway_anova}} (Description, Model and Testing){{cite:fisher1925_smrw}} (Ch. VII, §38, pp. 177–178)

## Intuition
Every observation's distance from the grand mean can be split into its group's distance from the grand mean and its own distance from the group mean. Squaring and adding those distances divides total variation into between-group and within-group pieces. If the true group means are equal, both mean squares estimate the same error variance, so their ratio tends to be near one. If the means differ, the between-group mean square tends to grow while the within-group denominator still measures ordinary scatter. A large ratio is evidence against equal means, provided the design and model assumptions justify its reference distribution.{{cite:nist_oneway_anova}} (§3.2.3.1, ANOVA table and Testing){{cite:fisher1925_smrw}} (Ch. VII, §38, pp. 177–178)

## Definition
With $k$ groups and $n_j$ observations in group $j$, the usual one-way normal model writes $Y_{ij}=\mu_j+\varepsilon_{ij}$ with independent $\varepsilon_{ij}\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$. The null is $H_0:\mu_1=\cdots=\mu_k$; its alternative says at least one mean differs. Using group means $\overline Y_{\cdot j}$ and the weighted grand mean $\overline Y_{\cdot\cdot}$, define $\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{treatment}}=\sum_jn_j(\overline Y_{\cdot j}-\overline Y_{\cdot\cdot})^2$ and $\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{error}}=\sum_{j,i}(Y_{ij}-\overline Y_{\cdot j})^2$. The test statistic is $F=\{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{treatment}}/(k-1)\}/\{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{error}}/(n-k)\}$. Under the null and stated normal assumptions it has the $F_{k-1,n-k}$ distribution.{{cite:nist_oneway_anova}} (§3.2.3.1, Model, ANOVA table and Assumptions)

## Interpretation — What the F ratio answers
Rejecting equal means says that the full set of group means is not constant; it does not tell which pair differs or by how much. A nonsignificant result does not prove equal effects, especially with few noisy observations. The F statistic is a ratio of two estimated variances, not a ratio of the largest and smallest group means. Its denominator also makes assumptions tangible: if one group has far more variable outcomes than another, a pooled within-group mean square may be a poor common scale. Random assignment helps interpret differences as effects of treatments; without it, ANOVA still compares conditional group means but confounding can change the causal story.{{cite:nist_oneway_anova}} (§3.2.3.1, Testing and Assumptions){{cite:fisher1925_smrw}} (Ch. VII, §38 and Ch. VIII, experimental applications)

## Properties — Decomposition and reference law
The identity $\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{total}}=\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{treatment}}+\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{error}}$ holds algebraically for any observed numbers, including unbalanced group sizes. The *F reference law* is a separate probabilistic conclusion: normal, independent equal-variance errors give the treatment and error sums of squares independent scaled chi-squared distributions under $H_0$. Their degrees of freedom are $k-1$ and $n-k$, respectively. For $k=2$, the ANOVA F equals the square of the pooled two-sample t statistic. These relationships explain why the table separates sums of squares from the assumptions that turn them into a test.{{cite:nist_oneway_anova}} (§3.2.3.1, ANOVA table and Testing){{cite:fisher1925_smrw}} (Ch. VII, analysis of variance)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
Write $Y_{ij}-\overline Y_{\cdot\cdot}=(Y_{ij}-\overline Y_{\cdot j})+(\overline Y_{\cdot j}-\overline Y_{\cdot\cdot})$. On squaring and summing, the cross term vanishes because $\sum_i(Y_{ij}-\overline Y_{\cdot j})=0$ in each group. This proves the sum-of-squares decomposition. Under the null normal model, the vector of all $n$ standardized observations is isotropic normal. Projection onto the $k$-dimensional space of vectors constant within each group gives the group means; removing the one-dimensional constant direction leaves $k-1$ orthogonal normal coordinates whose squared length is $\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{treatment}}/\sigma^2$. The orthogonal residual space has dimension $n-k$ and squared length $\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{error}}/\sigma^2$. Orthogonal normal projections are independent, so the two quantities have independent $\chi^2_{k-1}$ and $\chi^2_{n-k}$ laws; their degrees-of-freedom-scaled ratio has the stated F law. For two groups the between sum of squares is $(\overline Y_1-\overline Y_2)^2/(1/n_1+1/n_2)$, which makes $F=T^2$ after dividing by the pooled error variance.{{cite:nist_oneway_anova}} (§3.2.3.1, ANOVA table, Testing and Assumptions)
:::

## Statistical use — From omnibus test to contrasts
The one-way F test is an omnibus first step when the scientific question is whether any group mean differs. A planned contrast, such as corn variety three versus the average of varieties one, two, four and five, answers a more specific question and can be more sensitive to that pattern. If many pairwise comparisons are selected after looking at the data, their separate error rates do not control the chance of at least one false claim across the family. Tukey intervals or a Bonferroni correction account for that multiplicity. The page's corn example gives $F=6.39$ and $p=0.008$ for the five-variety omnibus test, then uses a prespecified contrast to ask a different question; neither calculation licenses a causal conclusion without considering how plots were assigned.{{cite:nist_oneway_anova}} (§3.2.3.1, Testing and example){{cite:fisher1925_smrw}} (Ch. VII–VIII, variance analysis and experiments)

The five-treatment cold-sore example illustrates the same structure with human outcomes: a small p-value for the overall F test motivates careful follow-up rather than a declaration that every treatment differs. Residual-versus-fitted and normal-quantile plots can reveal spread and shape problems, but neither plot proves independence. Independence is chiefly about sampling and assignment: several observations on one patient are not equivalent to separate randomly assigned patients.{{cite:nist_oneway_anova}} (§3.2.3.1, Assumptions and Uses)

If the highest-mean group also has much larger variance and group sizes differ sharply, the pooled denominator can give a misleading reference comparison; a heteroscedastic method such as Welch's ANOVA addresses the variance issue. If three measurements on one patient are treated as three independent patients, the degrees of freedom exaggerate the independent information. If scores have a severe ceiling effect, the equal-variance normal error model may fit badly even with balanced groups. These are variance, dependence and response-shape failures, respectively; each needs a different design or model response.{{cite:nist_oneway_anova}} (§3.2.3.1, Assumptions, equal Gaussian spread and independent observations)

## Worked example
Suppose three teaching methods produce scores $A=(7,8,9)$, $B=(8,9,10)$ and $C=(11,12,13)$. Their means are $8$, $9$ and $12$, and the grand mean is $87/9=29/3\approx9.666667$. The between-group sum of squares is $3[(8-29/3)^2+(9-29/3)^2+(12-29/3)^2]=26$. Each group has within-group squared deviations $1+0+1=2$, so $\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{error}}=6$ and $\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{total}}=32$. With degrees of freedom $2$ and $6$, the mean squares are $13$ and $1$, giving $F=13$. Under the normal equal-variance null, $\mathbb{P}(F_{2,6}\ge13)=27/4096\approx0.006592$. The data support a difference among means; the calculation alone does not isolate which method is responsible or establish that method caused the difference.{{cite:nist_oneway_anova}} (§3.2.3.1, ANOVA table and Testing)

**Your turn.** Two groups have observations $(4,5,6)$ and $(6,7,8)$. Compute their means, treatment and error sums of squares, F statistic and pooled two-sample t statistic. Check the relation between F and t.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The group means are $5$ and $7$, and the grand mean is $6$. The treatment sum of squares is $3(5-6)^2+3(7-6)^2=6$. Each group's within sum is $1+0+1=2$, so error sum is $4$ on $6-2=4$ degrees of freedom. Therefore $F=(6/1)/(4/4)=6$. The pooled variance is $s_p^2=1$, so $T=(5-7)/\sqrt{1(1/3+1/3)}=-\sqrt6\approx-2.449490$ and $T^2=6=F$.
:::

Three groups have observations $(1,3)$, $(2,2)$ and $(5,5)$. Compute the treatment sum of squares, the error sum of squares, and $F$.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The group means are 2, 2 and 5, and the grand mean is 3. The treatment sum of squares is $2(2-3)^2+2(2-3)^2+2(5-3)^2=12$. The error sum of squares is $1+1+0+0+0+0=2$. With 2 and 3 degrees of freedom, $F=(12/2)/(2/3)=9$.
:::

## Common misconceptions
The F test does not show that all group means are different; it only tests the joint equality claim. “Analysis of variance” does not mean the target is a variance difference: the ratio of variance estimates tests mean equality. A small p-value does not quantify the size or importance of a mean contrast. Residual normality and equal spread do not follow from seeing a balanced table, and randomization cannot be inferred from a tidy plot. Finally, ten unadjusted 95% pairwise intervals do not form a 95% simultaneous statement about ten pairs.{{cite:nist_oneway_anova}} (§3.2.3.1, Testing and Assumptions)

## Assumptions and limits — The F test is a joint mean comparison
The F test does not show that all group means are different; it only tests the joint equality claim. {{cite:nist_oneway_anova}} (§3.2.3.1, Testing and Assumptions)

## Connections
With two groups, [the pooled t test](two-sample-t-test.qmd) and the one-way F test encode the same null comparison. [Contrasts](contrast.qmd) ask planned questions inside a factor, while [multiple comparisons](multiple-comparisons.qmd) control claims made about many contrasts. The [normal linear model](normal-linear-model.qmd) treats group membership as a design matrix, making ANOVA a special linear hypothesis test. A [two-factor ANOVA](two-factor-analysis-of-variance.qmd) adds a second factor and possibly an interaction; it answers a larger question than the single-factor model.{{cite:nist_oneway_anova}} (§3.2.3.1, Model and ANOVA table)

## History
Fisher's 1925 *Statistical Methods for Research Workers* devoted a chapter to intraclass correlation and the analysis of variance, describing it as a separation of variation attributable to different groups of causes. His following chapter applied variance analysis to experimental questions. The modern one-way F table retains that central partition while making its normal-error conditions, degrees of freedom and decision rule explicit.{{cite:fisher1925_smrw}} (Ch. VII, §38, pp. 177–178; Ch. VIII){{cite:nist_oneway_anova}} (§3.2.3.1, ANOVA table and Testing)
