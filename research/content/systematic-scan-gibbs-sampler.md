## Lead context
A systematic-scan Gibbs sampler turns a difficult joint sampling problem into a repeated sequence of full-conditional draws. Each sweep visits coordinates in a fixed order and uses each newly drawn value as soon as it is available. The resulting sequence targets the joint distribution while generally retaining dependence between successive sweeps.{{cite:geman_geman1984}} (§XI, pp. 731–733) {{cite:gelfand_smith1990}} (§2.3, pp. 400–401)

## Motivating example
Suppose a posterior has two strongly correlated parameters. Sampling each parameter while holding the other fixed may be simple even when drawing both jointly is awkward. Alternating these two conditional draws makes a path of horizontal and vertical steps along the posterior ridge, as the figure shows. The method provides joint posterior draws without evaluating a normalising constant for the whole posterior. When the ridge is narrow, those easy updates may move only a short distance per sweep, so simulation quality must be judged from the chain rather than from the fact that each draw was accepted.{{cite:geman_geman1984}} (§XI, pp. 731–733) {{cite:gelfand_smith1990}} (§§1, 2.3, pp. 398–401) {{cite:geyer1992}} (§§1–2, pp. 473–476)

## Intuition
The word *scan* describes the order in which coordinates are visited. In a two-coordinate sweep, first draw $X$ conditional on the current $Z$, then draw $Z$ conditional on the **new** $X$. The complete pair after both draws is one Markov-chain state. Both single-coordinate updates preserve the desired joint distribution, so their composition does too. Changing their order changes the one-sweep transition probabilities and can change how quickly the chain explores, even when both orders have the same target. This is why the timing superscripts in the definition are part of the algorithm.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§10.4–10.6, pp. 28–30) {{cite:chen_ip2015}} (§§1, 3, pp. 3266–3271)

## Definition
Let $\pi(x_1,\ldots,x_p)$ be a proper target joint density or mass function. For coordinate $i$, $\pi(x_i\mid x_{-i})$ is its *full conditional*: all remaining coordinates are held fixed. A systematic sweep applies the update kernels $K_1,K_2,\ldots,K_p$ in one fixed order, once each, and records the vector only after the last update. The first update in sweep $t+1$ conditions on the previous completed state; later updates condition on the newest earlier coordinates and the still-old later coordinates. A draw from the exact full conditional is accepted, with no Metropolis accept/reject test.{{cite:geman_geman1984}} (§XI, pp. 731–733) {{cite:gelfand_smith1990}} (§2.3, pp. 400–401)

The full conditional is proportional to the joint as a function of the coordinate being updated, but the proportionality constant depends on the fixed coordinates. In deriving it, terms involving the updated coordinate cannot be discarded. The updates must be sampleable and arise from a compatible joint distribution if the intended target is $\pi$. A proposed collection of conditional distributions need not correspond to any joint law; in that situation the scan order may affect more than efficiency.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§10.6, pp. 29–30) {{cite:chen_ip2015}} (Abstract; §§1, 3, pp. 3266–3271)

## Properties
Each coordinate kernel leaves $\pi$ invariant, so a full sweep does too. This guarantees stationarity if a sweep begins with $\pi$; it does not assert that draws from arbitrary starts have already converged. On a finite product state space with strictly positive target mass everywhere, every complete sweep has positive probability of moving between any two states and of staying put. The sweep chain is then irreducible and aperiodic and converges to $\pi$ from every start. Continuous or constrained targets need separate conditions and can have communicating components that single-coordinate updates cannot cross.{{cite:geman_geman1984}} (§XII, Theorem A, p. 733) {{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§10.4–10.5, pp. 28–29)

The fixed-order sweep need not satisfy detailed balance even though each coordinate update does. Its transition kernel depends on composition order. This differs from a random-scan Gibbs sampler, which selects a coordinate at random each update, and from a block Gibbs sampler, which draws multiple coordinates jointly from a block conditional. The choices can have very different mixing when coordinates are strongly dependent. No ordering is universally best; the cost and dependence structure matter.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§10.4–10.6, pp. 28–30) {{cite:geman_geman1984}} (§XI, pp. 731–733)

**Proof idea.** Integrate the old coordinate out and insert its conditional law to show that one update preserves the target. Compose those identities for a sweep. Strict positivity on a finite product space then gives positive one-sweep transitions and the finite-state convergence theorem.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§10.4–10.5, pp. 28–29) {{cite:geman_geman1984}} (§XII, Theorem A, p. 733)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Fix coordinate $i$. If the current vector has joint law $\pi$, the unchanged coordinates have marginal $\pi_{-i}(x_{-i})$. Replacing only coordinate $i$ by a draw from $\pi(\cdot\mid x_{-i})$ gives joint density $\pi_{-i}(x_{-i})\pi(x_i\mid x_{-i})=\pi(x_i,x_{-i})$. Thus $\pi K_i=\pi$. Applying this identity successively to $K_1,\ldots,K_p$ gives $\pi K_1\cdots K_p=\pi$. The argument holds for any fixed permutation of a compatible collection of exact full conditionals. It proves invariance, not independence of successive states.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§10.4–10.6, pp. 28–30)

For a finite product state space, suppose $\pi(x)>0$ at every state. Every full-conditional probability is then positive for every combination of fixed coordinates. To get from any state $x$ to any state $y$ in one sweep, request coordinate $1$ of $y$ at the first update, coordinate $2$ of $y$ at the second, and so on. The product of these conditional probabilities is positive. Taking $y=x$ also gives a positive self-transition. The one-sweep chain is therefore irreducible and aperiodic; since it has invariant distribution $\pi$, the finite-state theorem gives convergence to $\pi$. This proof does not cover zero-mass barriers or general continuous state spaces.{{cite:geman_geman1984}} (§XII, Theorem A, p. 733) {{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§10.4–10.5, pp. 28–29)

Even with all four masses positive, the two-coordinate sweep can depend on order. Starting at $(x,z)$, the chance of ending at $(x',z')$ when updating $X$ then $Z$ is $\pi(x'\mid z)\pi(z'\mid x')$; in the opposite order it is $\pi(z'\mid x)\pi(x'\mid z')$. These products need not agree. Both kernels preserve $\pi$ by the preceding identity, so equal stationary distributions do not imply equal transition probabilities.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§10.4–10.6, pp. 28–30)

:::

## Statistical use
After allowing the chain to explore, posterior means and probabilities can be estimated by averaging retained sweep states. Their Monte Carlo error depends on serial correlation, so reporting only the number of sweeps exaggerates precision when a chain moves slowly. Trace plots, dispersed initial states, autocorrelation and effective sample size help assess the simulation. Discarding a fixed warm-up segment can reduce influence from the start but does not certify that the chain reached its target, and thinning generally discards usable draws unless storage is the constraint.{{cite:geyer1992}} (§§3.3–3.7, pp. 477–480) {{cite:gelman_rubin1992}} (§§1–2, pp. 457–460)

The two linked results above illustrate distinct tasks. In a normal model, full conditionals can be familiar Normal and inverse-Gamma laws even when the marginal variance posterior is not inverse-Gamma under independent priors. The warm-up result concerns how to interpret the simulated path after the updates are specified. Deriving valid conditional laws and diagnosing exploration are both necessary for a useful posterior calculation.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§10.6, pp. 29–30) {{cite:geyer1992}} (§§1–2, pp. 473–476)

## Worked example
Let a joint target for binary $X,Z$ have masses $\pi(0,0)=1/2$, $\pi(0,1)=1/6$, $\pi(1,0)=1/12$ and $\pi(1,1)=1/4$; they sum to one. Start at $(1,0)$. For the order $X$ then $Z$, $P(X'=0\mid Z=0)=(1/2)/(1/2+1/12)=6/7$. Given the newly drawn $X'=0$, $P(Z'=1\mid X'=0)=(1/6)/(1/2+1/6)=1/4$. Hence the chance of moving to $(0,1)$ in one sweep is $(6/7)(1/4)=3/14$. Reversing the scan order gives $P(Z'=1\mid X=1)=(1/4)/(1/12+1/4)=3/4$ and then $P(X'=0\mid Z'=1)=(1/6)/(1/6+1/4)=2/5$, so its one-sweep chance is $(3/4)(2/5)=3/10$. Both scan orders target the same joint law, but they make different transitions.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§10.4–10.6, pp. 28–30)

**Your turn.** From $(0,0)$ under the same four-cell target, calculate the chance of reaching $(1,1)$ in one sweep for $X$-then-$Z$ and for $Z$-then-$X$. Keep the intermediate conditioning value explicit.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§10.6, pp. 29–30)

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
In the $X$-then-$Z$ order, $P(X'=1\mid Z=0)=(1/12)/(1/2+1/12)=1/7$, followed by $P(Z'=1\mid X'=1)=(1/4)/(1/12+1/4)=3/4$. The transition chance is $(1/7)(3/4)=3/28$. In the reverse order, $P(Z'=1\mid X=0)=(1/6)/(1/2+1/6)=1/4$, followed by $P(X'=1\mid Z'=1)=(1/4)/(1/6+1/4)=3/5$. The chance is $(1/4)(3/5)=3/20$. Neither number is the stationary mass $\pi(1,1)=1/4$: a one-sweep transition is a conditional probability given the starting state.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§10.4–10.6, pp. 28–30)
:::

## Common misconceptions
Using all old coordinate values in a sweep defines a different update scheme from the newest-value systematic scan shown here. Accepting every conditional draw does not make successive sweeps independent or guarantee fast exploration. Likewise, a path that appears stable for a while can remain in one mode of a separated target. A fixed number of discarded draws is not a convergence proof, and a smaller collection of thinned draws is not automatically more informative than the full retained chain.{{cite:geman_geman1984}} (§XI, pp. 731–733) {{cite:geyer1992}} (§§3.6–3.7, p. 480) {{cite:gelman_rubin1992}} (§§1–2, pp. 457–460)

## Assumptions and limits
The displayed invariance proof assumes that every coordinate is drawn from the exact conditional of one proper joint target, at states where that conditional is defined. The finite-state convergence claim additionally assumes strictly positive mass on the full product state space. A continuous model may be irreducible under other conditions, but positivity alone in a formula is not a universal convergence proof. Incompatible conditionals supplied separately can give different limits under different scan orders. Strong dependence, narrow ridges and separated modes can cause long autocorrelation even when the mathematical stationary distribution is correct.{{cite:geman_geman1984}} (§XII, Theorem A, p. 733) {{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§10.4–10.6, pp. 28–30) {{cite:chen_ip2015}} (Abstract; §§1, 3, pp. 3266–3271)

## Connections
A [full conditional distribution](full-conditional-distribution.qmd) supplies each update, and a [Markov chain](markov-chain.qmd) links completed sweeps. [Block Gibbs sampling](../notes/bayes-04-mcmc.qmd#s-gibbs-sampling) replaces a group of coordinate draws with one joint block draw when that conditional is tractable. [Metropolis–Hastings](metropolis-hastings-algorithm.qmd) accepts or rejects proposals and can be used within a Gibbs sweep when an exact conditional sampler is unavailable. [Effective sample size](effective-sample-size.qmd) translates dependence into an approximate count of independent draws for a specified summary. The distinction between a stationary law, the transition kernel and the quality of a finite run is central to all of these methods.{{cite:geyer2005_markov_chain}} (§§9–10.6, pp. 25–30) {{cite:geyer1992}} (§§1–3, pp. 473–478)

## History
Geman and Geman's 1984 image-restoration study used sequential conditional updates for pixels and edges and proved convergence under its finite positive-state conditions. The fixed visiting order in that construction is an early systematic scan.{{cite:geman_geman1984}} (§§XI–XII, pp. 731–733)

Gelfand and Smith's 1990 account brought conditional simulation to Bayesian marginal calculations, including hierarchical and missing-data models. Later analysis of incompatible conditionals clarified that scan order can influence the limiting distribution when no common joint target exists. These developments explain why a sampler's update equations and its target must be specified together.{{cite:gelfand_smith1990}} (§§1–3, pp. 398–404) {{cite:chen_ip2015}} (Abstract; §§1, 3, pp. 3266–3271)
