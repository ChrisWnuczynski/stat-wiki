## Lead context
A spectrometer returns hundreds of intensities for each sample, and the chemical question is which of those intensities mark a concentration, a class, or a bad run. Chemometrics is the chemical discipline that uses mathematical and statistical methods to design the measurements and to extract chemical information from the data they produce.

## Definition — Scope
The International Chemometrics Society's early definition, recorded by later writers, is the science of relating measurements made on a chemical system or process to the state of that system by mathematical or statistical methods. A formulation still used in analytical chemistry is more specific about the two jobs: design or select the measurement procedures and experiments, and obtain as much chemical information as possible by analysing the chemical data.{{cite:sharma2023}} (Ch. 1, §1.1){{cite:otto2024}} (Ch. 1, "What is Chemometrics?")

The subject grew up inside chemistry, especially analytical chemistry, where modern instruments produce more numbers than a chemist can inspect one peak at a time. Typical aims are calibration, classification of materials, quantitative structure–activity relations, and the design of the experiment that collects the spectra. The same methods are used on chemical measurements in geology, biology, medicine, and engineering, including forensic chemistry.{{cite:otto2024}} (Ch. 1){{cite:sharma2023}} (Ch. 1, §1.1.1)

## Statistical use
Much of the early toolkit is multivariate and built for prediction. [Principal component](principal-component-directions.qmd) analysis and partial least squares reduce a spectrum to a few directions that still predict a concentration or a class. [Least squares](least-squares.qmd) and other [regression](multiple-linear-regression.qmd) methods relate the instrument response to the chemical quantity. [Designed experiments](designed-experiment.qmd) choose which samples and which instrument settings to run. Later practice added a wider set of multivariate and machine-learning procedures, including tree-based classifiers, as computing made them routine.{{cite:sharma2023}} (Ch. 1, §1.1){{cite:otto2024}} (Ch. 1)

The statistical question is not a new probability theory. It is the same estimation, classification, and experimental-design problem, with the rows being chemical samples and the columns being instrumental channels. Calibration — predicting a concentration from a spectrum — is the applied form that chemists meet most often. Pattern recognition is the form that assigns a sample to a material or a source.{{cite:otto2024}} (Ch. 1){{cite:sharma2023}} (Ch. 1)

## History
Chemical pattern recognition in the 1960s and 1970s, once laboratory computers could apply multivariate methods, is the practical start. Jurs and colleagues in 1969 used computerised learning machines on combined infrared and mass spectra. Svante Wold used the Swedish word *kemometri* in a 1971 grant proposal. In 1974 Wold and Bruce Kowalski founded the Chemometrics Society, later the International Chemometrics Society, and the definition quoted above is the one that society used. *Analytical Chemistry* and *Analytica Chimica Acta* were carrying the word by the mid-1970s. The *Journal of Chemometrics* and *Chemometrics and Intelligent Laboratory Systems* followed in the mid-1980s, after an early suspicion that elaborate analysis was only a patch for a poor experiment. Personal computers then made the same methods part of ordinary laboratory work.{{cite:sharma2023}} (Ch. 1, §1.1){{cite:otto2024}} (Ch. 1)

## Connections
[Engineering statistics](engineering-statistics.qmd) shares the designed experiment and the process measurement. [Geostatistics](geostatistics.qmd) shares the multivariate map when the chemical quantity is a concentration in space. [Biostatistics](biostatistics.qmd) shares regression and classification when the chemical measurement is a clinical assay. Partial least squares, the regression method most often paired with spectra, is one calibration tool inside this wider discipline.{{cite:otto2024}} (Ch. 1){{cite:sharma2023}} (Ch. 1, §1.1.1)
