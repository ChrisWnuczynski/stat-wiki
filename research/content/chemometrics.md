## Lead context

A spectrometer returns hundreds of intensities for each sample, and the chemical question is which of those intensities mark a concentration, a class, or a bad run. Chemometrics is the chemical discipline that uses mathematical and statistical methods to design the measurements and to extract chemical information from the data they produce. The data are multivariate, the channels are correlated, and the number of channels often exceeds the number of calibration samples. Those three facts are why the field has a toolkit of its own inside analytical chemistry, built from ordinary statistical ideas.

## Intuition

A chemist can look at one peak. A modern instrument returns a whole spectrum, a chromatogram, or a hyperspectral image, and the concentration of interest is a function of many channels at once. Looking at the channels one at a time misses the correlation among them and, when there are more channels than samples, cannot invert a full regression in the usual way. The useful move is to replace the spectrum by a few directions that still carry the chemical information, and to regress the concentration on those directions.

Otto's overview of the subject separates the jobs. Random measurements are summarised and tested by descriptive and inferential statistics, increasingly for quality control. Signals are smoothed, filtered, differentiated, or transformed, for example by a Fourier or Hadamard transform. Experiments are designed, and the design is read either by a model or by a sequential search such as a simplex. Patterns are found by projection and by clustering, and unknown samples are assigned to classes by a discriminant rule. Information theory is used to say how much an analytical procedure conveys. The list is a map of statistical tasks onto chemical measurements, not a new probability.{{cite:otto2024}} (pp. 11–12, §1.2)

Kumar makes the calibration step explicit. The analytical chemist first builds a model that relates the instrument response to the concentration, using samples whose concentrations are known. The chemist then applies that model to a new spectrum and predicts the concentration. Mean-centring, subtracting the mean spectrum of the calibration set from each spectrum, is the usual pre-processing step in spectroscopy, because a constant offset is not the chemical signal. The prediction is only as good as the match between the calibration samples and the new sample.{{cite:kumar2021}} (§2.1, §2.2)

## Definition

Otto records the definition still used in analytical chemistry. Chemometrics is the chemical discipline that uses mathematical and statistical methods, first, to design or select measurement procedures and experiments, and second, to provide as much chemical information as possible by analysing chemical data. The discipline originates in chemistry. The typical applications he names are quantitative structure–activity relationships and the evaluation of analytical measurements. The flood of numbers from modern instruments is the reason analytical chemists in particular developed the methods. The same methods are used on chemical measurements in other fields, but the home of the subject is the chemical measurement.{{cite:otto2024}} (p. 2)

Kumar quotes the same two-part definition, and he adds the historical placement. Svante Wold coined the term in 1971. The International Chemometrics Society was formed in the 1970s. The first international meeting was held in Cosenza in 1983. The society sponsored *Chemometrics and Intelligent Laboratory Systems* and the *Journal of Chemometrics*, which began in 1986 and 1987. Otto dates the word to 1972 and the society's foundation, with the first description of the discipline, to 1974, naming Wold and Bruce R. Kowalski. The two accounts agree on the definition and on Wold's priority for the word. They differ by a year on the coining and on the date of the society, which is a reminder to cite the formulation one is using.{{cite:otto2024}} (p. 1){{cite:kumar2021}} (§1)

The early statistical content is multivariate and built for prediction when the predictors are many and collinear. Multiple linear regression finds one direction that maximises the correlation between the spectrum and the concentration, and it fails in the usual spectral problem for two reasons Kumar states. There are more response channels than calibration samples, so the problem is underdetermined. The channels are collinear, so the matrix inversion is unstable. Principal component analysis replaces the spectrum by orthogonal directions that capture variance in the spectra. Principal component regression then regresses the concentration on the scores. That regression handles the collinearity and does not try to maximise the correlation with the concentration. Partial least squares, introduced in econometrics by Herman Wold and brought into analytical chemistry by Svante Wold, finds directions that both capture variance and correlate with the concentration. Kumar calls it the calibration tool analytical chemists reach for first.{{cite:kumar2021}} (§1)

## Statistical use

Calibration is the applied form chemists meet most often: predict a concentration from a spectrum. The workflow Kumar describes has two steps that should be kept apart. The calibration step fits the relation on samples with known concentrations. The validation step applies the fitted relation to spectra that were not used in the fit, and compares predicted and actual concentrations. A model that is judged only on the calibration samples has not been validated. Mean-centring is part of the calibration arithmetic in spectroscopy, not an optional plot.{{cite:kumar2021}} (§2.1, §2.2)

Classification is the form that assigns a sample to a material, a source, or a grade. Otto lists projection methods, principal component analysis and factor analysis, and cluster analysis for finding patterns, and discriminant analysis for building a class model that can be applied to a new object. A spectrum that is reduced to a few scores can be plotted, clustered, or passed to a discriminant rule. The chemical question determines which of those is the target. A concentration is a regression. A material identity is a classification.{{cite:otto2024}} (p. 12, §1.2)

Experimental design chooses which samples and which instrument settings to run. Otto contrasts simultaneous designs, read by a model or a graph, with sequential optimisation such as the simplex, which walks through the space of experimental variables toward an objective such as the yield of a reaction. Signal processing sits in front of both. A derivative, a smooth, or a Fourier transform changes the spectrum before any regression is fit. Doing that processing badly, or choosing the number of components in a partial-least-squares model on the same data that will be used to claim accuracy, is a statistical error of the ordinary kind: the procedure and the assessment are not separated.{{cite:otto2024}} (p. 11, §1.2)

The same toolkit shows up wherever the measurement is chemical and multivariate. Kumar lists process control, food analysis, forensic science, metabolomics, clinical diagnosis, environmental monitoring, reaction monitoring, and synthesis optimisation. A forensic spectrum and a clinical assay differ in the science. They share the statistical shape: many correlated channels, a smaller number of samples with known labels or concentrations, and a prediction for a new sample. [Least squares](least-squares.qmd), [principal component](principal-component-directions.qmd) directions, and [designed experiments](designed-experiment.qmd) are the general methods those applications specialise.{{cite:kumar2021}} (§1){{cite:otto2024}} (p. 2)

## Assumptions and limits

Partial least squares and principal component regression assume that a low-rank structure is enough: a few directions in the space of spectra carry the chemical information, and the rest is noise or offset that mean-centring and the component limit can set aside. If the concentration depends on a small sharp peak that those directions do not keep, the prediction fails for a chemical reason that a cross-validation score on similar samples may not reveal. Validation on new samples from the same distribution estimates the prediction error for that distribution. It does not estimate the error for a new instrument, a new matrix, or a concentration outside the calibration range.{{cite:kumar2021}} (§2.1)

Multiple linear regression's two failures, more channels than samples and collinear channels, are design conditions of the spectral problem. They are why the field moved to components and to partial least squares. They return if the number of components is allowed to grow until the fit interpolates the calibration noise. Otto's separation of experimental design from the subsequent analysis is the corresponding limit on the other side: a poor choice of samples is not repaired by a more elaborate decomposition of the spectra those samples produced.{{cite:kumar2021}} (§1){{cite:otto2024}} (pp. 11–12, §1.2)

The education of chemists in mathematics and statistics is, in Otto's judgement, usually not enough for the methods the instruments now require. One aim of the subject was to make the methods practicable, and commercial software has done part of that. Software that fits a partial-least-squares model does not choose the validation set or the pre-processing. Those choices remain part of the analysis.{{cite:otto2024}} (p. 2)

## History

Some analytical groups in the 1970s were already applying statistical and mathematical methods, on mainframe computers, that would now be called chemometric. Otto dates the word to Wold and Kowalski in 1972 and the International Chemometrics Society to 1974. Kumar dates the word to Wold in 1971 and places the society in the 1970s, with the Cosenza meeting in 1983 and the two specialist journals in 1986 and 1987. Conference series followed the society: Computer Application in Analytics, Computer-Based Analytical Chemistry, and Chemometrics in Analytical Chemistry. *Analytical Chemistry* and *Analytica Chimica Acta* carried the work before the specialist journals existed.{{cite:otto2024}} (p. 1){{cite:kumar2021}} (§1)

Personal computers, from the beginning of the 1980s, changed who could run the methods. Otto describes that change as a new period for acquiring, processing, and interpreting chemical data. The combination of the multivariate methods with a computer on the bench is also Kumar's explanation of why the techniques spread among analytical chemists: the volume of data from a single instrument, or from instruments used in combination, became too large to inspect peak by peak. Herman Wold's partial least squares, brought into chemistry by Svante Wold, became the calibration method most often paired with spectra. The definition quoted above, design the measurement and extract the chemical information, is the one both sources treat as the description of the discipline that those developments produced.{{cite:otto2024}} (pp. 1–2){{cite:kumar2021}} (§1)
