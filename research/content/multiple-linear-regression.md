## Motivating example
House prices may vary with floor area, location and age at the same time. A simple plot of price against area mixes those associations because larger houses may also occupy different locations. Multiple linear regression describes a conditional mean using several predictors together. Each coefficient refers to a specified comparison that holds the other design columns fixed in the model; without a causal design, that comparison does not establish the effect of an intervention.{{cite:openintro2019}} (§9.1, “Introduction to multiple regression”)

A predictor need not be a measured continuous quantity. Indicator variables represent categories, and a product of columns represents an interaction. The model is called linear because its mean is linear in the coefficients, not because every relationship must be a straight line in an untransformed raw measurement.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 364–365)

## Definition — One response and a fixed design matrix
For $n$ responses and a full-column-rank $n\times p$ design matrix $X$, write $Y=X\beta+\varepsilon$ with $\E(\varepsilon\mid X)=0$. The vector $\beta$ contains $p$ unknown coefficients, including an intercept when $X$ has a column of ones. [Ordinary least squares](ordinary-least-squares.qmd) minimizes $\lVert Y-Xb\rVert^2$. Differentiating gives the normal equations $X^\top X\widehat\beta=X^\top Y$, so
$$\widehat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top Y.$$
Full column rank makes $\widehat\beta$ unique, and $X\widehat\beta$ is the orthogonal projection of $Y$ onto the column space of $X$.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 365)

Each assumption plays a distinct role: $\E(\varepsilon\mid X)=0$ identifies the conditional mean $X\beta$; uncorrelated errors with constant conditional variance $\sigma^2$ give $\operatorname{Cov}(\widehat\beta\mid X)=\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$; and independent Gaussian errors supply exact finite-sample normal, $t$, and $F$ sampling laws, whereas moment assumptions alone do not guarantee an exact $t$ reference distribution. Numerical least-squares fitting requires none of those distributional assumptions.{{cite:openintro2019}} (§9.3, “Checking model conditions using graphs”){{cite:sas2012_glm}} (Ch. 4, pp. 78, 87, “Regression with the REG and GLM Procedures” and “Estimating the Precision”)

## Properties — What the other predictors remove
The normal equations imply $X^\top e=0$ for residuals $e=Y-X\widehat\beta$; when $X$ includes a column of ones, the residual sum is zero. That orthogonality is enforced by the fitting algebra rather than by the population error process, so even a misspecified mean model satisfies $X^\top e=0$ identically.{{cite:sas2012_glm}} (Ch. 4, p. 86, “Parameter Estimates and Associated Statistics”)

To interpret the coefficient on one predictor $x$, write $X=[Z,x]$ and remove the linear contribution of $Z$ from both $x$ and $Y$ via $M_Z=I-Z(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top$. Minimizing over the coefficients of $Z$ reduces the objective in the remaining slope $b$ to $\lVert M_ZY-bM_Zx\rVert^2$, yielding $\widehat b=x^\top M_ZY/(x^\top M_Zx)$ when $M_Zx\neq 0$: the partial slope is the simple slope of residualized $Y$ on residualized $x$.{{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§5.1.4, normal equations and squared-error fitting)

## Worked example — Two orthogonal comparisons
Use four observations with design rows $(1,0,0)$, $(1,1,0)$, $(1,0,1)$ and $(1,1,1)$, and responses $1,3,4,7$. The second and third columns are binary predictors. Solving the normal equations via $\widehat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top Y$ gives intercept $0.75$ and slopes $2.5$ and $3.5$. Fitted responses are $0.75,3.25,4.25,6.75$ and residuals are $0.25,-0.25,-0.25,0.25$, whose squared sum is $0.25$; all three design columns are orthogonal to the residual vector.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 365)

There are $n-p=1$ residual degrees of freedom, so the unbiased residual variance estimate is $s^2=0.25$. Here the inverse cross-product matrix is
$$ (X^\top X)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}3/4&-1/2&-1/2\\-1/2&1&0\\-1/2&0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$
Thus both slope standard errors are $0.5$ under homoscedastic uncorrelated errors. Four responses provide very little residual information; a numerically precise fit is different from precise inference about a wider population.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 365)

**Your turn.** Add two to all four responses without changing $X$. What happens to the coefficients and residual sum of squares?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The intercept rises from $0.75$ to $2.75$. Both slopes stay at $2.5$ and $3.5$. Every fitted response rises by two, so the residuals and their squared sum $0.25$ stay the same.
:::

## Common misconceptions
An adjusted coefficient is not “the effect of changing one predictor with everything else held fixed” in any causal sense. In the four-observation worked example, the first binary slope $2.5$ averages the two within-stratum differences $3-1=2$ (at $x_2=0$) and $7-4=3$ (at $x_2=1$). That calculation compares units that differ in $x_1$ and match in $x_2$ within the additive model; in observational data, any unmeasured confounder that still varies with $x_1$ inside levels of $x_2$ biases a causal reading, and even in the four-point table the two stratum differences ($2$ and $3$) differ by $1$ because no interaction column $x_1x_2$ was included.{{cite:sas2012_glm}} (Ch. 4, p. 98, “Interpreting Parameter Estimates from an Observational Study”){{cite:openintro2019}} (§9.1, “Introduction to multiple regression”)

Appending a collinear predictor destroys uniqueness of the full coefficient vector, yet it aliases only specific coordinates rather than every parameter. If a predictor column is duplicated—for example, appending a copy of $x_2$ to the four-observation design to form rows $(1,x_{i1},x_{i2},x_{i2})$ with parameters $(\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2,\beta_3)^\top$—every vector $(0.75,2.5,3.5-c,c)^\top$ satisfies the normal equations: the *individual slopes* $\beta_2$ and $\beta_3$ on the duplicated column are aliased, whereas the intercept $\beta_0$, the orthogonal slope $\beta_1$, and the slope sum $\beta_2+\beta_3$ lie in the row space of $X$ and remain uniquely estimable. Likewise, for five observations at $x=(0,1,2,3,4)$ with a duplicated predictor $z=x$, design rows $(1,x_i,x_i)$ of rank $2$, and responses $y=(0,0,1,3,2)$, the individual slopes $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ are aliased while the intercept $\widehat\beta_0=-0.2$ and slope sum $\widehat{\beta_1+\beta_2}=0.7$ are uniquely determined on $5-2=3$ residual degrees of freedom, with $\operatorname{SSE}=1.9$, $s^2=19/30$, standard error $\sqrt{19/300}\approx0.25166$, and ratio $t=0.7/\sqrt{19/300}\approx2.78152$ against $t_{3,0.975}\approx3.18245$ under independent Gaussian errors; moment assumptions alone yield unbiasedness and variance for $\widehat{\beta_1+\beta_2}$ without guaranteeing an exact $t_3$ reference distribution.{{cite:sas2012_glm}} (Ch. 4, pp. 87, 89–92, “Estimating the Precision”, “Models Not of Full Rank”, and “Testing Linear Hypotheses”; Ch. 15, pp. 251–252, 256–257, “Estimability” and “A Multiple Regression Model”)

## Assumptions and limits — Adjustment can be fragile
If a design column is a linear combination of the others, $X^\top X$ is singular and the individual coefficients involved in that dependence cannot be identified separately, even though the orthogonal projection $X\widehat\beta$ onto the column space remains unique. Nearly dependent columns create a companion precision problem: small perturbations in $Y$ can swing individual partial slopes sharply while leaving fitted values stable.{{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§5.1.4, discussion of invertibility){{cite:sas2012_glm}} (Ch. 4, pp. 89–90, 99, “Models Not of Full Rank” and “Correlated Regressors”)

Diagnostics should examine residual patterns, high-leverage cases, and whether the additive mean form is plausible; independence is a property of the sampling design and cannot be verified from a residual plot alone. Extrapolating beyond observed predictor combinations requires substantive justification, especially because correlated predictors leave corners of the input space unsampled. When an interaction column is included, a main-effect coefficient compares units only at the zero baseline of the interacting variable rather than uniformly across all levels.{{cite:openintro2019}} (§§9.1, 9.3)

## Connections
In the four-observation worked example, the two binary predictors have zero centred inner product, making the $(2,3)$ entry of $(X^\top X)^{-1}$ zero so that each partial slope ($2.5$ and $3.5$) equals its [simple linear regression](simple-linear-regression.qmd) slope; when predictors are correlated instead, [multicollinearity](multicollinearity.qmd) inflates the diagonal variance factors and [ridge regression](ridge-regression.qmd) trades bias for stability. [Ordinary least squares](ordinary-least-squares.qmd) supplies the projection algebra within [regression analysis](regression-analysis.qmd), the [normal linear model](normal-linear-model.qmd) and [Gaussian linear model](gaussian-linear-model.qmd) supply exact finite-sample $t$ and $F$ laws together with the [ANOVA sum-of-squares decomposition](anova-sum-of-squares-decomposition.qmd) for nested subspaces, and a [generalised linear model](generalised-linear-model.qmd) keeps the linear predictor $X\beta$ while replacing the identity link and Gaussian error family.{{cite:sas2012_glm}} (Ch. 4, pp. 79, 91–92, 99, “Generalized Linear Regression”, “Testing Linear Hypotheses”, and “Correlated Regressors”)
