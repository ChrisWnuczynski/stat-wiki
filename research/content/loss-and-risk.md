## Motivating example
A weather service must choose a precautionary action before tomorrow's temperature is known. A small underestimate may be much more costly than a small overestimate. Describing an estimator as accurate is therefore incomplete until the consequences of its errors have been specified. A loss evaluates one realized decision; risk averages that loss over possible samples generated by a fixed parameter.{{cite:downey2014_thinkstats2}} (§8.1, The estimation game)

## Intuition — Three levels of averaging
An action $a$ can be a number, a class prediction, or another decision. A loss $L(\theta,a)$ assigns its cost at parameter $\theta$; zero loss at the truth is common for estimation but is not required for every decision problem. For a rule $T(X)$, frequentist risk is $R(\theta,T)=E_\theta L(\theta,T(X))$. It is a function of $\theta$, because the distribution of future data changes with the parameter. By contrast, empirical loss averages errors over observed cases, and posterior expected loss averages over a posterior parameter distribution after observing data. These averages answer different questions.{{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§12.5, Objective functions){{cite:siegrist2022_bayes_estimators}} (Basic Theory, Real Parameters)

## Properties — Squared error and a bias–variance tradeoff
For finite second moments,
$$E_\theta(T-\theta)^2=\operatorname{Var}_\theta(T)+\{E_\theta T-\theta\}^2.$$
To prove it, write $T-\theta=(T-E_\theta T)+(E_\theta T-\theta)$, square, and average. The cross term vanishes. An unbiased rule minimizes only the bias term; a biased rule can have lower total risk if it reduces variability enough.{{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§12.6, Variance–bias trade-off)

If $X\sim N(\theta,1)$, the rule $T_a=aX$ has risk $a^2+(a-1)^2\theta^2$. The usual rule $X$ has risk one everywhere. The shrinkage rule $X/2$ has risk $(1+\theta^2)/4$, beating $X$ for $|\theta|<\sqrt3$ and losing for $|\theta|>\sqrt3$. A constant zero rule has risk $\theta^2$. These crossing curves explain why a decision criterion, a restricted class or a prior is needed to turn risk comparison into one choice.{{cite:downey2014_thinkstats2}} (§8.1, comparing estimators by MSE)

For a posterior with finite mean $m$, completing the square gives $E\{(\theta-a)^2\mid X\}=\operatorname{Var}(\theta\mid X)+(a-m)^2$, so the posterior mean minimizes squared loss. Under absolute loss, a posterior median minimizes expected loss; its one-sided slopes depend on the probabilities below and above $a$. Under zero–one loss on a discrete parameter space, a posterior mode minimizes the posterior error probability. A continuous parameter with exact-match zero–one loss has probability zero at each single value, so this last rule requires a different loss or a discrete target.{{cite:siegrist2022_bayes_estimators}} (Basic Theory, Real Parameters)

## Worked example — One distribution, two decisions
Suppose posterior probabilities for $\theta=0,1,4$ are $0.2,0.5,0.3$. The posterior mean is $1.7$ and the unique median is $1$. Expected squared loss at $1.7$ is $2.41$, versus $2.9$ at $1$. Expected absolute loss at $1$ is $1.1$, versus $1.38$ at $1.7$. Each action wins under the criterion that defined it. This arithmetic makes clear why replacing one loss by another can change the answer even when beliefs about the parameter do not change.{{cite:siegrist2022_bayes_estimators}} (Basic Theory, Real Parameters)

**Your turn.** For $X\sim N(\theta,1)$ compare the squared risks of $X$ and $X/2$ at $\theta=0$ and $\theta=2$.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
At zero, the risks are $1$ and $1/4$. At two, they are $1$ and $5/4$. The shrinkage rule helps near its shrinkage target and hurts sufficiently far away.
:::

## Assumptions and limits
A no-uniform-winner argument needs conditions on the statistical experiment. Suppose squared loss has at least two parameter values whose sampling laws are mutually absolutely continuous. A rule beating every constant rule would need zero risk at every parameter, because the constant equal to that parameter has zero risk there. Zero risk forces the rule to equal each distinct parameter almost surely. Mutual absolute continuity makes those requirements incompatible. However, an experiment that reveals $\theta$ exactly can have a rule with zero risk for all parameters. The sampling model is therefore essential to the conclusion. Risk may also be infinite under heavy tails or sufficiently steep losses.{{cite:siegrist2022_bayes_estimators}} (Basic Theory, Real Parameters){{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§12.5)
