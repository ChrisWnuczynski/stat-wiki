## Motivating example
A weather service must choose a precautionary action before tomorrow's temperature is known. A small underestimate may be much more costly than a small overestimate. Describing an estimator as accurate is therefore incomplete until the consequences of its errors have been specified. A loss evaluates one realized decision; risk averages that loss over possible samples generated by a fixed parameter.{{cite:downey2014_thinkstats2}} (§8.1, The estimation game)

## Intuition — Loss scores a decision, risk scores a rule
An action $a$ can be a number, a class prediction, or another decision. A loss $L(\theta,a)$ assigns its cost at parameter $\theta$; zero loss at the truth is common for estimation but is not required for every decision problem. For a rule $T(X)$, frequentist risk is $R(\theta,T)=E_\theta L(\theta,T(X))$. It is a function of $\theta$, because the distribution of future data changes with the parameter. Empirical loss averages errors over the cases already observed, and posterior expected loss averages over a posterior distribution of the parameter after the data arrive. The three averages answer different questions, and a comparison is interpretable only once the average has been named.{{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§12.5, Objective functions){{cite:lehmann_casella1998}} (Ch. 1, §1.1, pp. 5–6)

## Properties — Squared error and a bias–variance tradeoff
For finite second moments,
$$E_\theta(T-\theta)^2=\operatorname{Var}_\theta(T)+\{E_\theta T-\theta\}^2.$$
To prove it, write $T-\theta=(T-E_\theta T)+(E_\theta T-\theta)$, square, and average. The cross term vanishes. An unbiased rule minimizes only the bias term; a biased rule can have lower total risk if it reduces variability enough.{{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§12.6, Variance–bias trade-off)

If $X\sim N(\theta,1)$, the rule $T_a=aX$ has risk $a^2+(a-1)^2\theta^2$. The usual rule $X$ has risk one everywhere. The shrinkage rule $X/2$ has risk $(1+\theta^2)/4$, beating $X$ for $|\theta|<\sqrt3$ and losing for $|\theta|>\sqrt3$. A constant zero rule has risk $\theta^2$. These crossing curves explain why a decision criterion, a restricted class or a prior is needed to turn risk comparison into one choice.{{cite:downey2014_thinkstats2}} (§8.1, comparing estimators by MSE){{cite:lehmann_casella1998}} (Ch. 1, §1.1, p. 5)

For a posterior with finite mean $m$, completing the square gives $E\{(\theta-a)^2\mid X\}=\operatorname{Var}(\theta\mid X)+(a-m)^2$, so the posterior mean minimizes squared loss. Under absolute loss, a posterior median minimizes expected loss; its one-sided slopes depend on the probabilities below and above $a$. Under zero–one loss on a discrete parameter space, a posterior mode minimizes the posterior probability of an exact miss. A continuous parameter with exact-match zero–one loss has probability zero at each single value, so that last comparison needs a discrete target, a tolerance neighbourhood, or a different loss.{{cite:lehmann_casella1998}} (Ch. 3, Example 1.26, pp. 157–158; Ch. 4, §4.3, pp. 240–241)

## Worked example — One distribution, two decisions
Suppose posterior probabilities for $\theta=0,1,4$ are $0.2,0.5,0.3$. The posterior mean is $1.7$ and the unique median is $1$. Expected squared loss at $1.7$ is $2.41$, versus $2.9$ at $1$. Expected absolute loss at $1$ is $1.1$, versus $1.38$ at $1.7$. Each action wins under the criterion that defined it. The arithmetic shows why replacing one loss by another can change the reported action even when the posterior distribution stays fixed.{{cite:lehmann_casella1998}} (Ch. 4, §4.3, pp. 240–241)

**Your turn.** For $X\sim N(\theta,1)$ compare the squared risks of $X$ and $X/2$ at $\theta=0$ and $\theta=2$.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
At zero, the risks are $1$ and $1/4$. At two, they are $1$ and $5/4$. The shrinkage rule helps near its shrinkage target and hurts sufficiently far away.
:::

## Statistical use — Pick the average that matches the decision
A forecast issued before the temperature is observed is scored by a loss. One afternoon's error of $2$ degrees has squared loss $4$ and absolute loss $2$. The risk of the forecasting rule is the expectation of that loss over the distribution of afternoons at a fixed parameter, so one error of $2$ is a single draw from that distribution and does not reveal the risk. An unbiased rule with variance $0.5$ has squared-error risk $0.5$. A rule with variance $0.2$ and bias $0.4$ has squared-error risk $0.2+0.4^2=0.36$. Squared-error risk adds the variance to the square of the bias, and at this parameter the second rule has the smaller risk.{{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§12.6, Variance–bias trade-off){{cite:downey2014_thinkstats2}} (§8.1, comparing estimators by MSE)

## Common misconceptions
Empirical loss on the observed sample, frequentist risk, and posterior expected loss answer three different averages. The first plugs in the cases at hand. The second averages future samples at a fixed $\theta$. The third averages $\theta$ under a posterior. A small empirical loss can belong to a rule with large risk at the $\theta$ that generated the sample, because the rule was free to adapt to those cases. A small posterior expected loss can belong to a rule with large frequentist risk at a $\theta$ the posterior treats as unlikely. Naming all three "the error" hides which average was taken.{{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§12.5, Objective functions)

## Assumptions and limits
A no-uniform-winner argument needs conditions on the statistical experiment. Suppose squared loss has at least two parameter values whose sampling laws are mutually absolutely continuous. A rule beating every constant rule would need zero risk at every parameter, because the constant equal to that parameter has zero risk there. Zero risk forces the rule to equal each distinct parameter almost surely. Mutual absolute continuity makes those requirements incompatible. However, an experiment that reveals $\theta$ exactly can have a rule with zero risk for all parameters. The sampling model is therefore essential to the conclusion. Risk may also be infinite under heavy tails or sufficiently steep losses. A comparison of risks at a single $\theta$ is a comparison at that $\theta$. Integrating the risk against a prior produces a Bayes risk and can prefer a rule that loses on a set the prior treats as small. That integral is a further choice of criterion.{{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§12.5){{cite:lehmann_casella1998}} (Ch. 1, §1.1, pp. 5–6)
