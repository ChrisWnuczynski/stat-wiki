## Lead context
Deviance measures the loss of fit when a statistical model is constrained to use fewer mean parameters than a model that reproduces the observed responses.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (p. 544, GLM fit and comparison){{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Sec. Value, dev.resids)

## Motivating example
Suppose two Poisson models assign mean $2$ to one count, but the observed count is $7$. The raw residual is $5$ under either model. Its meaning changes if a second observation has a fitted mean of $50$ and an observed count of $55$: a five-count miss is much less surprising at that scale. Deviance uses the fitted family likelihood to express discrepancy on a scale adapted to the model's variation. A saturated model supplies the reference likelihood by fitting each observation as closely as the family allows.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (p. 544, family and fit as distinct components){{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Sec. Value, dev.resids and variance)

## Intuition
The saturated fit is deliberately flexible: it assigns one mean to each observation and, for the usual GLM families, reproduces the observations exactly or as a boundary limit. It is a benchmark, not a useful predictive model. The fitted model gives up that perfect training fit in order to express a common pattern through relatively few coefficients. The drop in maximized log likelihood measures the cost of that restriction. Multiplying by two puts the difference on the conventional likelihood-ratio scale. A deviance of zero means that the fitted model attains the saturated likelihood; it does not establish that its assumptions or future predictions are sound.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (pp. 543–544, GLM formulation and fitting)

## Definition
For observations $y$ in one response family, write $\ell(\tilde\mu;y)$ for the saturated log likelihood and $\ell(\hat\mu;y)$ for the fitted log likelihood. With dispersion fixed at one, $D=2\{\ell(\tilde\mu;y)-\ell(\hat\mu;y)\}\geq0$. For a family with dispersion $\phi$, the *unscaled* deviance $D$ is conventionally defined so that the log-likelihood difference is $D/(2\phi)$ when that form applies; $D/\phi$ is the scaled deviance. The inherited formulas below display the normal, Poisson, binomial and Gamma cases and the cholesterol example. R's family object supplies a `dev.resids` function for each observation, and `glm` reports residual and null deviances.{{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Sec. Value, dev.resids; Usage, family definitions){{cite:rstats_glm2026}} (Sec. Value, deviance and null.deviance)

For a Poisson count $y=0$, use $0\log(0/\mu)=0$ by continuity. For a binomial count, the analogous $0\log0$ terms are also zero. Saturation at a boundary, such as a Bernoulli observation of zero or one, is understood as a limit. These conventions make the formulas well defined without pretending that the saturated fit can be estimated as a smooth finite-parameter regression in every case.{{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Sec. Value, dev.resids and validmu)

## Properties
Deviance is nonnegative because the saturated model has at least as high a maximized likelihood as a constrained fit within the same family. For nested models fitted to the *same data and likelihood*, the reduction $D_{\rm small}-D_{\rm large}=2(\ell_{\rm large}-\ell_{\rm small})$ when dispersion is one. The saturated reference cancels, so this difference is a likelihood-ratio statistic for the nested comparison. A lower residual deviance means a closer in-sample fit within the family, but adding predictors can lower it even when those predictors do not generalize.{{cite:rstats_glm2026}} (Sec. Value, deviance and null.deviance){{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (p. 544, model fit)

The inherited normal formula reduces to the residual sum of squares before division by $\sigma^2$. The Poisson and binomial contributions use their family likelihoods, while the Gamma contribution uses a positive response and variance proportional to the squared mean. They are not interchangeable discrepancy formulas. In particular, an unscaled Poisson deviance cannot be subtracted from a Gamma deviance to obtain an ordinary likelihood-ratio test. Nor does a small deviance on its own establish that a variance model is adequate.{{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Sec. Usage; Arguments, variance; Value, dev.resids)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
For a Poisson count, $\ell(\mu;y)=y\log\mu-\mu-\log(y!)$. The saturated mean is $\tilde\mu=y$ for $y>0$ and the boundary limit $\tilde\mu=0$ for $y=0$. Subtraction gives $2\{y\log(y/\hat\mu)-(y-\hat\mu)\}$, with $y\log(y/\hat\mu)=0$ when $y=0$. For a binomial count $y$ out of $m$, subtract the log likelihood at $p=y/m$ from that at $\hat p$: the combinatorial constant cancels, leaving $2\{y\log[y/(m\hat p)]+(m-y)\log[(m-y)/(m(1-\hat p))]\}$. This proves the inherited count formulas observation by observation.{{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Sec. Value, dev.resids; Usage, Poisson and binomial families)

For a normal observation with known variance $\sigma^2$, $\ell(\mu;y)$ differs from $-(y-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)$ by a term independent of $\mu$. The saturated choice $\mu=y$ gives $2(\ell_{\rm sat}-\ell_{\rm fit})=(y-\hat\mu)^2/\sigma^2$, hence unscaled $D=(y-\hat\mu)^2$. For a Gamma observation with shape $1/\phi$ and scale $\phi\mu$, the terms involving $\mu$ are $-(1/\phi)\{\log\mu+y/\mu\}$. Subtracting at $\tilde\mu=y$ gives $2(\ell_{\rm sat}-\ell_{\rm fit})=(2/\phi)\{-\log(y/\hat\mu)+(y-\hat\mu)/\hat\mu\}$, the inherited Gamma formula after multiplying by $\phi$.{{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Sec. Usage; Arguments, Gaussian and Gamma families; Value, variance and dev.resids)

Finally, if model $M_0$ is contained in $M_1$, $D_0-D_1=2\{(\ell_{\rm sat}-\ell_0)-(\ell_{\rm sat}-\ell_1)\}=2(\ell_1-\ell_0)$. The cancellation proves the exact identity for a common fixed dispersion; a chi-squared reference distribution, when appropriate, needs additional large-sample conditions and is not implied by this algebra.{{cite:rstats_glm2026}} (Sec. Value, deviance and null.deviance)
:::
:::

## Statistical use
The null deviance compares an intercept-only fit with the saturated fit. The residual deviance compares the chosen predictor model with the same saturated fit. Their difference therefore asks whether the predictors improve on the intercept-only model, subject to the usual nested-model conditions. A separate goodness-of-fit reading asks whether the residual deviance is plausible under the fitted response family; it depends on sample size, degrees of freedom, discreteness and dispersion. These are different questions. The answered model-adequacy check below makes the distinction with a null deviance of $410$ and a residual deviance of $310$.{{cite:rstats_glm2026}} (Sec. Value, null.deviance and deviance){{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Sec. Details, quasipoisson dispersion)

When comparing models using different links but the same binomial likelihood and observations, each has a residual deviance relative to the same saturated fit. The smaller deviance has the larger in-sample likelihood. The usual chi-squared test based on their difference requires nesting and cannot be applied directly to logit and probit fits, which are not nested. AIC uses the likelihood with a penalty for model size; the inherited cholesterol analysis reports it alongside deviance for three links. Close AIC values should be read as limited discrimination, not as proof that the curves are identical.{{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Sec. Value, aic and dev.resids){{cite:rstats_glm2026}} (Sec. Value, deviance)

## Worked example
Consider three independent Poisson counts $y=(0,3,5)$ and proposed fitted means $\hat\mu=(1,2,4)$. The first observation contributes $2\{0-(0-1)\}=2.000000$ to deviance. The second contributes $2\{3\log(3/2)-1\}=0.432791$, and the third contributes $2\{5\log(5/4)-1\}=0.231436$. Their sum is $D=2.664226$. The zero count is not omitted; its contribution expresses the gap between a mean of one and a zero observation. This number describes discrepancy relative to a saturated Poisson fit. Without specifying how the means were estimated and what model is being checked, it is not a p-value or a verdict on adequacy.{{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Sec. Usage, Poisson; Value, dev.resids)

**Your turn.** For two independent Poisson counts $y=(2,0)$ with fitted means $\hat\mu=(1,1)$, compute each deviance contribution and their total. Use the continuous convention for $0\log0$.{{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Sec. Value, dev.resids)

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The first contribution is $2\{2\log2-(2-1)\}=0.772589$. The second is $2\{0-(0-1)\}=2.000000$. Hence $D=2.772589$. The equal raw residual magnitudes of one have unequal deviance contributions because an upward count of two and a zero count have different likelihood ratios relative to the same Poisson mean of one.{{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Sec. Usage, Poisson; Value, dev.resids)
:::

## Common misconceptions
A significant reduction from null to residual deviance does not imply that the final model fits adequately; both models can fit poorly, as the answered question below shows. Deviance is not a raw sum of residuals and is not a universal metric independent of the family: its contributions depend on the assumed likelihood. A smaller deviance after adding predictors is not evidence of a scientifically useful improvement without considering model complexity and out-of-sample behavior. AIC is not always literally $D+2k$ across different response families, because constants and dispersion conventions matter.{{cite:rstats_glm2026}} (Sec. Value, deviance; AIC details){{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Sec. Value, aic and dev.resids)

## Assumptions and limits
The likelihood comparison requires the fitted and saturated models to use the same observations and response family. If a Poisson model has mean near $2$ but conditional variance near $10$, the Poisson deviance is still computable but a naive chi-squared goodness-of-fit reference may be badly calibrated. A binomial model with many tiny group sizes can likewise make a simple residual-deviance chi-squared approximation unreliable. For nonnested links, the ordinary nested chi-squared difference test is unavailable even though both deviances can be computed. A very flexible model can approach zero training deviance and still predict poorly on new data. Missing or censored observations also change the likelihood and saturated reference; inserting their raw values into the complete-data formulas would be wrong.{{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Sec. Details, quasi families; Value, dev.resids){{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (p. 544, family and fit)

## History
Nelder and Wedderburn's 1972 generalized linear models unified fitting for exponential-family responses. McCullagh's later introduction to their paper places that unification in the development of likelihood-based model fitting. The modern R family interface retains family-specific deviance residual functions and reports null and residual deviance for GLM fits. The historical connection is to likelihood comparison within a specified response family, not to a claim that one deviance scale works unchanged for every distribution.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (pp. 543–544){{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Sec. Value, dev.resids){{cite:rstats_glm2026}} (Sec. Value, deviance and null.deviance)
