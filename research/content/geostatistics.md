## Lead context

Ore grade, rainfall, and a soil pollutant are not sprinkled independently across a map. Nearby sites tend to be alike, and a histogram of the samples throws the map away. Geostatistics is the statistical treatment of measurements that come with coordinates, used to describe how a quantity is arranged in space and to estimate the quantities a decision needs, together with the error of those estimates. {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 229) {{cite:matheron1963}} (pp. 1246–1247, Introduction)

## Definition

In the mining problems that gave the field its modern mathematical form, geostatistics is concerned with the distribution in space of values an engineer or a geologist can use: grade, thickness, or accumulation, including the error of an evaluation of a deposit. Knowledge of grades and reserves, and of the error in those figures, is what a decision to mine has to rest on. The same idea applies to any regionalised measurement. A working description used outside mining is that the data are spatial, the coordinates are known, and the analysis can be hierarchical: simple models are built locally, and those local pieces are assembled into a joint model that is still analysable. {{cite:matheron1963}} (p. 1246, Introduction) {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 229)

A classical sample mean and a histogram can be computed from the grades, and they answer how often a grade occurs. They do not answer where the high grades sit, or how large a payable body is, or where that body is. Matheron’s statement of the gap is direct. Classifying grades on a histogram does not take the location of the samples into account. It is not enough to know the frequency of a given grade. It is also necessary to know how the grades follow one another on the ground, and in particular the size and the position of the economic orebodies. The spatial arrangement is the feature the reserve calculation needs, and it is the feature ordinary independent-sample summaries discard. {{cite:matheron1963}} (p. 1247, “Insufficiency of classical statistical concepts”)

The language that grew up around the subject still says “variance” and “covariance,” but those words were given a spatial content. The similarity is easy to over-read. At the end of the development the theory was facing natural phenomena distributed in space rather than a sequence of independent random draws, and its methods were closer to those of mathematical physics, and in particular of harmonic analysis, than to a list of independent replicates. {{cite:matheron1963}} (p. 1247)

## Statistical use

The practical targets are forecasts and reserve figures that respect coordinates. Dodge’s list of domains is wider than a single mine: precipitation, the porosity of the ground, concentrations of heavy metals, and the treatment of satellite images, because a pixel is a measurement with a location. In each case the analysis is asked both for a level and for a map. A global mean grade, or a proportion of samples above a cutoff, still describes the composition of the material. It becomes the wrong answer when it is asked where the cutoff grade occurs. A reserve calculation needs the amount and the position. {{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 229–230) {{cite:matheron1963}} (p. 1247)

The same geometry changes every procedure that assumes independent replicates. A confidence interval for a regional mean that uses the ordinary sample variance treats widely scattered boreholes and a tight cluster as equivalent. They are not equivalent looks at the region. A regression of grade on depth that ignores location can attribute to depth a pattern that is really a patch of rich ore. The South African practice associated with Krige and Sichel is the historical illustration of the drift: the calculations were described as classical statistics, and they diverged from classical statistics as they were adjusted to the spatial object. {{cite:matheron1963}} (p. 1247)

Global summaries keep a role once their question is stated honestly. The histogram, the mean, and the proportion above a cutoff describe composition. Prediction at an unsampled place is a different question, and it uses the samples together with their coordinates rather than the samples as an unordered list. Hierarchical modelling, local pieces assembled into a joint model, is one way the Dodge entry describes keeping both the local fit and the global analysis. {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 229)

Soil contamination, rainfall, and image data use the same discipline when the variable is a concentration or a pixel value. The coordinate is part of the observation. A summary that discards it answers how the values are distributed as numbers. It does not answer how they are arranged. {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 230) {{cite:matheron1963}} (p. 1246)

## Common misconceptions

A large number of assays is not, by itself, a spatial analysis. If the assays are reduced to a histogram, the locations have already been thrown away, and no increase in the number of bars restores them. Matheron puts the point as an inability of common statistics, in their ordinary form, to take the spatial aspect into account. That aspect is the one the mining decision uses. {{cite:matheron1963}} (p. 1247)

The word “variance” inside a geostatistical calculation is easy to hear as the variance of an independent sample. The theory kept the word and changed the content: the relevant dispersion is the dispersion of a phenomenon spread over a region, not the dispersion of independent draws from one distribution. Treating a cluster of neighbouring boreholes as five independent replicates overstates how much of the deposit has been seen. {{cite:matheron1963}} (p. 1247)

Geostatistics is also wider than one interpolation recipe. The 1963 programme was an estimation theory for reserves, with an error attached to the estimate, built because classical probability calculus had been recognised as insufficient once the spatial characters of the distributions had to be put back in. Forecasting a concentration or a rainfall field is the same programme on a different map. The programme is the estimation problem, not a requirement that every spatial data set pass through one algorithm. {{cite:matheron1963}} (pp. 1246–1247) {{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 229–230)

A further confusion is historical. Mining practice that weighted samples by thickness and by an influence zone was already taking space into account, and Matheron treats those traditional methods as the starting point of the theory rather than as a rival to it. The modern formalism systematised that experience. It did not begin from a blank map in 1963. {{cite:matheron1963}} (p. 1246)

## Connections

A [time series](time-series.qmd) is the one-dimensional relative: dependence is organised by lag, and a spatial field organises dependence by separation in the plane or in space. A point pattern of events, such as disease cases or tree positions, is a different spatial object from a continuous grade measured at chosen sites. [Sampling](sampling.qmd) still matters, because where the sites were placed determines what the map can support, but the likelihood is not the likelihood of independent draws. Environmental records of a pollutant and official maps of rainfall sit in the same frame when the scientific question is about arrangement as well as about level. {{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 229–230) {{cite:matheron1963}} (p. 1247)

## History

Hart used the word in 1954 for statistical methods applied to observations spread over a region, in a geographical setting. The theory that mining engineers now call geostatistics was formulated by Georges Matheron in the early 1960s at the École des Mines in Paris, in order to estimate reserves from samples that carry coordinates. The 1963 paper in *Economic Geology* sets out the principles: traditional mining calculations that respected space, the later use of classical probability calculus for dispersion, and the further step of building a formalism once that calculus was seen to omit the spatial characters of the deposit. Krige and Sichel’s South African work is named there as the practice that had already bent classical formulas toward the orebody. {{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 229–230) {{cite:matheron1963}} (pp. 1246–1247)

Matheron dates an informal geostatistics to the moment miners computed mean grades from samples weighted by thickness and by zones of influence. The twentieth-century change was to state the error of a reserve estimate and to stop pretending that a histogram was a map. Later applied work, as Dodge records it, extended the same maps to soil contamination, natural-resource evaluation, and image data. The word stayed with the spatial problem, not with a single commodity. {{cite:matheron1963}} (p. 1246) {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 230)
