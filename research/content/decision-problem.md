## Lead context
An inference that stops at a posterior probability still leaves someone to act. A decision problem is that next question written in full: which actions are available, which outcomes they can lead to, and how those outcomes are scored. The probability model supplies the distribution of outcomes given an action. The decision problem supplies the list of actions and the score, a utility or a loss, that picks one of them.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 9, §9.1, pp. 237–238)

## Motivating example
A 95-year-old patient with a lung tumour can choose radiotherapy, surgery, or no treatment. There is a $90\%$ chance the tumour is malignant. Life expectancy is $16.7$ months with radiotherapy if it is cancer and $34.8$ months if it is not. Surgery carries a $35\%$ chance of immediate death and otherwise a longer expectancy. No treatment leaves $5.6$ months if the tumour is malignant. Treatment also costs about a month of comfort, a loss subtracted from the months of life. Scoring each action by expected quality-adjusted lifetime makes radiotherapy the best of the three, at about $17.5$ months against $13.5$ and $8.5$. Adding a bronchoscopy, which might change the probability of cancer before the treatment is chosen, is a second decision point in the same problem. The calculation shows that the test would not change the treatment, so the test is not worth its risk.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 9, §9.3, pp. 245–246)

## Definition
A decision problem, in the Bayesian account used for applied decision analysis, has four parts. Decision theory is the name of that account: actions, a distribution of outcomes given the action, a numerical score, and a rule that picks the action with the best expected score.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 9, §9.1, pp. 237–238)

1. A set of decisions $d$ and a set of outcomes $x$. Outcomes may be money, years of life, or a vector of several attributes. The outcome can include quantities that will eventually be observed and parameters that will not.
2. A probability distribution of the outcome given each decision, written $p(x\mid d)$. The decision itself is not assigned a probability. Every probability in the problem is conditional on the action under consideration.
3. A utility $U(x)$ that scores outcomes on one numerical scale, or a loss that scores them as a cost. With several attributes the score has to weigh them against each other, as quality-adjusted life years do when a month of treatment discomfort is subtracted. The loss function is that score written so that a worse outcome has a larger number.
4. The expected utility $E\bigl(U(x)\mid d\bigr)$, or the expected loss if the score is a cost, computed for each $d$. The preferred decision maximises expected utility or minimises expected loss. In a tree of several decisions the expectation at each node uses only the information available at that node.

A full analysis does all four steps. Many applications stop after the first two and leave the weighing of outcomes to the person who must act.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 9, §9.1, pp. 237–239)

Point estimation is a decision problem of a narrower kind. The action is the number reported for a parameter $\theta$, and a loss function $l(\hat\theta,\theta)$ scores the use of the estimate in place of the unknown value. When the loss is quadratic, $l(\hat\theta,\theta)=(\hat\theta-\theta)^2$, the point estimate is the mean of the distribution calculated for the parameter. Hypothesis testing is placed in the same family: a loss for accepting a hypothesis and a loss for rejecting it, with the action chosen by which integrated loss is smaller.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 34)

A household radon problem has the same four parts. It asks whether to remediate at once, do nothing, or measure and then remediate only if the measurement is high. The actions, the uncertain exposure, and the dollar cost of a reduction in risk are the decision problem. The statistical model that estimates exposure is an input to it.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 9, §9.4, pp. 246–248)

## Properties
Expected utility, or expected loss if the score is written as a cost, ranks actions. It does not rank states. In the lung-tumour numbers the probability of cancer is $0.90$ under every treatment that does not include a fatal test. The actions differ because the life expectancy attached to cancer, and the chance of dying from the action itself, differ. Radiotherapy’s quality-adjusted expectancy is $0.9\times 16.7+0.1\times 34.8-1=17.51$ months. Surgery’s is $0.65\times(0.9\times 20.3+0.1\times 34.8-1)=13.49$ months, which the source rounds to $13.5$. No treatment’s is $0.9\times 5.6+0.1\times 34.8=8.52$ months. The ranking uses one probability and three different outcome maps.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 9, §9.3, p. 245)

Information has value inside the problem only when it can change a later action. After bronchoscopy the posterior probability of cancer is about $0.997$ if the test is positive and about $0.73$ if it is negative, and radiotherapy remains the best treatment in both cases. The test’s quality-adjusted expectancy, including a $5\%$ chance the test is fatal, is about $16.6$ months, below the $17.5$ months of radiotherapy without the test. A datum that does not switch the action does not pay for itself in this score.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 9, §9.3, pp. 245–246)

The action with the highest probability of the best state is not automatically the solution. The solution maximises expected utility, which can favour an action that sometimes fails badly if it does well often enough under the score in use. A test with a high detection rate is not automatically worth doing. If every result leads to the same next action, the test has no decision value beyond the loss it imposes. A decision problem is not the same object as a hypothesis test. A test rejects or retains a claim about a parameter. A decision problem chooses an action whose consequences are scored by a loss or a utility.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 9, §9.1, pp. 237–239; §9.3, pp. 245–246) {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 34)

## Worked example
Using the life expectancies above and a cancer probability of $0.90$, radiotherapy scores $17.51$ quality-adjusted months, surgery $13.49$, and no treatment $8.52$. Radiotherapy is preferred. Now change only the cancer probability, to $0.50$, and recompute radiotherapy and no treatment. Radiotherapy scores $0.5\times 16.7+0.5\times 34.8-1=24.75$ months. No treatment scores $0.5\times 5.6+0.5\times 34.8=20.2$ months. Radiotherapy is still ahead. The gap changed because the base rate changed, which is a change in the decision problem’s probabilities, not a change in the actions.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 9, §9.3, p. 245)

**Your turn.** Keep the cancer probability at $0.50$ and the same life expectancies. Surgery still kills immediately with probability $0.35$, and otherwise follows the cancer-specific expectancies, minus one month, and that whole quantity is then multiplied by $0.65$. Compute surgery’s quality-adjusted expectancy and say whether it beats radiotherapy’s $24.75$.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The quantity inside is $0.5\times 20.3+0.5\times 34.8-1=26.55$. Multiply by $0.65$ to get $17.2575$ months. Radiotherapy’s $24.75$ is larger, so surgery is not preferred at this cancer probability either.
:::

## Assumptions and limits
The ranking is only as good as the probabilities and the utility, or the loss. The lung-tumour expectancies are described as extrapolations from the literature, and a different discomfort penalty would move the scores. Expected utility treats the score as something one may average. A person who refuses a small chance of immediate death at any average gain is using a different score, and the problem should say so. The analysis also assumes the list of actions is complete. An action that was never written down cannot win. Quadratic loss for a point estimate is a particular score. A different loss function picks a different summary of the same distribution.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 9, §9.3, p. 245; §9.1, p. 238) {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 34)

## History
Statistical decision theory took shape as a way to place inference inside a choice among actions with gains and losses. The Bayesian writing used here states the pattern as four explicit steps: list decisions and outcomes, assign $p(x\mid d)$, choose a utility or a loss, and optimise the expected score, with a tree when later actions depend on data the first action made available. In the estimation problem the same pattern is older in the encyclopedia account: specify $l(\hat\theta,\theta)$, and if the loss is quadratic the point estimate is the mean of the calculated distribution. Medical examples made the steps concrete. A single treatment choice among radiotherapy, surgery, and no treatment is a one-node problem. Offering a diagnostic test first makes it multistage, and the arithmetic can show that the test does not change the treatment. Household risk problems, such as whether to measure radon before spending on remediation, use the same tree with dollars and cancer risk in place of months of life.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 9, §9.1, pp. 237–239; §9.3, pp. 245–246; §9.4, pp. 246–248) {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 34)
