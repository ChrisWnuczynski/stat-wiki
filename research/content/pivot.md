## Lead context
A pivot turns an unknown-parameter problem into a probability calculation with known cutoffs, making confidence intervals possible.{{cite:siegrist2022_set_estimation}} (§8.1, Pivot Variables)

## Motivating example
A sample mean of 12.4 may look like a precise estimate of a population mean, but its uncertainty depends on the sample's spread and size. If the observations follow a normal model and the population standard deviation is unknown, the standardized difference between the sample mean and a proposed population mean has a Student distribution after the sample standard deviation replaces the unknown scale. That distribution is the same for every possible population mean and scale. Its fixed cutoffs can therefore be inverted into an interval for the unknown mean.{{cite:siegrist2022_normals}} (§6.08, The T Variable){{cite:siegrist2022_set_estimation}} (§8.1, Pivot Variables)

## Intuition
A pivot is a random quantity calculated from the data *and a candidate parameter value*. When the candidate equals the data-generating parameter, its sampling distribution no longer contains unknown features of the model. Before observing data, we can say how often it falls between two fixed quantiles. After observing data, ask which candidate parameter values make the observed quantity fall between those cutoffs. Those candidates form a confidence set. The probability statement concerns the random procedure before sampling; it is not a posterior probability for a fixed parameter after the sample arrives.{{cite:siegrist2022_set_estimation}} (§8.1, Pivot Variables and confidence sets)

A pivot often contains the very unknown parameter we seek. That does not make it a statistic, since a statistic is computed from data alone. For example, $(\overline X-\mu)/(S/\sqrt n)$ contains $\mu$, so it cannot be evaluated numerically until a candidate $\mu$ is inserted. Its *distribution* under the true $\mu$ is nevertheless known. This distinction corrects the tempting but inaccurate shorthand that every pivot is a statistic.{{cite:geyer2023_pivots}} (§4.4.2, exact pivotal quantities)

## Definition
For a model $\{P_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\}$, an exact pivot is a function $T(X,\theta)$ whose distribution under $P_\theta$ is the same known distribution for all admissible $\theta$. If the model also has nuisance parameters, the pivot distribution must be free of them as well. An *asymptotic* pivot has a limiting distribution that is parameter-free as sample size grows; a normal approximation based on an estimated standard error is commonly of that kind. The exactness applies to the stated sampling model, not to every possible population.{{cite:geyer2023_pivots}} (§4.4.2, exact and asymptotic pivotal quantities){{cite:siegrist2022_set_estimation}} (§8.1, Pivot Variables)

Let $a$ and $b$ be fixed quantiles of an exact pivot such that $\Pr_\theta\{a\leq T(X,\theta)\leq b\}=1-\alpha$ for every $\theta$. Then $C(X)=\{\theta:a\leq T(X,\theta)\leq b\}$ has coverage $1-\alpha$. If the inequalities can be solved for a scalar parameter, the set is often an interval. The algebra can instead produce two disjoint pieces, an unbounded set, or a joint region; a pivot is a device for constructing a confidence *set*, not a promise of a tidy symmetric interval.{{cite:siegrist2022_set_estimation}} (§8.1, Pivot Variables)

## Interpretation
For a normal sample with unknown $\mu$ and $\sigma$, $T=(\overline X-\mu)/(S/\sqrt n)$ has $t_{n-1}$ distribution. The numerator measures discrepancy from a candidate mean; the denominator estimates the scale of that discrepancy using the same sample. Its heavier tails account for uncertainty in that estimated scale. With known $\sigma$, $(\overline X-\mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)$ is standard normal instead. If $\sigma$ is unknown, that latter expression is not a usable pivot for $\mu$ alone because its numeric cutoffs cannot be applied without knowing the nuisance scale.{{cite:siegrist2022_normals}} (§6.08, The T Variable){{cite:geyer2023_pivots}} (§4.4.2)

The properties show that pivots can target different parameters: the difference between two normal means, a ratio of variances, or a regression slope. Their reference laws differ because the standardized numerators and estimated scales differ. A pooled two-sample $t$ needs a common population variance; the $F$ ratio of sample variances does not need the two variances to be equal, but does need independent normal samples. For the regression slope pivot, the normal-error model and its residual degrees of freedom yield $t_{n-2}$.{{cite:siegrist2022_normals}} (§6.08, The Two Sample Model){{cite:fox2016_regression}} (Ch. 6, §6.1.3, pp. 111–112)

## Properties
A known monotone transformation of a pivot is another pivot, though it may lead to a differently shaped confidence set. Pivots are therefore not unique. For a symmetric distribution, equal-tail cutoffs are convenient, but other tail allocations can have the same coverage and a different width. A pivot's exact reference distribution can also be used to test a proposed parameter: compare the observed $T(X,\theta_0)$ with its tails. Inverting all such tests returns the corresponding confidence set.{{cite:siegrist2022_set_estimation}} (§8.1, Pivot Variables and tail allocation)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Let $T(X,\theta)$ have parameter-free distribution and choose $a,b$ with $\Pr_\theta\{a\leq T(X,\theta)\leq b\}=1-\alpha$. By the definition of $C(X)$, the event $\{\theta\in C(X)\}$ is exactly the event between braces, hence $\Pr_\theta\{\theta\in C(X)\}=1-\alpha$. If $g$ is a fixed measurable function, the law of $g(T)$ is the image of the same parameter-free law and is also parameter-free.{{cite:siegrist2022_set_estimation}} (§8.1, Pivot Variables)

For iid $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ data, the normal-sample theorem gives $Z=(\overline X-\mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)\sim N(0,1)$ and $V=(n-1)S^2/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-1}$ independently. Thus $T=Z/\sqrt{V/(n-1)}\sim t_{n-1}$. Replacing $Z$ by the standardized difference of two independent normal sample means and $V$ by the sum of their independent scaled residual sums of squares proves the pooled two-sample $t_{n_1+n_2-2}$ result when their variances agree. For independent normal samples, the separate scaled sample variances are independent chi-squares divided by their respective degrees of freedom; their ratio is $F_{n_1-1,n_2-1}$. In normal simple regression with fixed nonconstant predictors, the centered slope estimate divided by its true standard deviation is normal and independent of the residual mean square scaled to $\chi^2_{n-2}$, giving the slope $t_{n-2}$ pivot.{{cite:siegrist2022_normals}} (§6.08, The T Variable; The Two Sample Model){{cite:fox2016_regression}} (Ch. 6, §6.1.3, pp. 111–112)
:::
:::

## Statistical use
Start by naming the target parameter and nuisance parameters, then find a function of data and a candidate target value whose sampling law is known without the nuisance quantities. Choose quantiles of that law and invert the inequality carefully. State whether the distribution is exact or an approximation and which assumptions make it so. For a normal mean with unknown variance, the central $t$ inequality gives $\overline X\pm t_{n-1,1-\alpha/2}S/\sqrt n$. A one-sided bound instead uses one tail. When no convenient exact pivot exists, a large-sample standardized estimator or bootstrap approximation may provide approximate coverage, which should be described as such.{{cite:siegrist2022_set_estimation}} (§8.1, Pivot Variables){{cite:geyer2023_pivots}} (§4.4.2, approximate pivotal quantities)

## Worked example
Suppose $n=16$ independent normal measurements have $\overline x=12.4$ and sample standard deviation $s=2.4$. For a 95% interval for $\mu$, the exact pivot has $15$ degrees of freedom and $t_{15,0.975}=2.13145$. Its estimated standard error is $2.4/\sqrt{16}=0.6$, so the margin is $2.13145(0.6)=1.27887$. Solving $-2.13145\leq(12.4-\mu)/0.6\leq2.13145$ gives $(11.121,13.679)$. The endpoints are fixed once these data are observed; 95% describes how often this construction covers the fixed $\mu$ across repeated normal samples.{{cite:siegrist2022_normals}} (§6.08, The T Variable){{cite:siegrist2022_set_estimation}} (§8.1, Pivot Variables)

**Your turn.** Nine independent normal observations have $\overline x=5.2$ and $s=1.5$. Use $t_{8,0.975}=2.30600$ to construct a 95% interval for $\mu$, and identify what must be true of the sampling model for this interval's coverage to be exact.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The standard error is $1.5/\sqrt9=0.5$ and the margin is $2.30600(0.5)=1.15300$. The interval is $5.2\pm1.15300=(4.047,6.353)$. The exact $t_8$ reference law requires independent observations from one normal distribution with a common unknown mean and variance. The calculation alone cannot establish those assumptions.{{cite:siegrist2022_normals}} (§6.08, The T Variable)
:::

## Common misconceptions
A pivot is not necessarily a statistic: it usually contains a candidate parameter. Parameter-free *distribution* does not mean parameter-free *formula*. Replacing a nuisance scale by its sample estimate does not automatically produce an exact pivot; the exact $t$ law relies on normal sampling and the independence result in the proof. A 95% interval obtained from a pivot does not put 95% probability on the fixed parameter after observing the data. Finally, two pivots with the same nominal coverage need not have the same width or shape, since tail allocation and inversion matter.{{cite:geyer2023_pivots}} (§4.4.2){{cite:siegrist2022_set_estimation}} (§8.1, Pivot Variables)

## Assumptions and limits
For a small sample from a strongly skewed population, the one-sample $t$ ratio need not have the exact $t_{n-1}$ law; its nominal 95% interval may miss more or less often. For two normal groups with very different variances, the pooled two-sample $t$ law fails because the pooled residual sum is no longer one common scaled chi-square. A ratio of two sample variances from heavy-tailed populations need not follow the stated $F$ distribution. And for regression with correlated errors, the residual-based slope standard error need not yield $t_{n-2}$. Each reference law attaches to a sampling model, not merely to an algebraic expression.{{cite:siegrist2022_normals}} (§6.08, normal-sample conditions){{cite:geyer2023_pivots}} (§4.4.2, exact versus approximate)

## Connections
A [statistic](statistic.qmd) is data-only; a pivot can also contain a candidate parameter. [Confidence intervals](confidence-interval.qmd) invert a pivot's probability inequality, and [hypothesis tests](../notes/inf-04-hypothesis-tests.qmd) compare its value at a null parameter with the same reference distribution. [Student's t distribution](student-t-distribution.qmd) appears when a normal mean is standardized by an estimated scale. The [bootstrap](bootstrap.qmd) can approximate a pivot's distribution where an exact analytic reference law is unavailable.{{cite:siegrist2022_set_estimation}} (§8.1){{cite:geyer2023_pivots}} (§4.4, bootstrap analogy)

## History
Student's 1908 study of the probable error of a mean established the small-sample reference distribution that now supplies a central example of a pivot with unknown variance. Neyman's 1937 theory made repeated-sample coverage of confidence regions explicit: random limits are chosen so that they contain a fixed parameter at a prescribed frequency under the model. The modern language of pivots isolates the distributional device used to construct many such regions, including the normal, Student and variance-ratio cases.{{cite:student1908}} (pp. 1–4){{cite:neyman1937}} (pp. 346–350, confidence construction){{cite:siegrist2022_set_estimation}} (§8.1, Pivot Variables)
