## Motivating example
Suppose a botanist has measured petal length and width on plants with known species. A new plant falls among three labelled training plants: two of species A and one of species B. A three-neighbour classifier predicts A, with local class proportion $2/3$. The calculation depends on the chosen distance: if width is recorded in millimetres but length in metres, raw Euclidean distance gives width a much larger numerical influence. Nearest neighbours are useful precisely because predictions can change across the predictor space without fitting one global boundary, but their locality is only as meaningful as the geometry supplied to them.{{cite:cover_hart1967}} (pp. 21–22, nearest-neighbour rule)

## Intuition
For a query $x$, the method borrows responses from training cases judged similar to $x$. A small neighbourhood follows fine detail, including chance labels; a large one averages away label noise but can cross a genuine class boundary. This is why $k$ is a tuning choice rather than a property of the population. At $k=n$, every query consults the full training sample and returns the same majority class. At $k=1$, a training case is its own nearest neighbour, making its apparent training error zero when duplicates are handled in that way, even if prediction on new cases is poor. Validation or cross-validation must therefore evaluate cases that were not used as their own neighbours.{{cite:cover_hart1967}} (p. 22, nearest-neighbour rule)

## Definition
Let the labelled training data be $(x_i,y_i)$ for $i=1,\ldots,n$, and choose a distance $d$. Sort the $x_i$ by $d(x,x_i)$ for the new query $x$, breaking equal distances by a declared rule. Write $N_k(x)$ for the selected indices. A classification rule takes the most frequent class among $\{y_i:i\in N_k(x)\}$; the corresponding local class-proportion estimate is $\widehat\tau_g(x)=k^{-1}\sum_{i\in N_k(x)}\mathbf1\{y_i=g\}$. For a numerical response, $\widehat m(x)=k^{-1}\sum_{i\in N_k(x)}y_i$ is nearest-neighbour regression. These estimates use exactly the same neighbours but answer different prediction questions. Ties between classes also require a rule, even when the distance ordering is unique.{{cite:cover_hart1967}} (p. 22, metric-space formulation)

## Properties
The local vote is a sample proportion. If the $k$ neighbour labels could be treated as independent Bernoulli draws with one common conditional class probability $\tau(x)$, the proportion would have mean $\tau(x)$ and variance $\tau(x)\{1-\tau(x)\}/k$. That idealization explains why larger $k$ stabilizes a vote. It also exposes the bias: a wide neighbourhood contains points at which the true class probability may differ from $\tau(x)$, so its average need not target the probability at the query. A fixed finite $k$ cannot drive this voting noise to zero as the sample grows; the familiar consistency regime instead lets $k$ grow while $k/n$ tends to zero, under suitable regularity conditions.{{cite:cover_hart1967}} (pp. 22–23, k-neighbour discussion and Fix–Hodges consistency)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Let $Z_j=\mathbf1\{Y_j=g\}$ for the $k$ selected neighbour labels. Under the stated idealized independent-common-probability assumption, $\mathbb E Z_j=\tau$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Z_j)=\tau(1-\tau)$. Hence $\mathbb E(k^{-1}\sum_j Z_j)=\tau$, while independence makes $\operatorname{Var}(k^{-1}\sum_jZ_j)=k^{-2}\sum_j\tau(1-\tau)=\tau(1-\tau)/k$. If the probabilities instead differ across neighbours, the expectation becomes their average $k^{-1}\sum_j\tau(x_j)$; the difference between this average and $\tau(x)$ is the local-smoothing bias. For $k=n$, $N_k(x)$ is the entire training index set for every $x$, so the class vote is constant across queries. These are finite-sample algebraic facts, not a proof of the asymptotic consistency or Cover–Hart results.{{cite:cover_hart1967}} (pp. 21–23, local vote and asymptotic setting)
:::
:::

## Interpretation — Distance, scale and ties
Euclidean distance adds squared coordinate differences, so a rescaling can reorder neighbours. If a plant height measured in millimetres changes by $100$ while a petal-width proportion changes by $0.2$, the first coordinate overwhelms the second numerically, regardless of predictive value. Standardizing each predictor using the **training** mean and standard deviation makes units comparable; the same fitted transformation must be applied to validation and future queries. It does not guarantee equal relevance, remove correlation or turn noise into signal. A distance tie at the $k$th position can make a prediction depend on training row order unless the tie policy is explicit. An odd $k$ only prevents a two-class vote tie when exactly $k$ labels are included.{{cite:cover_hart1967}} (p. 22, metric-space formulation)

## Statistical use — Choosing and using k
Fit preprocessing on training data, evaluate candidate $k$ values on held-out validation cases or by cross-validation, and freeze the choice before a final test evaluation. If a class is rare, overall accuracy alone can hide poor minority-class prediction; inspect an appropriate loss or class-specific rates as well. Distance-weighted voting is a variant in which close neighbours carry more influence, while a radius rule chooses all points within a fixed distance and allows the number of voters to vary. Those choices change the estimator and must be tuned with the same care. A nearest-neighbour query can require comparing the query with every stored point, although tree-based search can reduce the work in favourable geometries.{{cite:cover_hart1967}} (pp. 21–23, k-neighbour discussion and Fix–Hodges consistency)

## Worked example
Take a one-dimensional query at $x=4$ and labelled training positions $(1,A)$, $(3,B)$, $(5,B)$, $(8,A)$ and $(9,A)$. Their distances from the query are $3,1,1,4,5$. Resolve the distance tie by increasing training index. The three nearest labels are $B,B,A$, so three-neighbour classification predicts B and estimates its local proportion as $2/3$. If the same observations instead carry numerical responses $10,20,24,30,40$, the three-neighbour regression prediction is $(20+24+10)/3=18$. At $k=5$, the majority label becomes A, with proportion $3/5$, and the regression prediction becomes $124/5=24.8$. The change illustrates smoothing: a larger neighbourhood can change the decision, not just its confidence.{{cite:cover_hart1967}} (p. 22, metric-space formulation)

**Your turn.** The query is $x=6$ and training positions with class labels are $(2,A)$, $(5,B)$, $(7,B)$, $(10,A)$ and $(12,A)$. Find the three-neighbour class and local B proportion. Then state the five-neighbour class.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The distances are $4,1,1,4,6$. Under increasing training-index order for the tie at distance $4$, the three selected points are at $5$, $7$ and $2$, with labels $B,B,A$. The prediction is B and its local proportion is $2/3$. With all five points the labels are three A and two B, so the five-neighbour prediction is A. Specifying the tie rule matters to the selected set even though the three-neighbour prediction is B under either distance-four choice here.
:::

## Common misconceptions
The class fraction $2/3$ in a local vote is not automatically a calibrated probability for a future plant; its neighbours may not represent the conditional population at the query. An odd $k$ does not settle a distance tie, and it does not prevent a tie among more than two classes. Standardization only changes geometry; it cannot identify which features carry the biological signal. Zero apparent error at $k=1$ is a statement about reusing training points, not a guarantee of zero error on new plants. The Cover–Hart factor-of-two statement concerns asymptotic one-neighbour risk under its conditions; it is not a finite-sample guarantee for every metric.{{cite:cover_hart1967}} (pp. 21–22, large-sample bound)

## Assumptions and limits — When it fails
If a disease label depends on a rare genetic marker but a dataset contains hundreds of irrelevant measurements, Euclidean neighbours can be close mainly on noise coordinates; the meaningful marker is diluted. If two otherwise identical patients are recorded in different units, distances can reorder as in the changing-units question. If a rare species occupies a narrow region and $k$ is chosen so large that neighbours cross into a common species region, majority voting can erase that local class. If an exact duplicate of a query has contradictory labels, distance zero does not determine a unique answer; the tie and vote rules must say what to do. These are concrete failures of metric choice, neighbourhood size and data consistency.{{cite:cover_hart1967}} (p. 22, metric-space formulation)

## History
Fix and Hodges studied a majority-vote rule using a growing number of neighbours; Cover and Hart explicitly credit their earlier formulation and consistency analysis. Cover and Hart's 1967 paper then studied the single-neighbour decision rule and derived its asymptotic error bound relative to Bayes error. The distinction remains useful: one neighbour gives a particularly simple rule with a risk bound, whereas an appropriately growing neighbourhood can average away label noise.{{cite:cover_hart1967}} (pp. 21–22, introduction and historical attribution)
