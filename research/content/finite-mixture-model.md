## Lead context
A mixture describes a population by combining several component distributions when the component that produced each observation is not observed.{{cite:mclachlan_peel2000_book}} (Ch. 1, publisher summary)

## Motivating example
Suppose a laboratory records a continuous measurement from specimens collected at two sites, but the site label is lost. Measurements from one site may cluster near zero and those from the other near four. A single normal curve blurs the two concentrations into an inflated spread. A two-component mixture instead records a distribution for each site and a probability of drawing from each. It can describe the combined measurements even though the site of an individual specimen is unknown. If site membership is the scientific question, the mixture gives probabilities of membership; it does not recover the missing labels with certainty.{{cite:sklearn_mixture2026}} (§2.1, Gaussian mixtures and probabilistic assignment)

## Intuition
To generate one observation, first draw an unobserved component label $Z$, then draw $X$ from that component's distribution. Forgetting $Z$ leaves the weighted average of the component densities. The weights describe the mix of sources *before* looking at a measurement. Once $X=x$ is observed, components that put more density near $x$ become more plausible origins. This distinction between a mixing weight and a posterior membership probability is the reason a model can represent uncertain group assignments rather than forcing every point into one cluster.{{cite:sklearn_mixture2026}} (§2.1.1, latent Gaussians and EM)

The same sum can be used for a different purpose: to approximate an irregular density by simpler pieces. In that use, a component need not correspond to a physical subpopulation. Two fitted normal curves can improve density fit without establishing two biological species or two causal mechanisms. Thus the interpretation of components must come from the study design and external evidence, not from a visible pair of bumps alone.{{cite:mclachlan_peel2000_book}} (Ch. 1, publisher summary; book description, identifiability and spurious clusters)

## Definition
Let $Z$ take values $1,\ldots,H$ with $\Pr(Z=h)=\pi_h$, where $\pi_h\geq0$ and $\sum_h\pi_h=1$. If $X\mid Z=h$ has density $f_h(x)$ with respect to a common measure, the observed density is $f(x)=\sum_{h=1}^H\pi_h f_h(x)$. For discrete $X$, replace densities by probability mass functions. A *finite* mixture has a fixed finite number $H$ of components. For fixed $H$ and fixed parametric component families, the model is parametric; allowing $H$ to grow with sample size describes a sequence of increasingly flexible models.{{cite:mclachlan_peel2000_book}} (Ch. 1, publisher summary)

For an independent sample $x_1,\ldots,x_n$, the observed likelihood is $L=\prod_{i=1}^n\sum_h\pi_h f_h(x_i)$. The component label has been summed out inside every observation's factor. In particular, $\log L=\sum_i\log\{\sum_h\pi_hf_h(x_i)\}$, which cannot be changed into $\sum_i\sum_h\log\{\pi_hf_h(x_i)\}$. The latter expression would treat each observation as if it had been generated by every component.{{cite:mclachlan_peel2000_book}} (Ch. 2, publisher contents, ML fitting){{cite:sklearn_mixture2026}} (§2.1.1, unlabeled-data fitting)

## Interpretation
When the component label is missing, Bayes' rule gives its posterior probability $\tau_h(x)=\Pr(Z=h\mid X=x)=\pi_hf_h(x)/f(x)$ for $f(x)>0$. These *responsibilities* add to one. They depend both on the prior mixture proportions and on how well each component explains the observed value. A point where two weighted densities are similar has an uncertain label even if a software routine prints only its most likely component.{{cite:sklearn_mixture2026}} (§2.1.1, sample probabilities and assignments)

The components also combine at the level of moments. If $\mu_h=\mathbb E(X\mid Z=h)$ and $\sigma_h^2=\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Z=h)$ exist, then $\mu=\sum_h\pi_h\mu_h$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X)=\sum_h\pi_h\sigma_h^2+\sum_h\pi_h(\mu_h-\mu)^2$. The first variance term measures spread within sources; the second measures separation among their means. This decomposition explains why an undifferentiated single-normal fit can report a large variance even when every component is relatively tight. The inherited two-normal calculation below makes the two terms concrete.

## Properties
The weighted sum is a valid density because its integral is one. Its component labels are arbitrary: permuting component numbers, means, variances and weights together leaves the observed density and likelihood unchanged. This *label switching* is a specific failure of parameter identifiability, even when the mixture density itself is estimable. Any summary of component-specific estimates across repeated fits must align labels or use a label-invariant quantity.{{cite:mclachlan_peel2000_book}} (Description, identifiability problems){{cite:sklearn_mixture2026}} (§2.1.1, latent Gaussian components)

A mixture need not have as many modes as components. The inherited example has separated means and two peaks; moving two equal-variance normal means from zero and four to zero and one can yield one visible peak. Conversely, a small dense component can create a narrow peak while having little effect on the overall mean. The component count $H$, the number of modes and the number of real-world groups answer different questions. A Gaussian mixture also permits unequal component covariance matrices, so an elongated cloud and a compact cloud can receive different responsibilities even at equal distances from their centers.{{cite:sklearn_mixture2026}} (§2.1, covariance structures and clustering)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Since each $f_h$ integrates to one, $\int f(x)\,dx=\sum_h\pi_h\int f_h(x)\,dx=\sum_h\pi_h=1$. The joint law gives $\Pr(Z=h,X\in dx)=\pi_hf_h(x)\,dx$; dividing by $f(x)\,dx$ proves the responsibility formula and summing its numerators proves $\sum_h\tau_h(x)=1$. Taking conditional expectations gives $\mathbb E X=\sum_h\pi_h\mu_h$. Expanding $X-\mu=(X-\mu_h)+(\mu_h-\mu)$ conditional on $Z=h$, the cross term has expectation zero, so $\operatorname{Var}(X)=\sum_h\pi_h\{\sigma_h^2+(\mu_h-\mu)^2\}$. For any permutation $s$ of $1,\ldots,H$, $\sum_h\pi_{s(h)}f_{s(h)}(x)$ is the same sum, proving label invariance.{{cite:mclachlan_peel2000_book}} (Ch. 1, publisher summary, mixture definition)

For the Gaussian singularity, take two distinct observations and at least two components with positive weights. Fix one component at a normal density with positive variance, so it assigns positive density to every observation. Center a second normal component at $x_1$ with standard deviation $\epsilon$. Its contribution at $x_1$ is $\pi_1/(\sqrt{2\pi}\epsilon)$, which tends to infinity as $\epsilon\downarrow0$. At every other observation the fixed component supplies a positive lower bound. Hence the product likelihood tends to infinity. This proves that unconstrained Gaussian-mixture maximum likelihood need not have a finite maximizer; it does not say that every fitted sequence follows that path.{{cite:sklearn_mixture2026}} (§2.1.1, Singularities)

## Statistical use
For density estimation or clustering, specify a component family and a candidate $H$, fit its weights and component parameters, and inspect the resulting density and responsibilities. The EM algorithm alternates between responsibilities given current parameters and parameter updates using those fractional memberships. It searches for a local fit; initialization matters. The inherited source chapter develops the full Gaussian likelihood and its EM updates. Comparing candidate counts with held-out predictive performance or a stated information criterion asks a separate question from computing responsibilities under one fixed fit.{{cite:sklearn_mixture2026}} (§2.1.1, EM, initialization and number of components){{cite:mclachlan_peel2000_book}} (Ch. 2 and 6, publisher contents)

For a $p$-dimensional Gaussian mixture with unrestricted full covariances, one component has $p$ mean coordinates and $p(p+1)/2$ distinct covariance entries. Together with $H-1$ free weights, the count is $d=Hp+Hp(p+1)/2+(H-1)$. For $H=3$ and $p=2$, $d=6+9+2=17$. A common covariance or diagonal covariances change that count and the fitted geometry. For a binary variable alongside the continuous coordinates, the inherited location-model construction fits a mixture within each binary category and multiplies by its category probability. It can ask whether the two categories occupy different continuous clouds, but it increases the number of parameters and shares no component parameters across categories unless additional structure is imposed.{{cite:sklearn_mixture2026}} (§2.1.1, full, diagonal and tied covariances)

## Worked example
Keep the inherited mixture $0.3\,N(0,1)+0.7\,N(4,1)$. Its marginal mean is $0.3(0)+0.7(4)=2.8$, and its variance is $0.3(1)+0.7(1)+0.3(0-2.8)^2+0.7(4-2.8)^2=1+2.352+1.008=4.36$. The marginal standard deviation is $\sqrt{4.36}=2.0881$, much larger than either component standard deviation of one. This extra spread is between components, not extra noise inside either source.

Now observe $x=1$. The two normal densities have a common factor $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$, so the first component's posterior membership is $\tau_1(1)=0.3e^{-1/2}/\{0.3e^{-1/2}+0.7e^{-9/2}\}=0.9590$. Although its prior weight was only $0.3$, a value of one is much more likely under the component centered at zero. The assignment is strong but still probabilistic; the probability of the second component is $1-0.9590=0.0410$.

**Your turn.** In the same mixture, compute the posterior probability that an observation at $x=3$ arose from the component centered at zero. Explain why it differs so much from the prior weight $0.3$.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
At $x=3$, $\tau_1(3)=0.3e^{-9/2}/\{0.3e^{-9/2}+0.7e^{-1/2}\}=0.007788$. The observation is three standard deviations from the first mean but only one from the second. The likelihood ratio shifts the prior odds sharply toward the component centered at four; its posterior probability is $1-0.007788=0.992212$.
:::

## Common misconceptions
A mixing weight is not the posterior probability of a component for every observation: the latter changes with $x$. A fitted Gaussian component is not automatically a real cluster or a causal class; one population with a skewed shape might also be approximated by several normals. More components do not guarantee more density modes, as the close-mean normal example shows. Finally, EM's increasing likelihood does not certify a global maximum, especially when an unconstrained variance can collapse onto one data point.{{cite:sklearn_mixture2026}} (§2.1.1, EM and singularities){{cite:mclachlan_peel2000_book}} (Description, identifiability and fitting)

## Assumptions and limits
The density formula requires nonnegative weights summing to one and component distributions on the same observation space. The ordinary iid likelihood additionally assumes observations are conditionally generated independently under fixed mixture parameters. A two-normal mixture may fit a long-tailed single population by placing a broad, low-weight component over outliers; reading that component as a distinct type of person would be an unsupported conclusion. With nearly identical components, membership probabilities remain close to the mixing weights over much of the data, so individual origin is poorly determined even when the overall density is precise. With an unconstrained Gaussian variance, the singular path proved above makes a largest finite likelihood unavailable; covariance constraints or regularization must be declared, and model-count comparisons then refer to that constrained fit.{{cite:sklearn_mixture2026}} (§2.1.1, singularities, covariance choices and component count)

## History
In his 1894 paper on the mathematical theory of evolution, Karl Pearson proposed that apparent asymmetry in a frequency curve could arise from an unobserved combination of normal groups, and worked on separating such curves into their component distributions. That was a modelling question about hidden composition, not merely a new shape parameter for one homogeneous curve. The later EM formulation by Dempster, Laird and Rubin in 1977 treated maximum likelihood with missing data generally, providing a systematic framework for the unobserved labels in mixture fitting. Modern accounts also emphasize identifiability, component-count choice and applications beyond normal components.{{cite:pearson1894_mixture}} (pp. 71–72, introduction){{cite:dempster_laird_rubin1977}} (Abstract, incomplete-data maximum likelihood){{cite:mclachlan_peel2000_book}} (Description; Ch. 1 and 5, publisher contents)
