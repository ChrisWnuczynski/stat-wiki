## Lead context

National statistical offices in the middle of the nineteenth century were already counting people, trade, and births, but they did not count the same things in the same way. A return for England and Wales could not be set beside a return for the United Kingdom without mixing territories. The First International Statistical Congress was the Brussels meeting that tried to make those documents comparable.

## Motivating example

A parliamentarian who asks for a statistical return may receive it for England and Wales one year, for Great Britain the next, and for the United Kingdom the year after. The three documents can share a title and still describe different populations, so a ratio of the printed totals is not a ratio of like with like. In 1853 the governments that sent deputies to Brussels were asked to say how their statistics were actually collected, and the answers showed departments that did not even publish on the same plan.{{cite:levi1854}} (pp. 3–4)

## Definition

The First International Statistical Congress opened in Brussels on Monday 19 September 1853. It was called by the Central Statistical Commission of Belgium, with Adolphe Quetelet presiding over the commission, and it was the first of nine international statistical congresses held in European cities from 1853 to 1876. The stated aim was uniformity in the themes and methods of national statistics, so that documents from different countries could be compared. The official proceedings record the sittings of 19, 20, 21, and 22 September 1853.{{cite:levi1854}} (p. 3){{cite:randeraad2011}} (pp. 50, 53, 64)

Quetelet gave the introductory address. A deputy from each state then described how statistics were collected and how the work was organised. M. Piercot, the Belgian minister of the interior, was elected president. Official representatives came from states across Europe and beyond, among them Austria, France, Great Britain, Prussia, several Italian states, Egypt, and the United States.{{cite:levi1854}} (p. 3)

Comparison needs a shared list of categories and a shared territory. If two offices agree on what a birth is, which people are in the count, and when the count is taken, their rates can be subtracted. If they do not, the arithmetic still produces a number, but the number does not estimate a common quantity. The congress was an attempt to negotiate that shared language before the tables were printed.{{cite:levi1854}} (p. 1){{cite:randeraad2011}} (p. 50)

## Properties

The next city was chosen in the last plenary of a congress, and the host country set the agenda. Population statistics, especially censuses, and the organisation of statistics at home and abroad came back at meeting after meeting. Hosts also added themes of their own. At Brussels the Belgian commission, following Auguste Visschers, wanted statistics of workers' budgets. That politically charged topic was dropped after Brussels and later returned, in another form, as a discussion of mutual funds at Berlin.{{cite:randeraad2011}} (p. 55)

Uniformity was a demand on classifications. A national administration that declined the common nomenclature stayed outside the programme, because the congress had no authority to replace that administration's categories. Levi's account of the programme asked for a nomenclature held in common, simpler base tables, a central statistical commission in each state, and an exchange of publications and of the schedules used to collect information. A centre in each country would send and receive those documents.{{cite:levi1854}} (p. 4)

Nine meetings from 1853 to 1876 are separated by eight gaps. The calendar span is \(1876-1853=23\) years, so equal spacing would put \(23/8=2.875\) years between consecutive meetings. The congresses were not in fact equally spaced; the arithmetic only shows how short the series was relative to the ambition of a permanent international routine.{{cite:randeraad2011}} (p. 50)

## Statistical use

The practical proposal was institutional. A central commission, formed from heads of administration together with people practised in statistical work, would settle what was collected and how it was published, and would correspond with similar commissions elsewhere. Branch commissions would handle what was only provincial. Without that machinery, returns stayed scattered: any member of Parliament might demand a table, and the table might never appear again in the same form.{{cite:levi1854}} (pp. 3–4)

The same difficulty appears whenever a rate is computed on a territory that the next table does not use. Suppose one office records 240 events among 800 people and another records 150 events among 600 people. The rates are \(240/800=0.30\) and \(150/600=0.25\), and their difference is \(0.05\). Adding the counts is legitimate only after the events and the populations have been defined in the same way. When they have, the pooled rate is
\[
\frac{240+150}{800+600}=\frac{390}{1400}=\frac{39}{140}\approx 0.2786.
\]
If the categories differ, \(39/140\) is not a rate for a combined population. It is only the arithmetic of two incompatible tables.{{cite:levi1854}} (pp. 1, 4)

## Worked example

Take the two offices above as a miniature of the confusion Levi described for England and Wales, Great Britain, and the United Kingdom. The separate rates are \(0.30\) and \(0.25\). Their difference, \(0.05\), answers a question about these two tables. It does not answer a question about one population observed twice. The pooled figure \(39/140\) answers a question about one population only when both numerators count the same kind of event and both denominators count the same kind of person.{{cite:levi1854}} (pp. 3–4)

**Your turn.** A second pair of tables records 80 events among 200 people and 90 events among 300 people, under definitions that do match. Find each rate, the difference of the rates, and the pooled rate.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The rates are \(80/200=0.4\) and \(90/300=0.3\). The difference is \(0.1\). The pooled rate is \((80+90)/(200+300)=170/500=0.34\).
:::

## Common misconceptions

The meeting was not a permanent statistical office, and it did not by itself make later national tables comparable. No further congress of this series met after 1876, even though participants still hoped the series would continue. Treating the Brussels resolutions as if every later census had adopted them confuses a programme with a completed reform.{{cite:randeraad2011}} (p. 51)

The congress was also not a mathematical society in the later sense. At a subsequent session there was still no settled agreement on what the subject was. Quetelet refused a split between a historical, administrative school and a mathematical, academic school, and Ernst Engel reported that he had gathered 180 definitions of statistics from textbooks and periodicals. A demand for uniform tables sat on top of that unsettled meaning.{{cite:randeraad2011}} (p. 55)

Comparability assumed that the facts behind the numbers were already the same kind of fact. Nineteenth-century administrations were changing quickly, and national statistical services did not share one institutional form. French and German arguments about population figures show how a count could be pressed into a national claim. Nationalism was not the only obstacle: the belief that statistics was a neutral science made the remaining disagreements harder to see.{{cite:randeraad2011}} (pp. 50–51)

A host's special theme could travel poorly. Workers' budgets mattered in Brussels and then left the agenda. Ethnographic statistics suited Vienna; sanitary statistics suited London in 1860, where William Farr and Edwin Chadwick spoke for public health and Florence Nightingale pressed a scheme for hospital statistics. Those additions were real statistical work, and they were also national projects. A classification built for one of them did not automatically serve the next congress.{{cite:randeraad2011}} (p. 55)

## History

The idea was formed around the Great Exhibition in London in 1851, by Quetelet, with Auguste Visschers and a small circle of specialists. Napoleon III's coup in December 1851 and the Schleswig-Holstein disputes of 1852 delayed the meeting until September 1853. International scientific gatherings were still a new form. Quetelet had already taken part in others, but the statistical series was the one devoted to the content and methods of national counts.{{cite:randeraad2011}} (p. 53)

Across 1853–1876 the nine congresses brought together more than 5,000 participants, mostly European, and later from the Americas, North Africa, and Turkey. By 1876 the early hope of rapid uniformity had not been met, and the series stopped. The transfer of a classification from one administration to another had turned out to be a political and institutional problem, not a matter of printing one more resolution.{{cite:randeraad2011}} (pp. 50–51)
