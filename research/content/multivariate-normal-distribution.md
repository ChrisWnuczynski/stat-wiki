## Lead context
A multivariate normal model gives a joint distribution to several measurements that move together. Its covariance matrix records both each measurement's spread and the way pairs co-vary. This matters whenever a prediction, region, or sum depends on more than one coordinate.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §§1.1, 1.3)

## Motivating example
Imagine measuring both sepal length and sepal width for flowers. A separate normal curve for each measurement describes its marginal spread, but it says nothing about whether longer sepals tend to accompany wider ones. A bivariate normal model adds their covariance. Its tilted elliptical contours make a simultaneous observation's location interpretable: a pair near each marginal mean need not be typical if it lies across the main direction of variation. The same joint model predicts width from an observed length and describes uncertainty in a sum or difference of measurements.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §§1.1, 1.3, “Multivariate normal model” and covariance ellipsoids)

## Intuition
In one dimension, a normal density depends on squared distance from its mean measured in standard-deviation units. In $p$ dimensions that distance becomes $D^2=(\boldsymbol x-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol x-\boldsymbol\mu)$. Points with the same $D^2$ have the same density. Eigenvectors of $\Sigma$ give the principal directions of the resulting ellipsoids; the square roots of its eigenvalues set their relative axis lengths. The mean shifts the centre, while covariance changes shape and orientation. The density and CDF plots above use two unit marginal variances and correlation $0.6$: the tilt in the density comes from the nonzero off-diagonal entries.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §1.3, “Multivariate normal model”; covariance ellipsoids) {{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 6 Extra, multivariate normal geometry)

## Definition
For the full-dimensional model, let $\boldsymbol\mu\in\mathbb R^p$ and let the symmetric covariance matrix $\Sigma$ be positive definite. The infobox density then uses $|\Sigma|>0$ and $\Sigma^{-1}$. The diagonal entries are coordinate variances, and entry $\sigma_{ij}$ is the covariance of coordinates $i$ and $j$. A positive semidefinite but singular covariance still defines a jointly normal vector through an affine transformation of independent standard normals, although the resulting law has no density with respect to $p$-dimensional volume. This distinction matters when coordinates are exact linear combinations of one another.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §§1.1, 1.3; Ch. 3, §3.1, Ex. 3.9) {{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 6 Extra, Thm. 31)

Equivalently, write $\boldsymbol X=\boldsymbol\mu+L\boldsymbol Z$ where the components of $\boldsymbol Z$ are independent standard normals and $LL^\top=\Sigma$. With positive-definite $\Sigma$, a Cholesky factor $L$ is invertible and the change-of-variables formula gives the displayed density. The representation also explains why every fixed linear combination of coordinates is normal, including a constant one if its variance is zero. A list of individually normal marginals alone does not establish this representation or joint normality.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §1.3; Ch. 3, §3.1, Ex. 3.9) {{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 6 Extra, Thm. 31)

## Properties
For $\boldsymbol X\sim\operatorname N_p(\boldsymbol\mu,\Sigma)$ and a fixed matrix $A$ and vector $\boldsymbol b$, the affine image $A\boldsymbol X+\boldsymbol b$ is normal with mean $A\boldsymbol\mu+\boldsymbol b$ and covariance $A\Sigma A^\top$. Taking rows of $A$ selects marginal coordinates; taking a single row forms a normal sum or contrast. If $A$ loses rank, the output covariance may be singular even though the original covariance is positive definite. The infobox moment-generating and characteristic functions are the scalar normal formulas applied to $\boldsymbol t^\top\boldsymbol X$.{{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 6 Extra, Thm. 31 and proof; Ex. 32) {{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 3, §3.1)

Partition $\boldsymbol X=(\boldsymbol X_A,\boldsymbol X_B)$ and $\Sigma$ into matching blocks. With invertible $\Sigma_{BB}$, the conditional law of $\boldsymbol X_A$ given $\boldsymbol X_B=\boldsymbol x_B$ is normal with mean $\boldsymbol\mu_A+\Sigma_{AB}\Sigma_{BB}^{-1}(\boldsymbol x_B-\boldsymbol\mu_B)$ and covariance $\Sigma_{AA}-\Sigma_{AB}\Sigma_{BB}^{-1}\Sigma_{BA}$. The second expression, a Schur complement, does not depend on the observed value. For two coordinates it becomes $\operatorname{Var}(X_1\mid X_2)=\sigma_1^2(1-\rho^2)$. With joint normality, zero cross-covariance makes blocks independent; absent joint normality, zero covariance alone does not generally imply independence.{{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 6 Extra, Thms. 24, 26 and conditional-normal discussion) {{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §1.3)

For positive-definite $\Sigma$, the squared Mahalanobis distance $D^2$ has a $\chi^2_p$ distribution. Thus the ellipsoid $D^2\leq\chi^2_{p,1-\alpha}$ contains probability $1-\alpha$ under the specified population model; a density contour by itself is not a confidence region for an estimated mean. The density is maximised at $\boldsymbol\mu$. Its differential entropy is $\tfrac12\log[(2\pi e)^p|\Sigma|]$, which changes with the measurement units and covariance determinant.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §1.3, “Multivariate normal model”; covariance ellipsoids and entropy) {{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 6 Extra, multivariate normal geometry)

**Proof idea.** The standard-normal representation supplies affine closure and marginals. Completing a square in the joint exponent supplies the conditional formula. Whitening turns the quadratic distance into a sum of independent squared standard normals.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §1.3; Ch. 3, §3.1) {{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 6 Extra, Thms. 24, 26, 31)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Choose invertible $L$ with $LL^\top=\Sigma$ and let $\boldsymbol Z\sim\operatorname N_p(0,I)$. In the transformation $\boldsymbol x=\boldsymbol\mu+L\boldsymbol z$, $\boldsymbol z=L^{-1}(\boldsymbol x-\boldsymbol\mu)$ and $|\det L|=|\Sigma|^{1/2}$. Substituting in $(2\pi)^{-p/2}\exp(-\boldsymbol z^\top\boldsymbol z/2)$ gives the density in the infobox and proves that it integrates to one. Applying $A$ gives $A\boldsymbol X+\boldsymbol b=A\boldsymbol\mu+\boldsymbol b+AL\boldsymbol Z$, whose mean and covariance are the stated values. This representation remains valid for singular $AL L^\top A^\top$, although there is no ordinary full-dimensional density in that case.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §1.3; Ch. 3, §3.1, Ex. 3.9) {{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 6 Extra, Thm. 31)

For any $\boldsymbol t$, $\boldsymbol t^\top\boldsymbol X$ is univariate normal with mean $\boldsymbol t^\top\boldsymbol\mu$ and variance $\boldsymbol t^\top\Sigma\boldsymbol t$. Its moment-generating function at one is $\exp(\boldsymbol t^\top\boldsymbol\mu+\boldsymbol t^\top\Sigma\boldsymbol t/2)$; replacing $\boldsymbol t$ by $i\boldsymbol t$ gives the characteristic function. Choosing rows of $A$ proves the marginal statements. If $\Sigma_{AB}=0$, the joint characteristic function factors into the characteristic functions for blocks $A$ and $B$, which proves their independence under joint normality. The factorisation does not follow merely from zero covariance for an arbitrary distribution.{{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 6 Extra, Thms. 24, 31 and proofs)

For conditional normality, define $C=\Sigma_{AB}\Sigma_{BB}^{-1}$ and $\boldsymbol R=\boldsymbol X_A-\boldsymbol\mu_A-C(\boldsymbol X_B-\boldsymbol\mu_B)$. The pair $(\boldsymbol R,\boldsymbol X_B)$ is jointly normal by affine closure and has cross-covariance $\Sigma_{AB}-C\Sigma_{BB}=0$, so its blocks are independent. Its first-block covariance is $\Sigma_{AA}-\Sigma_{AB}\Sigma_{BB}^{-1}\Sigma_{BA}$. Therefore, conditioning on $\boldsymbol X_B=\boldsymbol x_B$ leaves $\boldsymbol R$ unchanged and adds $\boldsymbol\mu_A+C(\boldsymbol x_B-\boldsymbol\mu_B)$, giving the stated conditional mean and covariance.{{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 6 Extra, Thm. 26 and proof)

Finally, $L^{-1}(\boldsymbol X-\boldsymbol\mu)=\boldsymbol Z$, so $D^2=\boldsymbol Z^\top\boldsymbol Z=\sum_{j=1}^p Z_j^2\sim\chi^2_p$. Differentiating the log density shows its unique maximum at $\boldsymbol\mu$. Taking minus the expectation of that log density uses $\mathbb E D^2=p$ and yields $\tfrac12\log[(2\pi e)^p|\Sigma|]$. Diagonalising $\Sigma$ makes a fixed-$D^2$ contour's axis lengths $\sqrt{D^2\lambda_j}$.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §1.3, “Multivariate normal model”; covariance ellipsoids and entropy)

## Statistical inference
Independent vector observations from a common $\operatorname N_p(\boldsymbol\mu,\Sigma)$ model give a sample mean for location and a sample covariance for dependence. Inference about a linear contrast $\boldsymbol a^\top\boldsymbol\mu$ uses the univariate normal law of $\boldsymbol a^\top\boldsymbol X$; inference about a simultaneous mean region must also account for estimation of $\Sigma$. The population ellipsoid above describes a new vector under known parameters, so its $\chi^2$ cutoff should not be reused as though the parameters had been known after fitting. This distinction matters because plugging sample estimates into a population distance does not leave its exact reference law unchanged.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §§1.1, 1.3) {{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 6 Extra, Thm. 31)

The conditional formula is also a prediction rule. Once one block of measurements is observed, the cross-covariance shifts the mean of the unobserved block. Its conditional covariance is smaller in the positive-semidefinite ordering than its marginal covariance. A prediction interval for one coordinate uses the conditional variance and the normal quantiles, with extra uncertainty when the mean and covariance parameters must be estimated from a finite sample.{{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 6 Extra, Thm. 26) {{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §1.3)

## Generating random samples
Compute a factor $L$ satisfying $LL^\top=\Sigma$, generate $p$ independent standard-normal values as $\boldsymbol Z$, then return $\boldsymbol\mu+L\boldsymbol Z$. Cholesky factorisation works for positive-definite $\Sigma$. An eigenfactor can represent a singular positive-semidefinite covariance too; it produces samples on a lower-dimensional affine subspace. Simulating each marginal normal independently would erase the desired nonzero cross-covariances.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 3, §3.1, Ex. 3.9) {{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 6 Extra, Thm. 31)

## Worked example
Let $(X_1,X_2)^\top$ be jointly normal with $\boldsymbol\mu=(1,2)^\top$ and $\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\1&9\end{pmatrix}$. Both principal minors are positive and $|\Sigma|=35$, so this is a positive-definite covariance. Suppose $X_2=5$ is observed. The conditional mean of $X_1$ is $1+(1/9)(5-2)=4/3$ and its conditional variance is $4-1^2/9=35/9$. Hence $X_1\mid X_2=5\sim\operatorname N(4/3,35/9)$. The joint model also says $T=X_1+X_2\sim\operatorname N(3,4+9+2(1)=15)$, so $P(T>3)=1/2$. The covariance contributes twice to the sum variance; omitting it would give the wrong uncertainty.{{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 6 Extra, Thms. 26, 31)

**Your turn.** In the same model, observe $X_2=-1$. Find the conditional mean and variance of $X_1$; then find the distribution of $X_1-X_2$.{{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 6 Extra, Thms. 26, 31)

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The conditional mean is $1+(1/9)(-1-2)=2/3$ and the variance remains $35/9$. Thus $X_1\mid X_2=-1\sim\operatorname N(2/3,35/9)$. For the difference, use $\boldsymbol a=(1,-1)^\top$: its mean is $1-2=-1$ and its variance is $4+9-2(1)=11$, so $X_1-X_2\sim\operatorname N(-1,11)$. The two calculations condition and transform in different ways.{{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 6 Extra, Thms. 26, 31)
:::

## Common misconceptions
Normal marginal distributions do not by themselves imply a jointly normal vector: dependence can give the joint law another shape. A zero covariance implies independence between blocks here because the *joint* law is normal; applying that rule to arbitrary measurements is an error. Likewise, whitening makes a covariance matrix the identity, but it cannot turn a non-normal population into a normal one or make separate observations independent. The covariance is a matrix of squared measurement units, not a matrix of correlations unless all marginal standard deviations equal one.{{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 6 Extra, Thms. 24, 26, 31) {{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §§1.1, 1.3; Ch. 3, §3.1)

## Assumptions and limits
The simple density formula requires a positive-definite covariance. With a singular covariance, use the affine representation and recognise that ordinary $p$-dimensional density and $\Sigma^{-1}$ are unavailable. An actual measurement with hard bounds or a highly asymmetric joint distribution may be poorly represented by the normal model even if its first two moments can be computed. The model's elliptical contours, linear conditional means and constant conditional covariance are substantive shape assumptions to check against the application. Also distinguish independent sample *vectors* from dependent coordinates within each vector.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §§1.1, 1.3; Ch. 3, §3.1) {{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 6 Extra, Thms. 26, 31)

## Connections
At $p=1$, this is the ordinary [normal distribution](normal-distribution.qmd). A diagonal covariance makes its coordinates independent. Its quadratic distance is tied to the [chi-squared distribution](chi-squared-distribution.qmd), and the conditional formula is a concrete instance of [conditional distribution](conditional-distribution.qmd). The covariance eigenvectors connect its density contours to [principal component directions](principal-component-directions.qmd), while affine closure explains normal linear predictors and contrasts. These connections are useful because each turns a complicated joint calculation into a simpler normal or chi-squared one under the stated model.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §1.3; Ch. 3, §3.1) {{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 6 Extra, Thms. 24, 26, 31)

## History
Galton's 1885 presidential address to the Anthropological Institute is described by Stigler as introducing multivariate analysis, an early step toward treating related measurements together. That historical claim concerns multivariate analysis broadly; it does not credit Galton alone with the modern multivariate normal density.{{cite:stigler2010_galton_multivariate}} (Summary, p. 469)

The covariance-matrix formulation used today makes the joint model, its ellipsoidal geometry and affine transformations precise. It lets the older question of how measurements co-vary be expressed as a probability distribution for the complete observation vector.{{cite:strimmer2025_mva}} (Ch. 1, §§1.1, 1.3; Ch. 3, §3.1)
