## Motivating example
A service desk receives calls at a steady average rate of three per hour. If calls arrive independently and that rate does not change during the hour, the time until the next call has an exponential distribution. The model turns a rate into an answer about waiting: a short wait is more likely than a long one, yet there is no fixed deadline after which a call must arrive. The same calculation can describe the time until a component fails, provided its failure rate stays constant with age.{{cite:siegrist2022_exponential}} (§14.2, Poisson arrivals and waiting times){{cite:nist_exponential_reliability}} (§8.1.6.1, reliability and failure rate)

## Intuition
Write the rate as $\lambda$ in events per unit time. The survivor function $S(t)=\Pr(X>t)=e^{-\lambda t}$ says that each additional unit of waiting multiplies the chance of still waiting by $e^{-\lambda}$. This multiplicative structure is the source of memorylessness: once a wait has lasted $s$, the chance it lasts at least another $t$ is still $S(t)$. Equivalently, the instantaneous event rate conditional on no event so far, $h(t)=f(t)/S(t)$, always equals $\lambda$. A larger rate contracts the whole time scale, so both the mean and median fall; the right skew remains.{{cite:siegrist2022_exponential}} (§14.2, distribution and memoryless property){{cite:nist_exponential_reliability}} (§8.1.6.1, failure rate)

## Definition
The infobox uses the **rate** convention, $X\sim\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)$ with $\lambda>0$. A scale parameter $b$ in another source means $b=1/\lambda$; for example, a mean lifetime of $1000$ hours corresponds to rate $0.001$ per hour. The density is zero on negative times, while its value at zero can be taken as $\lambda$ without changing any probability. The CDF is $F(t)=1-e^{-\lambda t}$ for $t\ge0$, and the probability of an interval $(a,b]$ is $F(b)-F(a)=e^{-\lambda a}-e^{-\lambda b}$. The quantile $Q(u)=-\log(1-u)/\lambda$ converts a desired cumulative probability into a waiting time.{{cite:siegrist2022_exponential}} (§14.2, distribution function and quantiles){{cite:nist_exponential_reliability}} (§8.1.6.1, probability density and reliability)

## Properties
The mean $1/\lambda$ is a time, while the variance $1/\lambda^2$ is in squared time units. The median $(\log2)/\lambda$ is shorter than the mean because occasional long waits pull the mean to the right. The mode is the boundary value zero: density is highest immediately after observation begins, but $\Pr(X=0)=0$. Skewness $2$ and excess kurtosis $6$ do not depend on the rate, because changing $\lambda$ only rescales the time axis. The MGF $\lambda/(\lambda-t)$ exists for $t<\lambda$; replacing $t$ by $it$ yields the characteristic function in the infobox. Differential entropy $1-\log\lambda$ depends on the time unit, so its numerical value should not be interpreted without specifying that unit.{{cite:siegrist2022_exponential}} (§14.2, moments and transformations){{cite:nist_exponential_handbook}} (§1.3.6.6.7, summary of distributional properties)

The sum of independent exponential waits with a common rate has a Gamma law: if there are $k$ stages, its shape is $k$ and its rate remains $\lambda$. By contrast, the *minimum* of independent exponential waits with rates $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_k$ is exponential with rate $\sum_i\lambda_i$. A series system fails when its first component fails, which explains the minimum rule; a parallel system survives until its last component fails, and its maximum is generally not exponential. These relationships explain why the five-component example below has mean lifetime $200$ hours when each component has mean $1000$ hours.{{cite:siegrist2022_exponential}} (§14.2, minimum of independent exponentials){{cite:siegrist2022_gamma_waiting}} (§14.3, sums of exponential waiting times)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
For $t\ge0$, integration of $f(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$ gives $F(t)=1-e^{-\lambda t}$ and $S(t)=e^{-\lambda t}$; taking $t\to\infty$ shows the density integrates to one. Thus $S(s+t)/S(s)=e^{-\lambda t}$ and $f(t)/S(t)=\lambda$. Conversely, if a positive, continuous survivor function satisfies $S(s+t)=S(s)S(t)$ and $S(0)=1$, then $g(t)=-\log S(t)$ is continuous and additive on nonnegative times. Hence $g(t)=\lambda t$ for some $\lambda\ge0$; a proper finite waiting-time law has $\lambda>0$. For $t<\lambda$, $M(t)=\int_0^\infty e^{tx}\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\,dx=\lambda/(\lambda-t)$. Differentiation gives $\mathbb EX=1/\lambda$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X)=1/\lambda^2$. Setting $F(m)=1/2$ gives $m=(\log2)/\lambda$, and solving $F(Q(u))=u$ gives the quantile formula. For independent $X_i$, $\Pr(\min_iX_i>t)=\prod_i e^{-\lambda_i t}=e^{-t\sum_i\lambda_i}$, proving the minimum rule.{{cite:siegrist2022_exponential}} (§14.2, distribution, memorylessness, moments and minima)
:::
:::

## Related distributions
The exponential is the shape-one Gamma distribution. Waiting for the third event of a homogeneous Poisson process has Gamma shape three; counting how many events occur in a fixed interval has a Poisson distribution. The geometric law is the discrete waiting-time analogue: both have a memoryless property, but one measures a continuous duration and the other counts trials. A Weibull distribution with shape one is exponential; other Weibull shapes allow failure rate to rise or fall with age. If a Pareto variable $Y$ has survival $(x_m/y)^\alpha$ for $y\ge x_m$, then $\log(Y/x_m)$ is exponential with rate $\alpha$, showing how a logarithm converts a power tail into an exponential one.{{cite:siegrist2022_exponential}} (§14.2, related laws and transformations){{cite:nist_exponential_reliability}} (§8.1.6.1, Weibull special case)

## Statistical inference
For independent uncensored waits $x_1,\ldots,x_n$, the log likelihood is $n\log\lambda-\lambda\sum_i x_i$, so the maximum likelihood rate is $\widehat\lambda=n/\sum_i x_i=1/\bar x$. This is a rate per unit exposure, not a mean duration. If a component is still running at the end of observation, treating that censored exposure as a completed failure would inflate the estimated rate; its likelihood contribution is the survivor probability instead. With $d$ completed failures and total time at risk $T$, the simple constant-hazard estimate becomes $d/T$. A Gamma prior on the rate updates through the failure count and exposure, as the Bayesian example illustrates.{{cite:nist_exponential_handbook}} (§1.3.6.6.7, estimation){{cite:nist_exponential_reliability}} (§8.1.6.1, failure and reliability functions)

## Generating random samples
If $U$ is uniform on $(0,1)$, then $X=-\log(1-U)/\lambda$ has CDF $1-e^{-\lambda x}$. Since $1-U$ is also uniform, the sampling formula $-\log U/\lambda$ generates the same law. Software should avoid the exact endpoint $U=0$ in that second form because $-\log0$ is infinite. If a simulation's mean time is near $1/\lambda$ and its observed survivor proportions follow $e^{-\lambda t}$ at several times, those are useful checks on rate and unit conventions.{{cite:siegrist2022_exponential}} (§14.2, quantile and simulation)

## Worked example
At three calls per hour, the chance of at least one call within twenty minutes is $1-e^{-3(1/3)}=1-e^{-1}\approx0.63212$. The mean wait is $1/3$ hour, or $20$ minutes, while the median is $(\log2)/3$ hour, about $13.86$ minutes. If no call has come after thirty minutes, the chance of still waiting another ten minutes is $e^{-3(1/6)}=e^{-1/2}\approx0.60653$. Elapsed time changes the chance of having waited *this long*, but it does not change the distribution of the remaining wait under this model.{{cite:siegrist2022_exponential}} (§14.2, distribution and memorylessness)

**Your turn.** A machine has exponential failure time at rate $4$ per hour. Find its chance of lasting more than thirty minutes and, given that it has already lasted thirty minutes, its chance of lasting another fifteen minutes.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
Thirty minutes is half an hour, so $\Pr(X>1/2)=e^{-4(1/2)}=e^{-2}\approx0.13534$. The conditional chance of another quarter-hour is $\Pr(X>1/4)=e^{-4(1/4)}=e^{-1}\approx0.36788$. The second answer depends on the length of the *additional* interval, not on the machine's current age.{{cite:siegrist2022_exponential}} (§14.2, memoryless property)
:::

## Common misconceptions
The rate $\lambda$ is not the mean wait: at rate $4$ per hour, the mean is a quarter-hour. Memorylessness does not say an old component has lived for less time than a new one; it compares their *remaining* lives conditional on survival. A constant hazard does not make the CDF linear: $F(t)=1-e^{-\lambda t}$ curves toward one. An exponential density also does not assign positive probability to an exact observed time, since a continuous random variable has zero mass at any one point. Finally, a large mean need not indicate a large rate; mean and rate move in opposite directions.{{cite:siegrist2022_exponential}} (§14.2, rate, distribution and memorylessness)

## Assumptions and limits — When the model fails
An ageing light bulb whose failure risk increases after prolonged use cannot have an exponential lifetime with one constant $\lambda$; a Weibull model with shape above one allows rising hazard. Calls during a busy shift may arrive faster at noon than at dawn, so a single-rate Poisson waiting law is inappropriate across that period. Two components damaged by the same power surge are dependent, invalidating the independent-minimum calculation for a series system. A deterministic replacement after exactly one year puts a point mass at that deadline, which no continuous exponential law can represent. Such failures concern hazard shape, time variation, dependence and discrete replacement separately.{{cite:nist_exponential_reliability}} (§8.1.6.1, constant failure-rate assumption){{cite:siegrist2022_exponential}} (§14.2, Poisson-process conditions)

## History
Erlang's 1909 paper *The Theory of Probabilities and Telephone Conversations* grew from telephone-traffic congestion, the earliest problem studied in queueing theory. His later work connected Poisson call arrivals and exponential holding times to multichannel telephone systems.{{cite:shortle2018_queueing}} (Ch. 1, p. 2) Dodge's encyclopedia presents the exponential law alongside its constant-rate waiting-time interpretation and its Gamma and geometric relatives.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 194, “Exponential Distribution”) The use of a constant hazard in reliability analysis gives the same law a second practical reading: the age of a surviving component does not alter its instantaneous failure rate.{{cite:nist_exponential_reliability}} (§8.1.6.1, failure rate)
