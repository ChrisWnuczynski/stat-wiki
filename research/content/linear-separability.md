## Motivating example
A threshold detector sounds when a weighted combination of two measurements exceeds a cutoff. Adjusting its weights rotates the boundary; adjusting its bias moves it. Some label patterns can be captured this way, while XOR requires an additional feature or a richer model. Linear separability asks about the existence of a perfect affine separator for the given observations, before asking how one should estimate a useful predictor from them.{{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§6.1, Learning XOR)

## Definition — Strict separation and the margin
For a finite labelled sample $(x_i,y_i)$ with $y_i\in\{-1,+1\}$, strict affine separability means that some $w,b$ satisfy
$$y_i(w^\top x_i+b)>0\quad\text{for every }i.$$
A finite strictly separable sample has a positive minimum functional margin $m=\min_i y_i(w^\top x_i+b)$. Dividing $(w,b)$ by $m$ gives the equivalent canonical constraints $y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\ge1$. If $w\ne0$, the minimum geometric margin is $m/\|w\|$. Multiplying both $w$ and $b$ by the same positive constant changes the functional margin but leaves this distance unchanged.{{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§5.7.2, support vector machines)

If a point lies exactly on the boundary, its score is zero and strict separation fails for that choice of weights. A weak separation with zero margins therefore differs from a strict separation. When both labels occur, a strict separator must have nonzero $w$. The labels, coordinate transformation and treatment of the intercept are all part of the question.{{cite:zhang_d2l2023}} (§5.1, multilayer perceptrons and limitations of linear models)

## Properties — Convex combinations expose an obstruction
If a strict separator exists, the convex hulls of the two classes cannot intersect. Every convex combination of positive points has positive score, because an affine function preserves convex combinations. Every convex combination of negative points has negative score. A point in both hulls would have to have both signs, a contradiction.

For XOR on $\{0,1\}^2$, the midpoint of the two positive points $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ is $(1/2,1/2)$. It is also the midpoint of the negative points $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$. The convex hulls intersect, proving that no affine separator works. By contrast, adding the feature $x_1x_2$ gives the score $x_1+x_2-2x_1x_2-1/2$, positive exactly for the two XOR successes. The difficulty lies in the representation, rather than in a failure to search enough candidate lines.{{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§6.1){{cite:zhang_d2l2023}} (§5.1.1, limitations of linear models)

## Worked example — A biased threshold and its distances
On the four binary inputs, the AND labels are positive only at $(1,1)$. Choose $w=(1,1)$ and $b=-3/2$. The scores at $(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)$ are $-3/2,-1/2,-1/2,1/2$, so all labels are correct. The minimum functional margin is $1/2$ and the minimum geometric margin is $1/(2\sqrt2)\approx0.353553$. Doubling the coefficients gives canonical margin one without moving the boundary $x_1+x_2=3/2$.

With no bias, the point $(0,0)$ always has zero score. The same strict-separation problem therefore has no homogeneous solution. Appending a constant coordinate $1$ and giving it coefficient $b$ restores the affine model, though regularizing that coefficient can change the optimization problem.{{cite:zhang_d2l2023}} (§5.1, linear models and hidden representations)

## Statistical use — Separability is a property of a finite labelled set
The definition quantifies over the observed pairs $(x_i,y_i)$. It says nothing about a point that was not in the sample. A separator with geometric margin $0.353553$ on the AND corners can still label a new point near the boundary either way, and a small shift of one training point can destroy the strict inequalities. When both classes occur, a strict separator has $w\ne0$, because a constant score cannot be positive on one label and negative on the other. The canonical rescaling divides $(w,b)$ by the minimum functional margin and produces constraints $y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\ge1$ with the same decision boundary. For the AND weights $(1,1)$ and bias $-3/2$, the functional margin is $1/2$, so the canonical weights are $(2,2)$ and the canonical bias is $-3$. The geometric margin stays $1/(2\sqrt2)$.{{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§5.7.2, support vector machines)

The margin geometry is Euclidean in the coordinates that were supplied. For $w=(3,4)$ and a minimum functional margin of $2$, the norm is $5$ and the geometric margin is $2/5=0.4$. Multiplying $(w,b)$ by $10$ multiplies the functional margin by $10$ and leaves $0.4$ unchanged, because the norm grows by the same factor. A feature map that appends $x_1x_2$ changes the coordinates, and the Euclidean margin in the new coordinates is a different number from the margin in the original plane. XOR is not strictly separable in the original plane and is strictly separable after that one product is added. The existence question must name the coordinates.{{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§6.1){{cite:zhang_d2l2023}} (§5.1.1, limitations of linear models)

## Common misconceptions
Intersecting convex hulls are an obstruction to every affine separator, not merely to a particular algorithm. The XOR midpoint $(1/2,1/2)$ is a convex combination of the positive corners and of the negative corners, so no choice of $(w,b)$ can give those four points strict margins. Searching more random initial weights cannot remove a hull intersection. Adding a coordinate can, because the hulls are then computed in a larger space. Identical input vectors with opposite labels are the simplest obstruction: the same affine score would have to be both positive and negative. Duplicate points with the same label do not create that contradiction.{{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§6.1, Learning XOR)

A zero score is a different failure from a small positive score. Strict separation requires $y_i(w^\top x_i+b)>0$ at every $i$. A point that lands on the boundary has score zero, and the strict definition excludes that $(w,b)$. Weak separation allows zeros. The canonical margin constraints use $\ge1$ after rescaling a strictly separable sample, so they describe the same strict problem once a positive minimum margin exists. A sample that is only weakly separable has minimum functional margin zero, and division by that minimum is undefined. The AND example without a bias puts $(0,0)$ on the boundary for every $w$, which is why the homogeneous problem fails while the affine problem succeeds.{{cite:zhang_d2l2023}} (§5.1, linear models and hidden representations)

Strict training separation also changes a logistic fit. If a finite $(w,b)$ separates the sample strictly, then $c w$ and $c b$ for large positive $c$ push every fitted probability toward the observed label, and the Bernoulli likelihood increases toward its supremum without a finite maximizer. A quadratic or absolute penalty on the coefficients charges for that growth and can restore a finite optimum. The penalty changes the criterion. It does not change whether the unpenalized labels were separable. Separability is a geometric fact about the sample. Generalization is a fact about points the sample did not include, and a high-dimensional sample can be separable because there are many directions, even when the label distributions overlap.{{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§§12.4–12.6, training error and regularization){{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§5.7.2, support vector machines)

## Assumptions and limits
A perfectly separated training sample need not predict future labels well. Extra dimensions can make a small finite sample separable even when the population labels overlap. Validation measures generalization; separability describes the observed geometry. Under logistic likelihood, strict separation can also make coefficient magnitudes diverge: scaling a separator upward keeps improving each observation's likelihood toward one. A finite penalty changes that behavior by charging for large coefficients. The hull test assumes the affine score is the function being tested. A classifier that uses a nonlinear feature map is tested for separability after the map, in the coordinates $\phi(x)$. Applying the hull test in the original coordinates and concluding that no classifier of any kind exists confuses those two questions.{{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§§12.4–12.6, training error and regularization){{cite:zhang_d2l2023}} (§5.1.1, limitations of linear models)
