## Lead context
A study that cannot be repeated from its tables has stopped at the authors' desks. Data sharing is the practice of preserving the recorded observations, and the information needed to use them, so that another analyst can check the result or ask a new question. The practice has two layers that are easy to confuse: a policy that says what must be shared, and a set of properties that say what a shared holding has to make possible.{{cite:wilkinson2016}} (p. 4) {{cite:nih_dms2020}} (NOT-OD-21-013, §II)

## Definition
Scientific data, in the sense used by the U.S. National Institutes of Health in notice NOT-OD-21-013, are the recorded factual material commonly accepted in the scientific community as of sufficient quality to validate and replicate research findings, whether or not the data are used to support a publication. Laboratory notebooks, preliminary analyses, completed case report forms, drafts of papers, plans for future research, peer reviews, communications with colleagues, and physical objects such as laboratory specimens are outside that definition. The policy expects sharing of scientific data to be maximised, and it expects limitations to be justified rather than left implicit. It applies to research funded or conducted by NIH that generates scientific data.{{cite:nih_dms2020}} (NOT-OD-21-013, §II)

The FAIR guiding principles describe what a shared holding should make possible for a person and for a machine. Findable means a globally unique persistent identifier, rich metadata that include that identifier, and registration in a searchable resource. Accessible means the identifier can be resolved by a standardised protocol that is open, free, and universally implementable, with authentication and authorisation where they are needed, and that the metadata remain available even if the data themselves are gone. Interoperable means a formal, shared language for knowledge representation, vocabularies that are themselves FAIR, and qualified references to other data. Reusable means a plurality of accurate and relevant attributes, a clear and accessible licence, detailed provenance, and the standards of the relevant community. Wilkinson and co-authors publish these as Box 2 of the 2016 comment in *Scientific Data*.{{cite:wilkinson2016}} (p. 4, Box 2)

The two documents answer different questions. The NIH notice says which recorded material counts as scientific data and that sharing is the expectation. The FAIR comment says which properties a holding should have if a stranger, or a program, is to find it and use it. A file that meets the NIH definition and is locked in a drawer has been defined and has not been shared. A file that is posted without an identifier, a licence, or a description of how it was made is shared in the weak sense and fails the principles.{{cite:nih_dms2020}} (NOT-OD-21-013, §II) {{cite:wilkinson2016}} (p. 4)

## Statistical use
A second analyst who wants a [confidence interval](confidence-interval.qmd) or a refit by [least squares](least-squares.qmd) needs the observations, the definitions of the variables, and the rules by which cases were included. Metadata that name the identifier of the data, and provenance that records how the file was produced, are what make that recalculation possible. The reusable principles require the licence and the provenance explicitly. Without them the second analyst cannot tell whether the refit is permitted or whether the file is the one the paper describes.{{cite:wilkinson2016}} (p. 4, Box 2, R1.1–R1.2)

Workflows are shareable objects in the same sense. An analysis script that cannot be found and licensed cannot be rerun. Wilkinson and co-authors emphasise machines as well as scholars: a computational agent should be able to tell what an unfamiliar object is and what the licence allows. They distinguish a bespoke parser, written for one file format, from actionability that uses general-purpose technology. The second is the target, because new data types arrive faster than parsers for every language and every tool. For a statistical refit, the practical version of that target is a file whose columns are named in a shared vocabulary and whose construction is written down.{{cite:wilkinson2016}} (p. 4)

Sensitive records are not an exception that cancels the principles. The comment says the principles are separable and may be met incrementally. Rich metadata, including the process for obtaining access, can make a holding findable and reusable while the microdata stay behind an authentication step. That is the clause A1.2, authentication and authorisation where necessary, together with the example of personally identifiable data: publication of rich metadata, and clear rules for access, provides a high degree of FAIRness even when the data themselves are not published.{{cite:wilkinson2016}} (p. 4, Box 2 and the following paragraphs)

That split is the same problem addressed by [k-anonymity](k-anonymity.qmd) and by [differential privacy](differential-privacy.qmd) when a public file is released instead of a controlled one. The statistical summaries can travel further than the rows. The NIH notice records the public concern about participant privacy and confidentiality, and it keeps the expectation that sharing be maximised with justified limits. The timing in the notice is an outer bound: shared scientific data should be accessible as soon as possible, and no later than the time of an associated publication or the end of the award, whichever comes first. A paper can trigger release of the data that underlie it. Research that never produces a paper is not, under that wording, exempt.{{cite:nih_dms2020}} (NOT-OD-21-013) {{cite:wilkinson2016}} (p. 4)

A confidence interval computed from a private worksheet and a confidence interval computed from a described file are not the same scientific object, even when the numbers match. The second can be checked. The first can only be believed. The statistical use of sharing is that check, and the chance to ask a question the original analysts did not ask, on the same rows, with the same definitions.{{cite:wilkinson2016}} (p. 4, R1)

## Common misconceptions
Sharing is not the act of attaching a spreadsheet to a message. The principles require an identifier, metadata that include the identifier, a searchable registration, a protocol, a licence, and provenance. A file that has none of these can still be "shared" in ordinary speech. It is not findable or reusable in the sense Box 2 defines. Meeting one principle does not meet the others. Wilkinson and co-authors say the elements are related but independent, and that a provider can move along the list incrementally. Incremental is not the same as optional once a funder has required a plan.{{cite:wilkinson2016}} (p. 4)

The NIH definition does not treat every note in a laboratory as scientific data. Notebooks, drafts, peer reviews, and specimens are excluded. A demand to release those objects is a different demand from the one the notice states. Conversely, data that were not used in a paper are still inside the definition if they are recorded factual material of sufficient quality to validate and replicate findings. Absence from the publication is not absence from the policy.{{cite:nih_dms2020}} (NOT-OD-21-013, §II)

Privacy is not a reason the principles fail to apply. It is a reason the accessible principle includes an authorisation step, and a reason the NIH notice allows justified limitations. A public release of identifiable rows is not what either document asks for. A refusal to describe the data, or to say how a qualified researcher obtains them, is a failure of findability, not a protection of subjects.{{cite:wilkinson2016}} (p. 4) {{cite:nih_dms2020}} (NOT-OD-21-013)

Posting data at the end of a project, with no record of how the columns were defined, does not make a later refit possible. Provenance is one of the reusable clauses. A second analyst who cannot tell which rows were dropped, which units were used, or which version of the file was analysed is not in a position to recompute a standard error. The statistical mistake is to treat availability and identifiability as the same property.{{cite:wilkinson2016}} (p. 4, Box 2, R1.2)

## Connections
[Data science](data-science.qmd) depends on holdings that a second project can find and recompute. Cleveland's plan for the field gives a share of effort to tool evaluation and to computing with data. A tool that cannot be pointed at a described file cannot be evaluated, and a computation that cannot be repeated is not yet computing in the sense a second analyst needs.{{cite:wilkinson2016}} (p. 4) [Biostatistics](biostatistics.qmd) and [epidemiology](epidemiology.qmd) are frequent sources of the human data for which access rules and metadata have to travel together. The NIH notice is written for that setting: participant privacy, consent, and the expectation that volunteers' data are used as far as the justification allows.{{cite:nih_dms2020}} (NOT-OD-21-013)

Official statistics face the same split between a public table and a restricted microdata file. The FAIR example of sensitive data is the general form of that split. The statistical point is unchanged across those settings. A result that cannot be recomputed from a described file is a claim about a private worksheet. The principles and the notice are two ways of saying what would have to be true for the claim to be about a public, or a controlled, object instead.{{cite:wilkinson2016}} (p. 4) {{cite:nih_dms2020}} (NOT-OD-21-013, §II)

## History
Funders, publishers, and agencies had already begun to require data-management plans when a group of stakeholders from academia, industry, funders, and publishers wrote down the FAIR principles. A 2014 workshop in Leiden drafted them. A FORCE11 working group revised them. The 2016 comment in *Scientific Data* is the first formal publication, and it includes the rationale and Box 2. The principles were written for machines as well as for scholars.{{cite:wilkinson2016}} (p. 4)

NIH's final policy was issued as notice NOT-OD-21-013, with a release date of 29 October 2020 and an effective date of 25 January 2023. The notice defines scientific data, requires a data-management and sharing plan, and sets the outer bound on when shared data are to be accessible. It points to the FAIR principles when it discusses findability, including persistent identifiers as a way to make data findable. The notice is the administrative form of the expectation. The comment is the list of properties a holding is asked to have.{{cite:nih_dms2020}} (NOT-OD-21-013) {{cite:wilkinson2016}} (p. 4)
