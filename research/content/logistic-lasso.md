## Motivating example
A diagnostic study measures many genes on each patient but has far fewer patients than candidate coefficients. An unrestricted logistic fit can be unstable or separated. The logistic lasso charges for coefficient magnitude while fitting binary outcomes, allowing some coefficients to be exactly zero. Prediction, gene prioritization and causal explanation remain different goals even when the same fitted vector is useful for all three discussions.{{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§12.6.1, Penalization)

## Definition — Binary likelihood with an absolute penalty
For independent responses $y_i\in\{0,1\}$, let $\eta_i=\beta_0+x_i^\top\beta$ and $p_i=(1+e^{-\eta_i})^{-1}$. One common normalization minimizes
$$F(\beta_0,\beta)=\frac1n\sum_i\{\log(1+e^{\eta_i})-y_i\eta_i\}+\lambda\sum_j|\beta_j|,$$
where $\lambda\ge0$ and the intercept is unpenalized. The first term is average negative binomial log-likelihood, apart from constants. Reporting the normalization matters: multiplying the likelihood term by $n$ also requires multiplying the penalty by $n$ to obtain the same fit.{{cite:friedman_glmnet2010}} (§3, Logistic Regression)

## Properties — Why zero coefficients are possible
Write $g_j=n^{-1}\sum_i x_{ij}(p_i-y_i)$ for the derivative of the smooth loss. The optimality conditions are
$$g_j=-\lambda\operatorname{sign}(\beta_j)\quad(\beta_j\ne0),\qquad |g_j|\le\lambda\quad(\beta_j=0).$$
At zero, the absolute value has every subgradient in $[-1,1]$; a range of likelihood gradients can therefore be balanced without moving the coefficient away from zero. The intercept condition is $\sum_i(p_i-y_i)=0$ at a finite optimum. The Hessian of the smooth term is $X^\top W X/n$, with diagonal weights $p_i(1-p_i)\ge0$, proving convexity. The absolute penalty is also convex, so these conditions characterize global minima. Coefficients may be nonunique with dependent columns even when fitted linear predictors are unique.{{cite:friedman_glmnet2010}} (§3, quadratic approximation and coordinate descent)

With both outcome classes present, the intercept-only optimum is $\beta_0=\operatorname{logit}(\overline y)$. At that fit, all slopes can stay zero whenever
$$\lambda\ge\lambda_{\max}=\max_j\left|n^{-1}\sum_i x_{ij}(y_i-\overline y)\right|.$$
This follows immediately from the zero-coordinate conditions. A coefficient's size depends on predictor units, so standardization and the intercept convention belong in any reproducible specification.{{cite:friedman_glmnet2010}} (§§2–3, standardization and regularization paths)

## Worked example — A complete one-predictor path
Take two observations $(x,y)=(-1,0),(1,1)$. Symmetry gives intercept zero. For a nonnegative slope $\beta$, the objective is $\log(1+e^{-\beta})+\lambda\beta$. Its derivative is $-1/(1+e^\beta)+\lambda$. Thus the solution is zero when $\lambda\ge1/2$ and $\widehat\beta=\log\{(1-\lambda)/\lambda\}$ for $0<\lambda<1/2$. At $\lambda=1/4$, $\widehat\beta=\log3\approx1.098612$ and the fitted probabilities are $1/4$ and $3/4$. At $\lambda=0$, the likelihood supremum is approached as the slope tends to infinity, illustrating separation and the stabilizing effect of a positive penalty.

## Statistical use — Selection is part of the fitting procedure
Cross-validation chooses a penalty for a specified prediction objective, such as binomial deviance. Feature filtering and standardization must be learned within each training split. If many penalty values or methods are compared, a separate test sample or nested validation is needed for an honest final assessment. A selected coefficient is evidence that the variable helps that fitted predictor under those choices; a zero does not establish absence of a biological association, especially when genes are strongly correlated.{{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§§12.4, 12.6.2)

The fourteen-gene coefficient table describes one specified high-dimensional fit. Its counts, overlaps and coefficient comparisons can be checked directly; reproducing its selected penalty requires the exact preprocessing, folds and tuning choices. Those details determine which of correlated genes enters. Fitting principal components instead changes the scientific target from individual gene coefficients to combinations of genes.{{cite:friedman_glmnet2010}} (§2, correlated predictors){{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§12.6.1)

Hold the other fitted predictors fixed and increase one coordinate by one unit in the units that were penalized. The linear predictor increases by that coefficient, and the odds of the binary outcome are multiplied by $e$ to the power of the coefficient. For the coefficient $-0.588$, the multiplier is $e^{-0.588}\approx0.555437$. This is a conditional odds factor on the scale of the standardized or otherwise specified predictor. It is not a risk difference, and it is not a causal effect of changing that gene while the biological system adjusts the other genes. Exponentiating a coefficient whose penalty set it to zero gives a multiplier of one inside that fitted model, which restates the zero rather than proving the gene is unrelated to the outcome.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 4, §4.3.1, pp. 132–133){{cite:friedman_glmnet2010}} (§3, Logistic Regression)

## Interpretation — A one-predictor path shows the corner at zero
The two-point sample $(-1,0)$ and $(1,1)$ has an intercept of zero by symmetry. For a slope $\beta\ge0$ the penalized objective is $\log(1+e^{-\beta})+\lambda\beta$, with derivative $-1/(1+e^\beta)+\lambda$. The derivative at $\beta=0$ is $-1/2+\lambda$. When $\lambda\ge1/2$ the derivative at zero is nonnegative and the slope stays at zero, where both fitted probabilities are $1/2$. When $0<\lambda<1/2$ the root is $\widehat\beta=\log\{(1-\lambda)/\lambda\}$. At $\lambda=1/4$ the slope is $\log3\approx1.098612$ and the probabilities are $1/4$ and $3/4$. At $\lambda=0.1$ the slope is $\log9\approx2.197225$ and the probabilities are $0.1$ and $0.9$. At $\lambda=0$ the observations are separated and the slope that maximises the likelihood escapes to infinity. The positive penalty stops that escape. The numerical value of $\lambda$ belongs to this normalization, with the loss averaged over $n=2$ and the penalty written once.{{cite:friedman_glmnet2010}} (§3, Logistic Regression){{cite:tibshirani1996}} (§2.2, eqs. 3–4, pp. 269–270)

The same corner condition is the subgradient condition in several coordinates. A coefficient that is zero is optimal when the absolute value of its smooth gradient is at most $\lambda$. A gradient of $0.03$ at a zero coefficient with $\lambda=0.05$ satisfies the condition, because a subgradient of $-0.03/0.05$ lies in $[-1,1]$. A gradient of $0.08$ at the same $\lambda$ does not. Every coordinate, and the unpenalized intercept, has to satisfy its own condition before the vector is a minimizer. Dependent gene columns can leave the minimizer nonunique even though the fitted probabilities are unique, because two coefficient vectors can produce the same linear predictor.{{cite:friedman_glmnet2010}} (§3, quadratic approximation and coordinate descent){{cite:tibshirani1996}} (§2.1, pp. 268–269)

## Common misconceptions
The lasso penalty was introduced for a least-squares loss, where an orthonormal design reduces the fit to soft thresholding of each ordinary coefficient. Logistic loss replaces that squared error by the binomial deviance. The absolute penalty still creates a corner at zero, and coordinate descent still thresholds a partial residual, but the partial residual is the gradient of the logistic loss rather than a linear least-squares residual. Carrying a squared-error soft-threshold formula over to binary outcomes without that gradient uses the wrong smooth loss. The path in the two-point example is the logistic path: the threshold $\lambda=1/2$ is the logistic gradient at the intercept-only fit, not a residual from a linear regression of the labels.{{cite:tibshirani1996}} (§2.2, eqs. 3–4, pp. 269–270){{cite:friedman_glmnet2010}} (§§2–3, standardization and regularization paths)

A selected gene is a gene with a nonzero coefficient in one penalized fit. Eleven of the fourteen displayed coefficients also sit on a false-discovery list, and two sit on a third-component list. Those overlaps are counts of names in specified lists. They are not a second theorem that transfers an error rate onto the lasso selection. The preprocessing, the folds, and the chosen $\lambda$ determine which of a correlated block is the one that receives the nonzero coefficient. A laboratory follow-up list can prefer the overlap and drop a tiny coefficient such as the one on gene $249$. That preference is a ranking rule. It inherits an error guarantee only under a guarantee that was proved for that rule.{{cite:friedman_glmnet2010}} (§2, correlated predictors){{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§§12.4, 12.6.2)

Cross-validation of binomial deviance estimates the prediction loss of a procedure that includes the penalty choice. If the genes were screened on the full sample before the folds were cut, the held-out deviance describes a screen that already saw those held-out labels. Standardizing inside each training fold, and fitting the penalty path inside that fold, puts the selection on the same side of the split as the coefficients. A final number quoted after many such comparisons needs a split that was not used for any of them.{{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§§12.4, 12.6.2){{cite:cawley_talbot2010}} (§5.3, p. 2102)
