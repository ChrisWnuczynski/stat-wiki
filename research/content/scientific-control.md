## Lead context

A treatment contrast is readable when the groups differ in the treatment and are alike in everything else the investigator can hold still. Scientific control is that discipline: assign the treatment, pin down the other conditions, and keep a comparison that did not receive the experimental treatment. Studies in which the researchers assign treatments are experiments. When the assignment uses a random mechanism, the experiment is randomised, and a causal reading of the contrast is the reason for that design.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.4, §1.4.1){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "control group")

## Motivating example

Patients take a study pill with anything from a sip of water to a full glass. If the amount of water affects the outcome, the drug is tangled with the drink. Asking every patient to take the pill with a 12-ounce glass removes water as a difference between groups. That instruction is a control on a condition. It is not itself the control group. The control group is the set of observations that do not receive the experimental treatment, kept so that the treated outcomes have something to be compared with.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.4.1){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "control group")

## Intuition

An experiment is a programme for which conditions will vary and which will be held fixed. The factors that are meant to vary are set to chosen levels. The conditions that are not under study are kept common, so that a later difference has fewer alternative explanations. Random assignment covers the conditions that cannot be held fixed, because it balances them in expectation rather than by an instruction. Dietary habits that make one patient more susceptible than another are the sort of difference an instruction cannot name and a random split can balance.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 162){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.4.1)

## Definition

Scientific control is the practice of assigning treatments and of controlling other differences among the units, together with the maintenance of a comparison set that does not receive the experimental treatment. An experimental design states the levels of each factor, or of each combination of factors, that the units will receive. The aim is a conclusion the experiment can actually support, reached by an economical plan. The plans used most often are the completely randomised design, the randomised block design, and the Latin square.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.4.1){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "control group"){{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 162–163)

Four principles organise a randomised experiment: controlling other differences, randomising into groups, replicating so that the effect is estimated from enough cases, and blocking on a trait that is already known to matter. The first three belong in any such study. Blocking first splits units on a known trait, such as low and high risk of a heart attack, and then randomises within each block, so that each treatment arm receives the same mix of that trait. Control of a condition is an instruction that applies to every unit, such as the common glass of water. The control group is a subset of units that do not receive the experimental treatment. One is a shared protocol. The other is an arm of the comparison.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.4.1)

Randomisation is the assignment of units to treatments by a random mechanism, not by an arbitrary or systematic rule such as assigning the first twelve units to treatment A. Replication, in a single study, means a sample large enough to estimate the effect. A second study that repeats the first is replication of another kind. Different designs are different mathematical models. The analysis that belongs to a completely randomised plan is not the analysis that belongs to a Latin square. Variance analysis, covariance analysis, and regression are the analyses attached to those plans.{{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "randomization, scientific"){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.4.1){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 163)

## Statistical use

Scientific control is what makes a treated-minus-control contrast interpretable as a treatment effect rather than as a bundle of other differences. In agricultural and industrial experiments the design is chosen so that the planned comparison can be estimated and the unplanned differences are either held fixed or balanced. A prospective study can include a comparison group without the investigator controlling the exposure. That comparison is weaker, because the exposure was not assigned, and an observational association is not by itself a causal sentence. Conditions that are not measured and not randomised can still differ between groups. Control reduces the differences the protocol names. It does not name every cause. Human participants who know which arm they are in can still change their behaviour. Blinding is a further protection, not a consequence of having a control. The design's conclusion is only as wide as the levels that were actually used.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.4.1, §1.3.4){{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 162–163){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "control group")

## Worked example

Twelve patients all drink a 12-ounce glass of water with their pill, so water volume does not differ. Six are randomised to the drug and six to a placebo pill. The outcome changes are 8, 10, 6, 12, 9, and 9 on the drug, summing to 54, with mean \(54/6 = 9\). The placebo changes are 2, 4, 3, 3, 5, and 1, summing to 18, with mean \(18/6 = 3\). The contrast is \(9 - 3 = 6\). The common water volume is the controlled condition. The placebo arm is the control group.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.4.1){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "control group")

**Your turn.** The placebo changes gain one more patient with change 4, and the drug mean stays 9. What are the new placebo mean and the new contrast?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The placebo changes sum to 22 over 7 patients, so the mean is \(22/7\). The contrast is \(9 - 22/7\).
:::

## Common misconceptions

Scientific control is not a control group. Holding water intake fixed does not create an untreated arm, and an untreated arm does not by itself hold every other condition fixed. The footnote that separates the two uses of the word is the distinction the design depends on. A systematic assignment, first half treated and second half not, feels orderly and is not randomisation. Order in the list can line up with the outcome. A comparison group in a study that did not assign the exposure is a control in the weak sense of a contrast, and it is not scientific control of the treatment.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.4.1){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "randomization, scientific"){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "control group")
