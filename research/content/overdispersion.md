## Motivating example — Nights with the same mean can still be too variable
A clinic counts urgent calls each night. If the mean is two calls, a Poisson model assigns variance two as well, because a Poisson random variable has mean, variance and every cumulant equal to its mean.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.2.1, p. 195) Nights with no calls and nights with many calls may occur more often than that model expects, even after staffing and day of week have been included as predictors. The issue concerns variation *around a conditional mean*, not merely the fact that some observed counts are larger than others. If the missing source of variation is a changing call rate, treating every night as having the same Poisson rate can make the fitted mean look more precise than it is. A random exposure interval, or a cluster of events that each contribute a random amount, produces the same qualitative excess.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.2.3, p. 198)

## Definition — Variance above a stated Poisson benchmark
For a count response $Y_i$ with conditional mean $\mu_i$, the Poisson benchmark has $\operatorname{Var}(Y_i\mid\mathbf x_i)=\mu_i$. Overdispersion means that the conditional variance is greater than this benchmark, after the mean model and exposure have been specified. McCullagh and Nelder write the comparison as $\operatorname{var}(Y)>\mathbb{E}(Y)$.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.2.3, p. 198) It is a property relative to that benchmark: a raw sample variance greater than a raw sample mean is suggestive in an intercept-only analysis, but can reflect omitted changes in $\mu_i$ rather than a permanent variance feature. The corresponding case of variance below the benchmark is [underdispersion](underdispersion.qmd).

A common working description is $\operatorname{Var}(Y_i\mid\mathbf x_i)=\phi\mu_i$ with $\phi>0$, the form $\operatorname{var}(Y)=\sigma^2\mathbb{E}(Y)$. Relative to Poisson, $\phi>1$ describes overdispersion, $\phi<1$ describes underdispersion and $\phi=1$ restores the Poisson variance.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.1, pp. 193–194) The factor $\phi$ is a [dispersion parameter](dispersion-parameter.qmd), assumed common to the observations in this simple model. It multiplies variance, so it multiplies a model-based standard error by $\sqrt\phi$, not by $\phi$. The covariance of the quasi-likelihood estimator is the inverse of the Poisson information, multiplied by $\sigma^2$.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§9.2.3, eq. 9.6, p. 327)

## Properties — Why Poisson counts have equal mean and variance
The Poisson probability generating function is $G(z)=\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda}(\lambda z)^x/x!=e^{\lambda(z-1)}$. Consequently $G'(1)=\lambda$ and $G''(1)=\lambda^2$, so $E(Y)=\lambda$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=G''(1)+G'(1)-G'(1)^2=\lambda$. This equality follows from the *whole* Poisson distribution; it is not a universal identity for counts. The gamma–Poisson mixture whose variance is $\mu+\mu^2/k$ provides a count distribution with the same mean but greater variance.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.2.3, p. 199)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Differentiating $G(z)=e^{\lambda(z-1)}$ gives $G'(z)=\lambda G(z)$ and $G''(z)=\lambda^2G(z)$. At $z=1$, $G(1)=1$. The formulas for factorial moments therefore give $E(Y)=G'(1)=\lambda$ and $E\{Y(Y-1)\}=G''(1)=\lambda^2$, whence $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=\lambda^2+\lambda-\lambda^2=\lambda$. The same conclusion follows from the cumulant generating function $\mu(e^t-1)$, whose cumulants are all equal to $\mu$.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.2.1, p. 195)

For the mixture, let $Y\mid\Lambda\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\Lambda)$ and let $\Lambda$ have a gamma distribution with mean $\mu$ and variance $\mu^2/k$, where $k>0$. Total expectation yields $E(Y)=E\{E(Y\mid\Lambda)\}=E(\Lambda)=\mu$. Total variance yields $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=E\{\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid\Lambda)\}+\operatorname{Var}\{E(Y\mid\Lambda)\}=E(\Lambda)+\operatorname{Var}(\Lambda)=\mu+\mu^2/k>\mu$. The resulting marginal count distribution is negative binomial in the mean–size parametrization. McCullagh and Nelder obtain the same quadratic variance by mixing a Poisson mean with a gamma law of constant index.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.2.3, p. 199)

For a log-linear mean $\mu_i=\exp(\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta)$, differentiation gives $\partial\mu_i/\partial\boldsymbol\beta=\mu_i\mathbf x_i$. The quasi-score with variance $\phi\mu_i$ is therefore $\sum_i(y_i-\mu_i)(\mu_i\mathbf x_i)/(\phi\mu_i)=\phi^{-1}\sum_i\mathbf x_i(y_i-\mu_i)$, exactly $1/\phi$ times the Poisson score. When $\phi>0$ is common, the two estimating equations have the same roots. Scaling the coefficient covariance by $\phi$ scales each standard error by $\sqrt\phi$.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§9.2.3, eqs. 9.5–9.6, p. 327)
:::
:::

## Interpretation — Diagnosing the extra variation
For an intercept-only Poisson fit to $n$ independent counts, the fitted mean is their average. The Pearson statistic $X_P^2=\sum_i(y_i-\hat\mu_i)^2/\hat\mu_i$ compares squared residuals with the variance each fitted count was assigned. More generally, McCullagh and Nelder estimate the dispersion by $X^2/(n-p)$, with $X^2=\sum_i(y_i-\mu_i)^2/\mu_i$ evaluated at the fitted mean, when $p$ mean parameters were estimated and residual degrees of freedom are positive.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.2.4, eq. 6.4, p. 200) A value well above one prompts a check of the mean model, exposure, dependence and unusually influential counts. The ratio is a diagnostic estimate, not proof that a unique quasi-Poisson data-generating law exists. The assumed form $\operatorname{var}(Y)=\sigma^2\mu$ can and should itself be checked.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.2.3, p. 199)

The distinction matters because an unmodelled predictor can create apparently extra variation. Suppose two equally common groups each have Poisson counts, with means one and five. Within either group variance equals its mean, but without the group label the pooled mean is three and the pooled variance is $3+\{(1-3)^2+(5-3)^2\}/2=7$. The pooled variance-to-mean ratio is $7/3$, even though neither conditional distribution is overdispersed. A better mean model removes this particular source of excess variation.

## Statistical use — Choosing an account of the spread
A [quasi-Poisson](quasi-poisson.qmd) analysis keeps the log-linear mean $\mu_i=\exp(\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta)$ and specifies $\operatorname{Var}(Y_i\mid\mathbf x_i)=\phi\mu_i$. With the same data, unpenalized mean model and common positive $\phi$, the quasi-score is the Poisson score divided by $\phi$. Its roots, and hence the fitted coefficient estimates when identifiable, coincide; the estimated coefficient covariance is scaled by $\hat\phi$. R's `quasipoisson` family implements the free dispersion, whereas its `poisson` family fixes dispersion at one.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§2.3, p. 375){{cite:rstats_family2026}} (§ Usage and Details)

This moment specification does not determine all count probabilities. In particular, a quasi-Poisson fit alone cannot generate a new count or supply an ordinary likelihood for a likelihood-ratio test; a comparison that needs a full distributional likelihood is not available from the first two moments alone.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§9.1, pp. 323–324){{cite:ver_hoef_boveng2007}} (p. 2767) When a full predictive distribution is needed, the [negative binomial](negative-binomial-distribution.qmd) is one alternative. Its mean–size form has $\operatorname{Var}(Y_i\mid\mathbf x_i)=\mu_i+\mu_i^2/k$, so its extra variance grows quadratically in the mean rather than by one constant multiplier. Mixing a Poisson rate with a gamma distribution produces this form.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.2.3, p. 199){{cite:ver_hoef_boveng2007}} (p. 2767, eq. 2) The two approaches encode different variance patterns, and the iteratively weighted least-squares weights they induce are not the same, so they need not give identical fitted means.{{cite:ver_hoef_boveng2007}} (pp. 2768–2769)

## Worked example — Five nightly counts and a Pearson dispersion
Five independent nightly counts are $0,0,1,3,6$. An intercept-only Poisson fit estimates $\hat\mu=(0+0+1+3+6)/5=2$. The squared deviations from two sum to $4+4+1+1+16=26$. Thus $X_P^2=26/2=13$, and with $5-1=4$ residual degrees of freedom, $\hat\phi=13/4=3.25$. The observed sample variance is $26/4=6.5$, also $3.25$ times the fitted mean. This is clear evidence of variation beyond the simple Poisson description of these nights; it does not identify its cause.

For the intercept on the log scale, the Poisson model-based standard error is $1/\sqrt{\sum_i y_i}=1/\sqrt{10}\approx0.316$. Multiplying by $\sqrt{3.25}$ gives a quasi-Poisson standard error of about $0.570$. The fitted mean remains two under the same log-linear quasi-score, while its uncertainty increases. With only five nights, this numerical estimate of dispersion is itself unstable, so a wider sampling window and diagnostic plots would be valuable.

**Your turn.** Four independent counts are $0,1,1,6$. Fit an intercept-only Poisson mean, then calculate the Pearson statistic and its residual-degrees-of-freedom estimate of $\phi$. What would that estimate say about the Poisson variance assumption?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The fitted mean is $(0+1+1+6)/4=2$. Squared deviations total $4+1+1+16=22$, so $X_P^2=22/2=11$. There are $4-1=3$ residual degrees of freedom, giving $\hat\phi=11/3\approx3.667$. The fitted variance is much larger than the Poisson benchmark under this simple mean model; missing predictors or dependence are still possible explanations.
:::

## Common misconceptions — Extra variance is not a surplus of large counts
Overdispersion does not mean that the observed counts exceed the fitted means: positive and negative residuals both contribute to excess variance, because the Pearson sum squares every residual. A variance-to-mean ratio above one in a heterogeneous pooled sample does not establish conditional overdispersion, as the two-group calculation with pooled variance $7$ and pooled mean $3$ shows. Nor does changing to quasi-Poisson change fitted coefficients when its mean model and common-scale assumptions are unchanged; its immediate effect is on model-based uncertainty, because a common $\phi$ cancels from the score and remains in the covariance.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§9.2.3, eqs. 9.5–9.6, p. 327) Finally, a large Pearson ratio does not specify which alternative distribution generated the counts. The moment model has no unique mass function.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§9.1, pp. 323–324)

## Assumptions and limits — When the diagnosis misleads
If nights share an unobserved weekly shock, their counts are correlated and the independent-observation Pearson calibration can be misleading. The usual large-sample covariance for $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ assumes that the eigenvalues of the information grow and that the working variance is the one in the estimating equation.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.2.4, p. 200) If observation times differ but an exposure offset is omitted, a long night may contribute a large residual simply because it had more time at risk. If two patient groups have means one and five but are fitted with a common mean three, the pooled variance of seven reflects a misspecified mean. At the other extreme, a process that suppresses successive events can have variance below its mean; quasi-Poisson with an estimated $\phi<1$ describes that underdispersion, whereas a negative binomial cannot because $\mu+\mu^2/k>\mu$.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.1, p. 194; §6.2.3, p. 199)

## Connections — Dispersion, quasi-likelihood, and a full count law
The [dispersion parameter](dispersion-parameter.qmd) quantifies the scale change in a chosen variance function. [Poisson regression](poisson-regression.qmd) supplies the equal-mean-and-variance benchmark; [quasi-likelihood](quasi-score-and-quasi-likelihood.qmd) supports moment-based estimation when the variance function is known up to that scale; and the [negative binomial distribution](negative-binomial-distribution.qmd) supplies a full distribution when gamma variation in rates is a plausible account.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.2.3, p. 199; §9.1, pp. 323–324) These links matter when deciding whether the aim is coefficient uncertainty, prediction, or both. A correction to the standard errors does not, by itself, produce a probability for a future count.

## History — Variance became part of the 1972 model choice
Nelder and Wedderburn's 1972 paper brought the Normal, binomial, Poisson and gamma families into one exponential-family scheme fitted by iterative weighted least squares.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (Summary, p. 370; §§1.1–1.3, pp. 371–372) The variance function $V$, defined so that it is the variance when the scale factor equals one, is part of that model. The scale factor may be known, and for the Poisson distribution it is unity, or unknown, in which case it is estimated from the deviance once an adequate mean has been fitted.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§1.1, eq. 4, p. 371; §2.3, p. 375) That free scale is the 1972 root of a dispersion that is not locked to the Poisson variance. Much earlier, Greenwood and Yule had already seen accident counts spread more widely once individual liability was allowed to vary: the simple Poisson series was a poor graduation, and the compound series fitted from the mean and second moment was not.{{cite:greenwood_yule1920}} (§IV, pp. 273–276) In the later generalised-linear-model account, random exposure, clustering and accident-proneness are named mechanisms for the same extra-Poisson variation, and inter-ship variability is given as a reason to expect over-dispersion rather than a pure Poisson variance.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.1, p. 193; §6.2.3, pp. 198–199)
