## Motivating example
A prior can express a scientifically meaningful shape that is not preserved by multiplying by the likelihood. That changes the computation required to obtain the posterior, but does not invalidate Bayesian updating. Conjugacy is a useful algebraic property of a prior family relative to a sampling model. It is not a requirement that all reasonable prior beliefs belong to that family.{{cite:downey2020_thinkbayes2}} (Ch. 18, “Conjugate Priors”)

## Definition — Posterior normalisation is the essential step
For a nonnegative prior density $\pi(\theta)$ and likelihood $L(\theta)$, a proper posterior density is $L(\theta)\pi(\theta)/Z$ when $0<Z=\int L(u)\pi(u)\,du<\infty$. Conjugacy means that this density belongs to the same chosen parametric family as the prior for every admissible observation. A named closed form can make summaries easy; without it, quadrature, grids or simulation can evaluate the same integrals.{{cite:taraldsen2022_improper}} (§4.1, conditional densities and Bayesian inference)

For bounded likelihoods, every proper prior gives a finite integral, though positive evidence also requires that prior and likelihood overlap. With unbounded likelihoods, posterior propriety needs separate attention. Conversely, an improper kernel can sometimes yield a proper posterior, but that requires checking the normalising integral. Numerical output that looks smooth does not itself prove that the underlying integral is finite.{{cite:taraldsen2022_improper}} (§§1, 4.1)

## Interpretation — A finite mixture stays interpretable
Consider a prior that is a mixture of two beta densities, $w\operatorname{Beta}(a_1,b_1)+(1-w)\operatorname{Beta}(a_2,b_2)$. Binomial data update each beta component conjugately, while their posterior weights are proportional to prior weights times component marginal likelihoods. The resulting posterior is generally a beta mixture rather than one beta distribution. This provides an exact computation for a richer prior without replacing it by a single convenient shape. The beta update follows from multiplying the powers of the success and failure probabilities.{{cite:downey2020_thinkbayes2}} (Ch. 18, “Binomial Likelihood”)

## Worked example — A two-component prior for a probability
Let the prior put equal weight on $\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)$ and $\operatorname{Beta}(3,1)$. Observe two successes in two independent Bernoulli trials. Their component evidence values are $1/3$ and $3/5$, since each is the prior expectation of $\theta^2$. Normalising their products with the equal prior weights gives posterior component weights $5/14$ and $9/14$. The posterior components are $\operatorname{Beta}(3,1)$ and $\operatorname{Beta}(5,1)$, so the posterior mean is $(5/14)(3/4)+(9/14)(5/6)=45/56\approx0.803571$. Every step uses the probability rule even though the answer is a mixture.{{cite:downey2020_thinkbayes2}} (Ch. 18, beta–binomial updating)

The normalising evidence for the mixture is $7/15$. Direct integration of $\theta^2\{1/2+(3/2)\theta^2\}$ gives the same value, providing a second route to the posterior weights and mean. When a closed-form mixture is unavailable, the corresponding integral ratio for the posterior mean remains valid.{{cite:taraldsen2022_improper}} (§4.1)

**Your turn.** What is the posterior probability that the second mixture component generated the parameter before the data were sampled?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
It is the posterior mixture weight $9/14$. This is a latent-component probability, distinct from the posterior mean of $\theta$.
:::

## Assumptions and limits — Check approximation error separately
A grid can miss a narrow peak or truncate material tail probability. Its resolution and range should be varied, and integral summaries compared where possible. In several dimensions the number of grid points grows multiplicatively, motivating methods that explore the posterior more selectively. Markov chain samples also require assessment of dependence and convergence. Choosing a nonconjugate prior therefore adds a computational task; it does not remove the modelling task or the need to check the resulting posterior.{{cite:downey2020_thinkbayes2}} (Ch. 18, introduction; Ch. 19, “Grid Approximation”)

## Intuition
Conjugacy is a closure property. The prior sits in a family, the likelihood multiplies it, and the product is still in the family. A beta prior and a binomial likelihood are the standard example: successes add to the first shape and failures add to the second. The posterior mean, the predictive probability, and the normalising constant are then ratios of gamma functions, or of the shapes themselves. A prior that is a mixture of two betas is not a beta. Each component updates by the beta rule, and the data reweight the components by their marginal likelihoods. The posterior is a mixture. The calculation is exact. It is simply not a one-line update of a single pair of shapes.

In the worked mixture, equal prior weights sit on $\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)$ and $\operatorname{Beta}(3,1)$. Two successes in two trials have component evidence $1/3$ and $3/5$, the prior expectations of $\theta^2$. The unnormalised posterior weights are proportional to $(1/2)\cdot(1/3)=1/6$ and $(1/2)\cdot(3/5)=3/10$. Their sum is $1/6+3/10=7/15$, which is the marginal probability of the data. Dividing gives posterior weights $(1/6)/(7/15)=5/14$ and $(3/10)/(7/15)=9/14$. The updated components are $\operatorname{Beta}(3,1)$ and $\operatorname{Beta}(5,1)$, with means $3/4$ and $5/6$. The posterior mean of $\theta$ is the weighted average $(5/14)(3/4)+(9/14)(5/6)=45/56\approx 0.803571$, not the unweighted average of $3/4$ and $5/6$.

## Properties — The normalising constant is an integral, not a symbol
For a prior density $\pi$ and a likelihood $L$, the marginal probability of the data is $Z=\int L(u)\pi(u)\,du$ when the integral is finite and positive. The posterior density is $L\pi/Z$. If a quadrature reports $Z=2/9$, the density is the integrand multiplied by $9/2$. Conjugacy makes $Z$ a known function of the prior parameters and the counts. Absence of conjugacy means $Z$ is computed rather than recognised. The probability definition does not change.

A single beta component shows the same arithmetic without a mixture. A $\operatorname{Beta}(2,5)$ prior updated by one success and three failures becomes $\operatorname{Beta}(3,8)$, because the shapes add the success and the failures. The component mean moves from $2/7$ to $3/11$. That update is available inside a mixture for every component, whether or not the mixture weights have a closed form. The weights still require the marginal likelihood of each component. For a beta–binomial component those marginals are beta-function ratios. For a nonconjugate component they are integrals.

## Common misconceptions
A nonconjugate prior is sometimes described as if Bayesian updating no longer applied. Updating is multiplication by the likelihood and division by $Z$, whenever $Z$ is finite and positive. Conjugacy only changes whether the result is a named distribution. A proper prior and a bounded likelihood give a finite $Z$ as soon as the prior and the likelihood are not supported on disjoint sets. An improper prior kernel can leave $Z$ infinite. The output of a sampler does not certify finiteness. A posterior draw can look stable on a region while the tails of an improper posterior remain nonintegrable.

The mixture weight $9/14$ is the posterior probability of the second component. It is not a posterior probability for a numerical event such as $\{\theta>1/2\}$, and it is not the posterior mean $45/56$. Component labels are a latent index. Summaries of $\theta$ average over that index with the posterior weights. Reporting only the component with the larger weight replaces the mixture by a single beta and discards $5/14$ of the posterior mass.{{cite:downey2020_thinkbayes2}} (Ch. 18, beta–binomial updating)

## Connections
Grid approximation, quadrature, and Markov chain Monte Carlo all estimate the integrals that conjugacy would have returned in closed form. A grid on $[0,1]$ for a binomial likelihood is feasible because the parameter is one-dimensional and bounded. A hierarchical model with a variance parameter, a vector of means, and a regression coefficient is the setting in which the grid becomes a product of grids and a sampler is the practical tool. The modelling choice of the prior is still separate from that computation. A conjugate prior that contradicts the scientific information is a convenient answer to a different question. A nonconjugate prior that matches the information and is then summarised carefully is the posterior the likelihood and the prior define.{{cite:taraldsen2022_improper}} (§4.1, conditional densities and Bayesian inference)
