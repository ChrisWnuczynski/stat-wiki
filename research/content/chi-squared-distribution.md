## Motivating example

A bottle-filling machine has a stated process variance, and an engineer wants to know whether a small sample shows more variability than that claim allows. For normally distributed independent measurements, the scaled sample variance $(n-1)S^2/\sigma^2$ is a chi-squared random variable on $n-1$ degrees of freedom, where $\sigma^2$ is the actual process variance. Replacing $\sigma^2$ by the claimed value $\sigma_0^2$ therefore gives a $\chi^2_{n-1}$ statistic only under the claim itself: the null hypothesis that the actual variance equals $\sigma_0^2$. If the actual variance were twice the claim, the same statistic would instead be distributed as $2\chi^2_{n-1}$, so with $n=10$ its mean would be $18$, not $9$. The distribution supplies exact critical values for that question, under the claim being tested. The same family also appears as a large-sample reference for discrepancies between categorical observed and expected counts, although that second use has different assumptions.{{cite:siegrist2022_normals}} (§6.8, standard sample variance){{cite:nist_chisq_gof}} (§1.3.5.15, test statistic and critical region)

## Intuition

Squaring a standard-normal measurement turns both a large positive and a large negative deviation into a positive contribution. Adding independent squared deviations yields a measure of total standardized departure. The integer degree of freedom $\nu$ counts independent normal directions being squared, so increasing $\nu$ adds both mean and variance. A chi-squared variable cannot be negative, and small-$\nu$ densities are right-skewed. As $\nu$ grows, standardized fluctuations become closer to normal. The two plots show the density and cumulative probability for $\nu=4$: probability is the area under the density to the left of a chosen value, not the height of the density there.{{cite:siegrist2022_chisq}} (§5.9, definition, moments and normal limit){{cite:nist_chisq_handbook}} (§1.3.6.6.6, PDF and CDF)

## Definition

For integer $\nu\ge1$, let $Z_1,\ldots,Z_\nu$ be independent standard-normal variables and set $X=\sum_{i=1}^\nu Z_i^2$. Then $X\sim\chi^2_\nu$. The gamma density in the infobox extends the same family to every real $\nu>0$, even though a noninteger value no longer literally counts a finite set of normal coordinates. Its gamma **shape** is $\nu/2$, with rate $1/2$ or scale $2$. The CDF is $F_\nu(x)=\gamma(\nu/2,x/2)/\Gamma(\nu/2)$ for $x>0$, where the lower incomplete gamma function integrates from zero to $x/2$. The support includes $0$ as a boundary, but a continuous chi-squared variable assigns probability zero to exactly $0$.{{cite:siegrist2022_chisq}} (§5.9, distribution functions and relations){{cite:nist_chisq_handbook}} (§1.3.6.6.6, PDF and CDF)

## Properties

The gamma form gives $\operatorname E(X)=\nu$, $\operatorname{Var}(X)=2\nu$, and $M_X(t)=(1-2t)^{-\nu/2}$ for $t<1/2$. It also explains addition: independent $\chi^2_{\nu_1}$ and $\chi^2_{\nu_2}$ variables sum to $\chi^2_{\nu_1+\nu_2}$, because their MGFs multiply. At $\nu=2$ the law is exponential with mean $2$; for $\nu>2$ its density peaks at $\nu-2$, below its mean, reflecting the right tail. Its skewness is $\sqrt{8/\nu}$, which decreases with the degrees of freedom. Thus the familiar bell-shaped approximation improves with large $\nu$ only after centering and scaling; the raw variable still moves to the right as $\nu$ grows.{{cite:siegrist2022_chisq}} (§5.9, moments, MGF, relations and normal approximation){{cite:nist_chisq_handbook}} (§1.3.6.6.6, common statistics)

In normal sampling, the residual vector after subtracting the sample mean lies in an $(n-1)$-dimensional subspace. That is the geometric reason the sample-variance pivot has $n-1$, not $n$, degrees of freedom. Under a normal model, an orthogonal rotation separates the sample-mean direction from the residual directions, making their squared lengths independent. Without normality, uncorrelatedness alone does not generally give that independence or the exact chi-squared pivot. {{cite:siegrist2022_normals}} (§6.8, sample mean and standard sample variance) The joint statement, in which the sample mean is independent of the whole scatter, is developed in the [sampling law of the normal mean and scatter](sampling-law-of-the-normal-mean-and-scatter.qmd).

::: {.proof}
For $Z\sim N(0,1)$, $\Pr(Z^2\le x)=2\Phi(\sqrt x)-1$ for $x>0$. Differentiating gives $x^{-1/2}e^{-x/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$, the Gamma density with shape $1/2$ and rate $1/2$. Its MGF is $(1-2t)^{-1/2}$ for $t<1/2$. Independence multiplies the MGFs of $\nu$ such squares, giving $(1-2t)^{-\nu/2}$ and the displayed density for integer $\nu$. The gamma integral defines the same normalized density for positive real $\nu$; integrating it to $x$ with $u=x/2$ gives the stated CDF. Differentiating the MGF at zero yields mean $\nu$ and variance $2\nu$. Multiplication of two such MGFs proves the independent sum rule. The log-density derivative is $(\nu/2-1)/x-1/2$, so its interior maximum is $\nu-2$ when $\nu>2$. Gamma moments give skewness $\sqrt{8/\nu}$.{{cite:siegrist2022_chisq}} (§5.9, density, distribution functions, moments and relations)

For independent $X_i\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, form the standardized vector $Z=((X_i-\mu)/\sigma)_{i=1}^n$. Choose an orthogonal matrix whose first row is $n^{-1/2}(1,\ldots,1)$. Rotational invariance of the standard multivariate normal makes its output $U$ have independent standard-normal coordinates. The first is $U_1=\sqrt n(\bar X-\mu)/\sigma$; the squared norm of the remaining $n-1$ coordinates is $\sum_i(X_i-\bar X)^2/\sigma^2=(n-1)S^2/\sigma^2$. Hence the latter is $\chi^2_{n-1}$ and independent of $\bar X$. The first coordinate also proves $\bar X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)$. {{cite:siegrist2022_normals}} (§6.8, mean/variance independence and standard sample variance)

For the Pearson statistic with $k$ cells and fixed positive probabilities $p_i$, the multinomial central limit theorem makes $R_i=(N_i-np_i)/\sqrt{np_i}$ converge jointly to a standard Gaussian vector projected orthogonally off $u=(\sqrt{p_i})_{i=1}^k$. Because $\|u\|=1$, that projection has rank $k-1$, and $\sum_iR_i^2$ has limit $\chi^2_{k-1}$. If $r$ identifiable, smoothly varying parameters are estimated efficiently by maximum likelihood *from the binned counts*, first-order fitting projects the same Gaussian residual off the $r$-dimensional model-tangent subspace inside $u^\perp$. The remaining projection has rank $k-1-r$, giving the usual $\chi^2_{k-1-r}$ limit. The latter argument requires positive cell probabilities, full tangent rank, a regular interior parameter and large expected counts; it is not an exact finite-sample identity. {{cite:nist_chisq_gof}} (§1.3.5.15, binned-count MLE, statistic and degrees of freedom)

## Related distributions

The chi-squared law is a Gamma law with a fixed scale of $2$, so other Gamma shapes can be re-expressed in degrees of freedom. If $U\sim\chi^2_{d_1}$ and $V\sim\chi^2_{d_2}$ are independent, then $(U/d_1)/(V/d_2)$ has an $F_{d_1,d_2}$ distribution. The independence matters: using correlated quadratic forms in that ratio generally breaks the $F$ law. A one-dimensional Wishart law is the corresponding sum of squares, while the square root of a chi-squared variable has the chi distribution. These links explain why chi-squared variables sit beneath several classical normal-model tests.{{cite:siegrist2022_chisq}} (§5.9, Gamma and sums){{cite:siegrist2022_normals}} (§6.8, F ratio)

## Statistical inference

Normal-variance inference uses an **exact** chi-squared reference under the stated sampling model: $(n-1)S^2/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-1}$. In a categorical goodness-of-fit test, $\sum_i(O_i-E_i)^2/E_i$ uses a chi-squared reference **approximately** as the sample grows. The counts sum to $n$, removing one free direction, and estimating $r$ regular model parameters from those same binned counts removes another $r$. For fixed cell probabilities use $k-1$ degrees of freedom; for the specified fitted model use $k-1-r$. Small expected counts, data-dependent binning, or a different estimation method can spoil that simple reference.{{cite:siegrist2022_normals}} (§6.8, standard sample variance){{cite:nist_chisq_gof}} (§1.3.5.15, test statistic, count-based MLE and validity)

## Generating random samples

For integer $\nu$, generate $\nu$ independent standard normals, square each and sum. This construction mirrors the exact normal-sampling result. For positive noninteger $\nu$, a Gamma generator with shape $\nu/2$ and scale $2$ produces the same distribution; a Gamma call expecting a **rate** instead needs rate $1/2$. Simulation can check the analytic mean $\nu$ and variance $2\nu$, but numerical agreement of moments alone does not validate a chi-squared test's sampling assumptions.{{cite:siegrist2022_chisq}} (§5.9, normal-square and Gamma relations)

## Worked example

Four independent standardized measurement errors happen to be $1,-1,2,0$. Their sum of squares is $1+1+4+0=6$, a realization from $\chi^2_4$ under the independent-normal model. The reference distribution has mean $4$ and variance $8$; a sum of $6$ is above its mean but not especially extreme. For four degrees of freedom the Gamma shape is $2$, and $\Pr(\chi^2_4\ge6)=e^{-3}(1+3)=4e^{-3}\approx0.1991$. Thus about one fifth of independent-normal four-component sums would be at least this large. This tail probability assesses a *modelled random sum*, not whether a particular component is anomalous on its own.{{cite:siegrist2022_chisq}} (§5.9, sum of squared normals and Gamma tail)

**Your turn.** For $X\sim\chi^2_2$, find its mean, variance and probability of exceeding $4$. Explain which familiar positive distribution it is.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
Here $\nu=2$, so the mean is $2$ and variance is $4$. This is the exponential distribution with rate $1/2$ (scale $2$), giving $\Pr(X>4)=e^{-4/2}=e^{-2}\approx0.1353$. A value of $4$ is twice the mean, but its exceedance probability is still about thirteen and a half percent because the right tail is exponential.
:::

## Common misconceptions

The chi-squared **distribution** is a continuous probability law; a Pearson chi-squared **statistic** is a data calculation that may have this law only under a reference model. An exact normal-sample variance pivot does not imply that every sum of squared residuals is exactly chi-squared; fitting a nonnormal regression or using dependent observations changes its law. The degrees of freedom are not simply the count of observations: subtracting a sample mean uses one direction, and fitting cell probabilities spends additional directions. A large statistic measures discrepancy from expected values under the model, not the probability that the model is true. Finally, a noninteger degree of freedom is mathematically valid through the Gamma family even though it is not a literal count of independent squared normals.{{cite:siegrist2022_chisq}} (§5.9, positive degrees of freedom){{cite:nist_chisq_gof}} (§1.3.5.15, statistic and validity)

## Assumptions and limits — When chi-squared calibration fails

For a normal sample of size $n$, the variance pivot is exact; if the observations are strongly skewed, squaring their centred residuals generally does not produce the same finite-sample $\chi^2_{n-1}$ law. For a goodness-of-fit table with an expected count near zero, the Gaussian approximation to that cell's count is poor, so even a large total $n$ may not rescue the usual chi-squared calibration. If $r$ model parameters are estimated by maximum likelihood from the same binned counts but the test still uses $k-1$ instead of $k-1-r$ degrees of freedom, its reference tail area is wrong; the subtraction belongs to that regular fit from the binned counts, and estimating a parameter elsewhere in the analysis does not by itself justify it. And if counts are clustered rather than independent multinomial draws, the simple multinomial covariance behind Pearson's approximation fails. These examples concern distributional shape, sparse cells, parameter fitting and dependence separately.{{cite:siegrist2022_normals}} (§6.8, exact normality assumption){{cite:nist_chisq_gof}} (§1.3.5.15, count-based estimation and sample-size requirement)

## Connections

Normal variance intervals, Student $t$ pivots and $F$ ratios share the same orthogonal sum-of-squares geometry. The Gamma representation supplies the density and makes the $F$ ratio natural. Pearson's goodness-of-fit statistic reaches a chi-squared reference by a different route, a large-sample Gaussian approximation to constrained cell-count residuals. Keeping the exact and asymptotic routes distinct helps decide whether a printed critical value is justified for the data at hand.{{cite:siegrist2022_normals}} (§6.8, normal sample, t and F variables){{cite:nist_chisq_gof}} (§1.3.5.15, asymptotic goodness-of-fit reference)

## History

Chi-squared forms appeared before their modern testing role. One historical account credits Bienaymé with an 1838 limiting argument; another names Maxwell's three-dimensional case in 1860, Abbe in 1863 and Boltzmann's general case in 1881. Pearson's 1900 work then made a squared discrepancy between observed and expected frequencies central to goodness-of-fit testing. Those developments help explain the two prominent interpretations today: an exact sum of squared normal coordinates and an asymptotic reference for count discrepancies.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 71–72, historical accounts and Pearson test){{cite:siegrist2022_chisq}} (§5.9, normal-square definition)
