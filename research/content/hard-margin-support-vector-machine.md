## Motivating example
Suppose a laboratory score separates observed negative cases on the low end from positive cases on the high end. Many thresholds classify the observed cases perfectly; one can move a threshold close to either group and retain zero training error. A hard-margin support vector machine chooses the separator that leaves the widest empty band between the two groups. That extra room expresses how far an observation can move before its classification changes. The choice is meaningful only after specifying how distance is measured in feature space.{{cite:burges1998}} (§3.1, pp. 128–129, separable geometry)

## Intuition — The points that limit the gap
Imagine pushing two parallel planes apart, one touching each class, while keeping a decision plane halfway between them. The observations the planes touch stop the gap from growing; observations farther away can move without changing the answer until they reach a plane. This is the geometric reason for the name *support vector*. The dual coefficients make the same point algebraically: only training observations with positive coefficients appear in the normal vector. A point may lie exactly on a supporting plane yet have coefficient zero when constraints are redundant, so “on the margin” and “positive multiplier” are not always interchangeable.{{cite:burges1998}} (§§3.1–3.2, pp. 128–132, margin and KKT conditions)

The classifier predicts from the sign of its score. In the canonical scaling, the nearest supporting planes have scores $-1$ and $+1$, and the decision plane has score $0$. The distance from the decision plane to either supporting plane is $1/\|w\|$; the **full margin** between them is $2/\|w\|$. Multiplying both $w$ and $b$ by a positive constant leaves every predicted label the same, but changes those raw scores. The canonical constraints remove that arbitrary scale.{{cite:cortes_vapnik1995}} (§2.1, pp. 277–279){{cite:burges1998}} (§3.1, pp. 128–129)

## Properties — Why the dual is useful
The primal problem asks directly for a separating plane. Its dual assigns one nonnegative multiplier to each training constraint. At the optimum, $w=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i x_i$, so the score at a new point can be evaluated using inner products with the training points whose multipliers are positive. This also explains why replacing inner products with a valid feature-space kernel creates nonlinear boundaries in the original coordinates. The dual remains a concave quadratic because $\sum_{ij}c_ic_jy_iy_jx_i^{\mathsf T}x_j=\|\sum_i c_i y_i x_i\|^2\geq0$.{{cite:cortes_vapnik1995}} (§§2.1, 4, pp. 278–279, 283–284){{cite:burges1998}} (§§3.1, 4, pp. 129–130, 136–139)

The optimal $w$ is unique whenever the finite problem is feasible, since two different optimal weight vectors would have a feasible midpoint with smaller squared norm. This does not require all optimal multiplier vectors to be unique: several collections of points can represent the same $w$. With both labels present, a strictly separating plane can be rescaled so every canonical inequality is strict. Convexity and that strict feasibility yield strong duality, making equality of primal and dual objective values a useful certificate of an optimum.{{cite:burges1998}} (§§3.1–3.2, pp. 129–132, convexity and KKT conditions)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
For $w\ne0$, the signed perpendicular distance of $x$ from $w^{\mathsf T}x+b=0$ is $(w^{\mathsf T}x+b)/\|w\|$. Thus the score planes $-1$ and $+1$ are $2/\|w\|$ apart. On a strictly separable finite sample, the smallest signed score is positive; rescale $w,b$ by its reciprocal to obtain feasible canonical constraints, and by a slightly larger positive factor to make every constraint strict. The constraints define a convex feasible set. If feasible $(w_1,b_1)$ and $(w_2,b_2)$ have different normals, their midpoint is feasible and has squared norm strictly below the average of their squared norms, so two distinct normals cannot both minimize the objective.{{cite:burges1998}} (§3.1, pp. 128–130)

For $L=\|w\|^2/2-\sum_i\alpha_i[y_i(w^{\mathsf T}x_i+b)-1]$, minimization over $w,b$ requires $w=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i x_i$ and $\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0$. Substitution gives $Q(\alpha)=\sum_i\alpha_i-\|\sum_i\alpha_i y_i x_i\|^2/2$. Its quadratic term is nonnegative for every multiplier vector, so $Q$ is concave. Strict feasibility permits the convex KKT/strong-duality theorem to apply. Complementary slackness then says $\alpha_i[y_i(w^{\mathsf T}x_i+b)-1]=0$; in particular, $\alpha_i>0$ forces equality and makes $x_i$ a support vector. For any such $i$, equality gives $b=y_i-w^{\mathsf T}x_i$.{{cite:cortes_vapnik1995}} (§2.1, pp. 278–279; Appendix A){{cite:burges1998}} (§§3.1–3.2, pp. 129–132)

:::

## Interpretation — What the margin does and does not measure
The gap measures a geometric property of the fitted training sample, not a probability of correct prediction. A new point inside the gap still receives a sign-based prediction, but its small score says that modest movement could reverse that prediction. A point far beyond either supporting plane has a larger signed distance. The absolute size of the gap also depends on feature units: replacing a predictor measured in metres by centimetres changes Euclidean geometry unless the feature scaling is adjusted. Choosing a representation and assessing predictions on fresh data are therefore separate from solving the hard-margin programme.{{cite:burges1998}} (§§2.1, 3.1, pp. 123–124, 128–129, risk versus sample geometry)

## Statistical use
Hard-margin fitting is most natural when separation is credible and recorded labels are reliable, or when the separable problem serves as a geometric starting point for a richer classifier. For example, two controlled manufacturing categories may occupy disjoint ranges of a calibrated measurement. A held-out sample still matters: the maximized training gap says nothing by itself about how often future measurements will cross the boundary. If the groups overlap or a single label is wrong, a soft-margin fit admits violations instead of making the optimization infeasible or allowing one observation to dominate the gap.{{cite:burges1998}} (§§2.1, 3.1, 3.5, pp. 123–124, 128–129, 134–136)

## Worked example — A one-dimensional gap
Take four training scores, with negative observations at $-2$ and $-1$ and positive observations at $1$ and $2$. The nearest opposite-label pair is $-1,+1$; their midpoint is $0$. In canonical form $w=1,b=0$, so the scores of the four observations are $-2,-1,1,2$. Each signed score is at least $1$, and the two nearest points achieve equality. The full margin is $2/|w|=2$. Positive multipliers $\alpha_{-1}=\alpha_{+1}=1/2$ give $w=(1/2)(+1)+(1/2)(+1)=1$ and $\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0$; the other two multipliers are zero. The primal objective is $1/2$, and the dual is $(1/2+1/2)-(1/2)(1)^2=1/2$. The agreement certifies the fit, while moving $-2$ or $+2$ farther away does not change it.{{cite:burges1998}} (§§3.1–3.2, pp. 128–132, primal–dual and support-vector conditions)

**Your turn.** Keep the negative scores $-2,-1$, but move the nearest positive score from $1$ to $1/2$; the other positive score remains $2$. Find the new boundary, canonical $w,b$, full margin and the two nonzero multipliers.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The closest opposite-label scores are $-1$ and $1/2$, whose midpoint is $-1/4$. Their separation is $3/2$, so the full margin is $3/2$ and $w=2/(3/2)=4/3$. The boundary equation $wx+b=0$ at $x=-1/4$ gives $b=1/3$. Indeed $w(-1)+b=-1$ and $w(1/2)+b=1$. The two nonzero multipliers must be equal, say $\alpha$, and $w=\alpha[(1/2)-(-1)]=(3/2)\alpha$ gives $\alpha=8/9$ for each. The farther points satisfy their constraints strictly.{{cite:burges1998}} (§§3.1–3.2, pp. 128–132, canonical scaling and KKT conditions)
:::

## Common misconceptions
Perfect classification of the training set is not a guarantee of low error on future observations: a separator can fit a small, atypical sample. The full margin is $2/\|w\|$, not $1/\|w\|$; the latter is the distance from the decision plane to one margin plane. A nonzero multiplier implies a point lies on a margin plane, but the converse can fail when boundary constraints are redundant. Finally, the coefficients are not probabilities or direct measures of a point's causal importance; they are multipliers in a constrained geometric fit.{{cite:burges1998}} (§§2.1, 3.1–3.2, pp. 123–124, 128–132)

## Assumptions and limits — When hard separation fails
The four corners of a square with positive labels at $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$ and negative labels at $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$ form an XOR pattern. The positive convex hull and negative convex hull both contain $(1/2,1/2)$, so no affine separator can put them on opposite sides. Even more directly, giving the same feature vector $x$ both labels would require $w^{\mathsf T}x+b\geq1$ and $w^{\mathsf T}x+b\leq-1$ simultaneously. Either case makes the hard-margin feasible set empty. Soft-margin SVMs allow violations and penalise their size.{{cite:cortes_vapnik1995}} (§§2–3, pp. 277–282){{cite:burges1998}} (§3.5, pp. 134–136)

An unusual but correctly labelled observation can also make a feasible hard-margin fit fragile. In the worked example, moving one positive score from $1$ to $1/2$ shrinks the gap from $2$ to $3/2$ and shifts the boundary from $0$ to $-1/4$. If that observation is actually a mislabeled negative case, hard separation forces the fit to honour the mistake; if it collides with a negative case, fitting is impossible. Margin maximisation operates under the supplied labels and feature metric, so data quality and scale matter.{{cite:burges1998}} (§§3.1, 3.5, pp. 128–129, 134–136)

## Connections
The hard-margin primal is the zero-slack starting point for the soft-margin primal. In the latter, a penalty parameter trades a wide band against violations when classes overlap. The support-vector expansion of $w$ also prepares the kernel construction: substitute feature-space inner products for $x_i^{\mathsf T}x_j$ in the dual and for $x_i^{\mathsf T}x$ at prediction time. A kernel can separate an XOR-labelled set in a suitable feature space, though the separator is then nonlinear in the original coordinates.{{cite:cortes_vapnik1995}} (§§3–4, pp. 280–284){{cite:burges1998}} (§§3.5, 4, pp. 134–139)

## History
Maximum-margin separation was first developed for error-free separable classes. Boser, Guyon and Vapnik's 1992 training algorithm used inner products to extend the construction to nonlinear feature representations. Cortes and Vapnik's 1995 support-vector networks added a soft-margin treatment for training sets that cannot be separated without errors. The hard-margin problem remains the simplest setting in which the geometric gap, support vectors and quadratic dual can be seen together.{{cite:cortes_vapnik1995}} ("Introduction", pp. 275–276; §§2–3, pp. 277–281){{cite:burges1998}} (§§3–4, pp. 128–139)
