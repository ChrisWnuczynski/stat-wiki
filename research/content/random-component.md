## Motivating example
A ward records the number of infections each month and uses staffing and patient load to predict the expected count. A fitted mean of six infections is only part of the model: the analyst must also say how counts scatter around six. A Poisson response law assigns variance six, while a Gamma law with the same mean and dispersion one half assigns variance eighteen and even allows noninteger outcomes. The response is a count, so the Gamma law has the wrong support regardless of its useful mean–variance curve. Choosing the **random component** is the step that makes such distinctions explicit.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§§1.1–1.3, pp. 371–373){{cite:zhou2020_glm}} (§Introduction, Examples, Poisson and Gamma)

## Intuition
The predictor and link decide the conditional mean. The random component supplies a probability law around that mean, including its support, variance, tail probabilities and likelihood. If the mean count is six, changing the log link to another link changes how covariates produce six; it does not turn a Poisson count into a Gamma duration. Conversely, changing the response family can leave the fitted mean expression intact while changing the uncertainty and predictive probabilities. This separation is what lets one GLM framework cover several kinds of response.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§§1–2, pp. 370–375){{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (Summary; §1.1, pp. 370–371)

## Definition
For fixed covariates $\mathbf x_i$, the random component specifies $Y_i\mid\mathbf x_i$ as a member of one exponential family, with a parameter that may vary from observation to observation. In a basic GLM, the responses are conditionally independent. Write $\mu_i=\mathbb E(Y_i\mid\mathbf x_i)$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y_i\mid\mathbf x_i)=\phi V(\mu_i)$, where $V$ is the family's variance function and $\phi$ is its dispersion convention. The linear predictor $\eta_i=\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta$ and link $g(\mu_i)=\eta_i$ complete the model. Thus the random component is a conditional distribution, not a free-standing claim that all observations have the same mean.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§§1.1–1.3, pp. 371–373){{cite:zhou2020_glm}} (§Introduction, Definition and Moments)

The familiar choices illustrate what is being specified. A normal response has real-valued support and variance $\sigma^2$ that can be constant across means. A Poisson count has nonnegative-integer support and variance $\mu$. A Bernoulli response is zero or one, with variance $p(1-p)$ when its mean is $p$. A Gamma response is positive and continuous, with variance $\phi\mu^2$ in the GLM parameterization. A binomial count with $m$ trials instead has mean $mp$ and variance $mp(1-p)$; whether the response is a single binary observation or an aggregated count must be stated.{{cite:zhou2020_glm}} (§Introduction, Examples, normal, binomial, Poisson and Gamma){{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§3, worked response families)

## Properties
The variance function records how conditional spread changes as a fitted mean changes. If a Poisson mean doubles from $6$ to $12$, its variance doubles. If a Gamma mean doubles with fixed $\phi=1/2$, its variance rises from $18$ to $72$, fourfold. A normal component with fixed $\sigma^2=6$ keeps variance six. The three laws can therefore agree at one mean and diverge elsewhere, even if all use the same mean model.{{cite:zhou2020_glm}} (§Introduction, Examples, variance functions)

Specifying a full distribution is stronger than specifying only $V(\mu)$. A quasi-Poisson mean–variance model with $\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid x)=\phi\mu$ allows extra spread, but it does not supply every probability $\Pr(Y=y\mid x)$ or a likelihood for ordinary AIC. A Poisson law does supply those probabilities and has dispersion fixed at one. The variance function helps diagnose a choice of random component; it is not a substitute for its support or full shape.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§§1–2, pp. 370–375)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
For a Poisson variable, $\mathbb E(Y)=\operatorname{Var}(Y)=\mu$. A Bernoulli variable satisfies $Y^2=Y$, so $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=\mathbb E(Y^2)-\mathbb E(Y)^2=p-p^2=p(1-p)$. A binomial $Y=\sum_{j=1}^m B_j$ of independent Bernoulli trials has mean $mp$ and variance $mp(1-p)$. For a Gamma law of shape $k$ and rate $k/\mu$, the Gamma moments give mean $k/(k/\mu)=\mu$ and variance $k/(k/\mu)^2=\mu^2/k$. Set $\phi=1/k$ to obtain $\phi\mu^2$. At $\mu=6$ and $\phi=1/2$, this is $(1/2)6^2=18$; at $\mu=12$ it is $(1/2)12^2=72$. The support claims follow from the probability laws themselves.{{cite:zhou2020_glm}} (§Introduction, Examples, family moments){{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§3.4, pp. 380–381, Gamma moments)
:::
:::

## Statistical use — Selecting the response law
Begin with the measurement scale: is the response binary, a count, or positive continuous? Then examine conditional spread at comparable fitted means. Counts with repeated zeros and occasional large values may be more variable than a Poisson law permits; a Gamma response cannot solve that because it does not assign probability to integer counts as counts. For positive times with spread increasing roughly in proportion to the mean, Gamma is a candidate, while a normal law can put mass on impossible negative times. Residual patterns, study design and prediction checks test the chosen law rather than the word “random” in its name.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§§1–2, pp. 370–375){{cite:zhou2020_glm}} (§Introduction, Examples)

One should also state the sampling units and dependence assumptions. Monthly outcomes from the same ward can be correlated across time, so a conditionally independent GLM likelihood may understate uncertainty even with a plausible one-month distribution. A random intercept in a mixed model is an additional latent variable for between-ward differences; it is not the GLM's random component itself. A marginal estimating equation can retain a mean and working variance without claiming a complete joint response law.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§§1–2, pp. 370–375)

## Worked example
Suppose a count model predicts six infections for a particular ward-month. Under a Poisson random component, $\mathbb E(Y\mid x)=6$, $\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid x)=6$, and the probability of no infection is $e^{-6}\approx0.00248$. A positive Gamma model with mean six and dispersion $\phi=1/2$ has shape $k=2$, rate $k/\mu=1/3$, and variance $\phi\mu^2=18$. Its density permits values such as $6.5$ infections and assigns zero probability to the exact event $Y=0$. This is a poor count law even if the wider spread seems attractive. A normal model $N(6,6)$ matches the Poisson mean and variance but assigns probability $\Phi(-6/\sqrt6)=\Phi(-\sqrt6)\approx0.00715$ to negative values. The example shows why matching a mean or even the first two moments is not enough to specify the response.{{cite:zhou2020_glm}} (§Introduction, Examples, Poisson, normal and Gamma){{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§3.4, pp. 380–381, Gamma moments)

**Your turn.** A binary outcome has fitted success probability $p=0.8$. Give its Bernoulli variance and probabilities of zero and one. Would a Poisson component with mean $0.8$ represent the same experiment?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The Bernoulli variance is $p(1-p)=0.8(0.2)=0.16$, with $\Pr(Y=0)=0.2$ and $\Pr(Y=1)=0.8$. A Poisson law with mean $0.8$ has variance $0.8$ and assigns probability $1-e^{-0.8}(1+0.8)\approx0.1912$ to outcomes of two or more, impossible for this binary outcome. It is a different random component, despite the shared mean.
:::

## Common misconceptions
The random component is not a “random effect”; random effects introduce latent variables or group-level variation in a broader model. A link function does not choose the response distribution: Poisson and Gamma models may both use a log link but have different support and variance. A fitted mean does not determine a full probability law, as the six-infection example shows. Finally, the usual GLM assumption of independent responses does not say all means are identical: each $\mu_i$ may change with $\mathbf x_i$.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§§1–2, pp. 370–375)

## Assumptions and limits — Support, spread and dependence
For an ordinary Poisson GLM, a ward-month with fitted mean six but empirical variance around thirty across otherwise comparable months violates the Poisson variance relation. For an ordinary Gamma response, a recorded zero duration or a negative correction value lies outside the law's positive support. For a Bernoulli response, a reported value of two is impossible unless the observation is really a binomial count with multiple trials. Strong within-patient or within-ward dependence also breaks the simple independent-response likelihood. These failures suggest checking the measurement and sampling scheme before changing a link or inflating standard errors.{{cite:zhou2020_glm}} (§Introduction, Examples, supports and variances){{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§§1–2, pp. 370–375)

## History
Nelder and Wedderburn's 1972 formulation explicitly split a generalized linear model into a random component, a systematic linear component and a link. Their response-family examples included normal, Poisson, binomial and Gamma laws, providing a common fitting framework for distinct data types.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (Summary; §§1.1–1.3, pp. 370–373)
