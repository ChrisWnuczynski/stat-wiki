## Motivating example
The ages of patients in a hospital sample have a mean, but that single number does not show how widely the middle ages are spread. The lower and upper sample quartiles locate the ages below which roughly one quarter and three quarters of the observations lie. Their difference summarizes the middle half and is less driven by a few very old or young patients than the range. Before reporting a quartile, however, one must specify how an ordered position between two observations is handled: several common conventions give different answers in small samples.{{cite:hyndman_fan1996}} (pp. 361–363, definitions and software differences){{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 437, 440, Quantile and Quartile)

## Intuition — Locating a quantile in a sample
Sort the observations from smallest to largest. A $p$-quantile marks the transition at cumulative fraction $p$: for $p=0.5$ it is a median, and for $p=0.25$ or $0.75$ it marks a quartile. Ranking makes the idea resistant to the *magnitude* of an extreme value; changing the largest age from $95$ to $195$ does not move an interior rank. Yet a finite sample offers only finitely many steps in its cumulative distribution. If the desired fraction lies between steps, a rule must either choose an observed value or interpolate between adjacent values.{{cite:hyndman_fan1996}} (pp. 361–362, order-statistic definitions){{cite:r_stats_quantile}} (Description and Details, Types 1–9)

## Definition
Write the ordered sample as $x_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq x_{(n)}$ and its empirical distribution function as $F_n(t)=n^{-1}\sum_i1\{x_i\leq t\}$. One mathematically definite **sample $p$-quantile** is its generalized inverse

$$q_p^{\rm emp}=\inf\{t:F_n(t)\geq p\}=x_{(\lceil np\rceil)},\qquad 0<p\leq1.$$

At least a fraction $p$ of the sample is at or below this value, and the definition stays meaningful when observations tie. A sample median is a $0.5$ quantile, while the first and third quartiles correspond to $0.25$ and $0.75$; multiplying $p$ by $100$ names the corresponding percentile. An interpolated quantile may lie between observed values, and its literal fraction of observations at or below it need not equal $p$.{{cite:hyndman_fan1996}} (pp. 361–362, order-statistic and interpolation definitions){{cite:r_stats_quantile}} (Description and Types 1–2)

Many software summaries use interpolation. R's default `quantile()` method is type 7: for $0\leq p\leq1$ it sets $h=1+(n-1)p$, then linearly interpolates between $x_{(\lfloor h\rfloor)}$ and $x_{(\lceil h\rceil)}$ using the fractional part of $h$. At the endpoints it returns the sample minimum and maximum. This convention is useful and reproducible, but it is not the empirical inverse above and is not the only definition of a sample quantile. The median-of-halves convention for quartiles can differ from both.{{cite:r_stats_quantile}} (Description, Usage and Details, Type 7){{cite:hyndman_fan1996}} (pp. 361–363, sample quantile definitions)

## Properties
For a fixed convention, sample quantiles are nondecreasing in $p$: a higher percentile cannot be below a lower one. The empirical-inverse quantile always equals an observed order statistic and is unchanged by permuting the sample. If $g$ is strictly increasing and continuous, applying $g$ to every observation carries the empirical-inverse quantile to $g(q_p^{\rm emp})$. Linear interpolation also respects positive affine changes of units, but generally not nonlinear transformations: an interpolated quantile of squared values need not be the square of an interpolated quantile.{{cite:hyndman_fan1996}} (pp. 362–364, properties of sample quantiles){{cite:r_stats_quantile}} (Description and Details, Types 1–9)

The interquartile range is $Q_3-Q_1$ under the chosen quartile rule. It describes the middle half on the original measurement scale, while the median describes its centre. For a normal distribution, the *population* interquartile range is about $1.35$ standard deviations, which supplies a comparison scale, not a law that every sample obeys. With very few observations or many ties, different quartile conventions may give noticeably different IQRs.{{cite:hyndman_fan1996}} (pp. 361–364, quantile definitions and properties)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
The empirical CDF changes only at observed values. Before $x_{(\lceil np\rceil)}$ fewer than $\lceil np\rceil$ observations have been counted, so the empirical fraction is below $p$; at that order statistic at least $\lceil np\rceil/n\geq p$ have been counted. Ties can make the jump larger but do not alter the first value where the fraction reaches $p$. This proves the generalized-inverse formula. As $p$ grows, $\lceil np\rceil$ cannot decrease, proving monotonicity. A strictly increasing continuous $g$ preserves order and sends $x_{(j)}$ to $g(x_{(j)})$, proving the transformation property for the empirical inverse. For type 7, interpolation is a convex combination of adjacent order statistics; a positive affine map commutes with that combination, while a nonlinear map generally does not.{{cite:hyndman_fan1996}} (pp. 361–364, quantile definitions and properties)
:::
:::

## Statistical use
Quartiles, medians and percentiles describe positions in a dataset without assuming a normal model. They also anchor [boxplots](five-number-summary-and-boxplot.qmd), rank-based summaries and comparisons of distributions. State the convention when exact reproduction matters, especially when comparing a hand calculation with software. A sample quantile estimates a *population* quantile, but its uncertainty depends on sample size and how much probability mass lies near that position. An extreme percentile from a small sample can be unstable: the highest observed value is a poor stand-in for a rare population upper tail.{{cite:hyndman_fan1996}} (pp. 361–365, definitions and estimation properties){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 439, Quantile; p. 440, Quartile)

## Worked example
Consider the ordered observations $2,4,8,10$. At $p=0.25$ the empirical inverse chooses $x_{(\lceil4(0.25)\rceil)}=x_{(1)}=2$. R's type-7 rule instead uses $h=1+3(0.25)=1.75$, so it interpolates $75\%$ of the way from $2$ to $4$ and gives $3.5$. Both report a lower-quarter position under their stated rules; only the first is an observed value. At $p=0.5$, type 7 gives $h=2.5$ and median $(4+8)/2=6$, while the empirical inverse gives $x_{(2)}=4$. This small sample makes the convention visible.{{cite:r_stats_quantile}} (Description and Details, Types 1 and 7)

The patient-age example below uses R's `quantile()` output: for $299$ ages, its reported first and third quartiles are $51$ and $70$, giving an IQR of $19$ years. The sample mean $60.83$ and median $60$ are close, but their agreement alone does not establish a normal age distribution. The displayed age range is $40$ to $95$ and its standard deviation is $11.89$ years; the IQR-to-standard-deviation ratio $19/11.9\approx1.6$ differs from the normal benchmark of about $1.35$. These are descriptive comparisons of this sample, not a distributional test.{{cite:r_stats_quantile}} (Description and Type 7)

**Your turn.** For the ordered observations $2,4,8,10,14$, find the first and third quartiles using R's type-7 rule, and give the resulting IQR.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
With $n=5$, type 7 uses $h=1+4p$. At $p=0.25$, $h=2$, so $Q_1=x_{(2)}=4$. At $p=0.75$, $h=4$, so $Q_3=x_{(4)}=10$. The IQR is $10-4=6$. The absence of interpolation here is a consequence of these particular positions, not a different rule.{{cite:r_stats_quantile}} (Description and Details, Type 7)
:::

## Common misconceptions
A $25$th percentile does not require *exactly* $25\%$ of a finite sample to be at or below a unique number; the empirical CDF moves in jumps and ties can make a larger jump. The median is not always one of the observations under an interpolated convention. A population quantile and a sample quantile are different objects: the latter is calculated from data and estimates the former. And software functions need not agree merely because they use the word “quartile”; their rank and interpolation rules should be compared before treating a decimal difference as an error.{{cite:hyndman_fan1996}} (pp. 361–363, multiple sample definitions){{cite:r_stats_quantile}} (Description and Details, Types 1–9)

## Assumptions and limits
For values $1,3$ at $p=0.5$, type 7 reports $2$; after squaring the observations to $1,9$, it reports $5$, whereas squaring the original reported median gives $4$. Thus nonlinear transformations and interpolation do not commute. For a sample with many repeated values, a quantile can sit on a large CDF jump, so one number may represent a broad range of nominal percentile ranks. For a tiny sample, the nominal $99$th percentile may be determined almost entirely by the maximum, making it highly variable across samples. Finally, quantiles for unordered categories such as eye colour are undefined without a meaningful ordering; assigning arbitrary numeric codes does not create one.{{cite:hyndman_fan1996}} (pp. 361–364, ties and transformation properties){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 439, quantitative-variable limitation)

## History
Quantiles have long served as positional summaries, with quartiles dividing ordered data into four parts. Tukey's exploratory-data tradition made robust summaries of centre and spread especially prominent. Hyndman and Fan's 1996 comparison documented that statistical packages implemented several incompatible sample-quantile definitions and recommended a common language for them. R exposes nine of these conventions and currently uses type 7 by default, so the numerical choice is explicit rather than hidden in the word “quantile.”{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 439–440, Quantile and Quartile){{cite:hyndman_fan1996}} (pp. 361–365, definitions and recommendation){{cite:r_stats_quantile}} (Description, Usage and Details, Types 1–9)
