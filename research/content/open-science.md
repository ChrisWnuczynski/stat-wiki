## Lead context

Most scientists will say that data, code, and a clear plan ought to be available. The published record often does not match that sentence. Open science is the attempt to make the match: journal rules, archives, and citation practices that treat transparency as part of the work rather than as an optional extra after acceptance. The point is to let other people check a computation, repeat a procedure, and find the studies that were never published. A result that cannot be inspected can still be true. A result that can be inspected can still be false. Openness changes what a reader can do with the result, which is the practical content of the standards.{{cite:nosek2015}} (p. 1422, Standards){{cite:osc2015}} (Method)

## Definition

Open science, in the form set out for journals, is a set of standards that move publication toward openness about data, code, materials, and the plans that were made before the results were known. The Transparency and Openness Promotion guidelines group eight standards. Two of them reward effort already spent: citation standards that extend ordinary article citation to data, code, and research materials, and recognition of replication as a contribution. Four describe openness along the study itself: disclosure of the design and analysis, sharing of research materials, sharing of data in a trusted repository, and sharing of the analytic code. Two concern registration in advance: preregistration of studies, so that unpublished work can be found, and preregistration of analysis plans, so that the analysis is distinguished from explorations that came after the data were seen.{{cite:nosek2015}} (p. 1422, Standards)

Each standard has levels, from a disclosure that asks authors to say whether the materials exist, up to a condition of publication. Level 1 is designed to change journal workflow very little. A journal can adopt a stricter level on one standard without adopting every standard at the top level. The full text of the guidelines is kept in a public commons so that the version in a single article is not the only copy, and so that later revision is possible. The standards are about what is shared and when. They do not declare that an open data set is a true result. A false finding can be fully open. Openness makes the finding easier to inspect and easier to attempt again.{{cite:nosek2015}} (pp. 1422–1423, Standards and Levels)

Fields differ in what can be shared. Clinical and health research already had disclosure rules, and identifiable records are constrained by confidentiality. The guidelines treat that as a reason to fit the level to the discipline. A standard that demands a public microdata file of patients, when the law forbids it, is not a success of openness. A standard that demands a public description of the design, a registered analysis plan, and a data file available to qualified researchers under a use agreement is the version that matches the constraint.{{cite:nosek2015}} (pp. 1422–1423)

## Interpretation

The reward system is the reason a guideline was needed. Innovation was prized. The time spent documenting a data set, depositing code, or repeating someone else's study was not. Citation standards are an attempt to put data and code into the record that hiring and promotion already know how to read. Badges for open data, open materials, and preregistration, as used by *Psychological Science*, are a lighter step: they mark the articles that met a standard without yet requiring it of everyone. A badge is a signal. It is not a substitute for the file. A reader who cannot retrieve the data has a mark on the paper and nothing to recompute.{{cite:nosek2015}} (pp. 1422–1424)

Openness also changes the meaning of a negative result. A study that was registered and then found nothing is discoverable. A study that was never registered and never published leaves no trace, and the literature fills with the significant survivors. Preregistration does not make the significant survivors false, and it does not make the null studies true. It makes the selection visible. That is an interpretative change in the published record, not a change in the mathematics of a single test.{{cite:nosek2015}} (p. 1422, preregistration standards){{cite:osc2015}} (Method)

## Statistical use

The statistical content of the practice is ordinary and concrete. A registered analysis plan states the [outcome](response-variable.qmd), the model, and the handling of [multiple testing](multiple-testing.qmd) before the [p-value](p-value.qmd) is seen. A deposited data file and script are what make a result [reproducible](reproducibility.qmd) in the computational sense: another person can regenerate the table from the same inputs. Shared materials are what make a direct replication possible. Direct replication recreates the conditions believed necessary to obtain the original result, with new data. It can fail because the original result was a false positive, because the replication was a false negative, or because a condition that mattered was not recreated. The archive is what lets a later reader tell those failures apart.{{cite:osc2015}} (Method){{cite:nosek2015}} (p. 1422)

In the Open Science Collaboration, teams wrote a protocol and an analysis plan, obtained review, registered the protocol, and were required to archive replication materials and data. One hundred replications of experimental and correlational studies in psychology were completed. Effect sizes were put on a common correlation scale so that original and new studies could be compared, rather than leaving each paper in its own test statistic. Of 97 original positive results, 35 replications were positive in the same direction, which is 36.1 percent, with a 95 percent interval from 26.6 percent to 46.2 percent. A second summary asked whether the original effect size fell inside the replication confidence interval. Across the studies for which that comparison was made, the success rate was 47.4 percent. Those two summaries answer different questions: one is about a significance call, and one is about whether the original estimate is compatible with the new one. Reporting only the first understates the second, and reporting only the second hides how often the new study would itself have been called null.{{cite:osc2015}} (Results)

The project is also a demonstration of the practice it studies. Replication materials and data were archived at the Open Science Framework. A claim that a study was repeated, without the protocol and the data, is a different object from a claim that a reader can see the repeat. The statistical comparison of effect sizes is possible because the plans were registered and the files were kept.{{cite:osc2015}} (Method and Results)

## History

The gap the guidelines address was already visible in the reward system of the early twenty-first century. The Transparency and Openness Promotion committee met at the Center for Open Science in Charlottesville in November 2014 to write standards that journals could adopt in modules. The guidelines were published in *Science* in 2015, on pages 1422–1425 of volume 348, with the full text kept in a public commons and with an explicit statement that the version then offered was not the last word.{{cite:nosek2015}} (pp. 1422–1425)

Large replication projects made the operational meaning obvious in the same year. The psychology reproducibility project began its protocol work in November 2011, recruited collaborating teams, and published the estimate in 2015. A protocol that is registered and a data file that is public are the difference between a sentence that a study was repeated and a file that a reader can audit. Journals that award badges, and journals that require data and code as a condition of publication, are the institutional descendants of that argument. The standards remain modular because disciplines do not share one archive, one consent regime, or one definition of a replication.{{cite:nosek2015}} (pp. 1422–1425){{cite:osc2015}} (Method)

## Connections

Open science is the institutional neighbour of [reproducibility](reproducibility.qmd) and of the [replication crisis](replication-crisis.qmd). Reproducibility asks whether the numbers can be regenerated or the finding can be obtained again. Open science asks whether the journal and the archive make that attempt normal. It also neighbours statistical ethics and [official statistics](official-statistics.qmd): sharing is constrained by confidentiality when the data are identifiable records about people. A public microdata file that ignores that constraint is not a success of openness. The guideline that fits the discipline is the one that says what must be shared, what must only be described, and what was decided before the results arrived. [Misuse of statistics](misuse-of-statistics.qmd) is harder to hide when the analysis plan and the data are both visible, and it is still possible: an open file can be paired with a sentence the file does not support.{{cite:nosek2015}} (pp. 1422–1423){{cite:osc2015}} (Method)
