## Lead context

Most scientists will say that data, code, and a clear plan ought to be available. The published record often does not match that sentence. Open science is the attempt to make the match: journal rules, archives, and citation practices that treat transparency as part of the work rather than as an optional extra after acceptance. The point is not a slogan. It is to let other people check a computation, repeat a procedure, and find the studies that were never published.

## Definition — Scope

Open science, in the form set out for journals, is a set of standards that move publication toward openness about data, code, materials, and the plans that were made before the results were known. The Transparency and Openness Promotion guidelines group eight standards. Two of them reward effort already spent: citing data, code, and materials as contributions, and recognising replication. Four describe openness along the study itself: disclosure of the design and analysis, sharing of research materials, sharing of data in a trusted repository, and sharing of the analytic code. Two concern registration in advance: preregistration of studies, and preregistration of analysis plans. Each standard has levels, from encouragement up to a condition of publication, so that a journal can adopt a stricter rule without having to adopt every rule at once.{{cite:nosek2015}} (pp. 1422–1425)

The standards are about what is shared and when. They do not declare that an open data set is a true result. A false finding can be fully open. Openness makes the finding easier to inspect and easier to attempt again. Fields differ in what can be shared. Clinical and health research already had disclosure rules that the guidelines treat as a reason to fit the level to the discipline rather than to pretend one policy fits every archive.{{cite:nosek2015}} (pp. 1422–1425)

## Statistical use

The statistical content of the practice is ordinary and concrete. A registered analysis plan states the [outcome](response-variable.qmd), the model, and the handling of [multiple testing](multiple-testing.qmd) before the [p-value](p-value.qmd) is seen. A deposited data file and script are what make a result [reproducible](reproducibility.qmd) in the computational sense: another person can regenerate the table. Shared materials are what make a direct replication possible. In the Open Science Collaboration, teams wrote a protocol and an analysis plan, obtained review, registered the protocol, and were required to archive replication materials and data. Effect sizes were then put on a common scale so that original and new studies could be compared, rather than leaving each paper in its own test statistic.{{cite:osc2015}} (Method, Abstract)

Citation is part of the same apparatus. If a data set is cited the way an article is cited, the work of building it counts in the record that hiring and promotion already know how to read. Badges for open data, open materials, and preregistration, as used by Psychological Science, are a lighter step: they mark the articles that met a standard without yet requiring it of everyone.{{cite:nosek2015}} (pp. 1422–1425)

## History

The gap the guidelines address was already visible in the reward system. Innovation was prized; the time spent documenting a data set or repeating someone else's study was not. The TOP committee met at the Center for Open Science to write standards that journals could adopt in modules. By 2015 the guidelines were published as a short policy paper, with the full text kept in a public commons, and with an explicit statement that the version then offered was not the last word.{{cite:nosek2015}} (pp. 1422–1425)

Large replication projects made the operational meaning obvious. A protocol that is registered and a data file that is public are the difference between a claim that a study was repeated and a claim that a reader can see the repeat. The psychology project that archived its materials at the Open Science Framework was both a scientific study and a demonstration of the practice.{{cite:osc2015}} (Method)

## Connections

Open science is the institutional neighbour of [reproducibility](reproducibility.qmd) and of the [replication crisis](replication-crisis.qmd). Reproducibility asks whether the numbers can be regenerated or the finding can be obtained again. Open science asks whether the journal and the archive make that attempt normal. It also neighbours statistical ethics: sharing is constrained by confidentiality, especially when the data are [official statistics](official-statistics.qmd) about identifiable people. A public microdata file that ignores that constraint is not a success of openness. The guideline that fits the discipline is the one that says what must be shared, what must only be described, and what was decided before the results arrived.{{cite:nosek2015}} (pp. 1422–1425){{cite:osc2015}} (Method)
