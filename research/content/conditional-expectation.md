## Lead context
It is the prediction of that variable which uses only the information in the condition. Averaging the prediction recovers the unconditional mean, and no other prediction that uses only the same information has a smaller mean squared error.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§4.1)

## Motivating example
A shop's takings depend on how many customers arrive and on what each customer spends. The conditional expectation given the customer count is the mean takings for that count. It still has to be averaged over the possible counts before it becomes the mean takings for a day. The same idea applies to prediction: once a measurement $X$ is known, the conditional mean of $Y$ is the guess that the mean squared error prefers. The numerical version below makes both steps explicit.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§4.1)

## Intuition
Conditioning keeps only the distinctions the available information can see. If that information says which atom of a partition occurred, the conditional expectation is constant on each atom and equals the average of $Y$ there. If the information is a continuous measurement, the same role is played by an average against the conditional density, defined where the marginal density is positive. Different versions may disagree on a set of probability zero. They give the same integrals, and therefore the same predictions, on every event the information can name.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§4.1)

## Definition
Let $Y$ be an integrable random variable and let $\mathcal F$ be the information being conditioned on, a $\sigma$-field contained in the underlying $\sigma$-field. A **conditional expectation** $\mathbb E(Y\mid\mathcal F)$ is any random variable $Z$ such that $Z$ is $\mathcal F$-measurable and

$$
\int_A Y\,\mathrm d\mathbb P=\int_A Z\,\mathrm d\mathbb P
$$


for every event $A$ in $\mathcal F$. Any two such random variables agree with probability one. When the information is generated by a random variable $X$, write $\mathbb E(Y\mid X)$ for $\mathbb E(Y\mid\sigma(X))$. The number $\mathbb E(Y\mid X=x)$ is the value of a version of that function at $x$.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§4.1)

If $\{X=x\}$ has positive probability, the defining identity on that atom gives

$$
\mathbb E(Y\mid X=x)=\frac{\mathbb E(Y\mathbf 1_{\{X=x\}})}{\mathbb P(X=x)}.
$$


For a discrete $Y$ this is the sum of $y$ times the conditional probabilities. If $X$ and $Y$ have a joint density $f$, a version of the conditional mean is the density-ratio average

$$
m(x)=\frac{\int y\,f(x,y)\,\mathrm dy}{\int f(x,y)\,\mathrm dy}
$$


at values where the denominator is positive, and $\mathbb E(Y\mid X)=m(X)$. Where the marginal density of $X$ is zero, the ratio does not identify $m$.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§4.1)

## Interpretation
$\mathbb E(Y\mid X=x)$ is a number attached to a particular value $x$. $\mathbb E(Y\mid X)$ is a random variable, because it is that function evaluated at the random $X$. The tower property says that forgetting $X$ again returns the ordinary mean. The projection property says something stronger: among square-integrable functions of $X$, this random variable is the closest to $Y$ in mean squared error. A conditional mean can be an excellent prediction and still leave a large residual. The residual variance is part of the conditional distribution, not a defect in the mean.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§4.1)

For observed pairs, the slope $s_{YX}/s_{XX}$ is the least-squares slope: both formulas divide the centred cross-product by the centred sum of squares. A Gaussian model also specifies a conditional variance. Least-squares fitting alone specifies neither that variance nor a marginal distribution for $X$.{{cite:durrett2019}} (Thm. 4.1.15)

## Properties
On every positive-probability atom, $\mathbb E(Y\mid X)$ equals the average of $Y$ over that atom. If $Y$ is already a function of $X$ and is integrable, then $\mathbb E(Y\mid X)=Y$ with probability one. If $Y$ is independent of $X$, the conditional expectation is the constant $\mathbb E Y$. The tower property is

$$
\mathbb E\bigl(\mathbb E(Y\mid X)\bigr)=\mathbb E Y,
$$


whenever $Y$ is integrable. For square-integrable $Y$, $\mathbb E(Y\mid X)$ minimises $\mathbb E(Y-g(X))^2$ over square-integrable functions $g$.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§4.1)

For a bivariate normal pair with means $\mu_X,\mu_Y$, standard deviations $\sigma_X,\sigma_Y>0$ and correlation $\rho$,

$$
\mathbb E(Y\mid X=x)=\mu_Y+\rho\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}(x-\mu_X),
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X=x)=\sigma_Y^2(1-\rho^2).
$$


The conditional variance does not depend on $x$. The slope $\rho\sigma_Y/\sigma_X$ equals $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)/\operatorname{Var}(X)$.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§4.1)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
On an atom $A=\{X=x\}$ with $\mathbb P(A)>0$, the defining integral says $\mathbb E(Y\mathbf 1_A)=\mathbb E(Z\mathbf 1_A)$. A version that is constant on atoms has $Z=\mathbb E(Y\mid X=x)$ throughout $A$, so the right-hand side is that constant times $\mathbb P(A)$. Dividing gives the displayed average. Taking $A$ to be the whole sample space gives the tower property. If $Y=h(X)$ is integrable, $Y$ itself satisfies both parts of the definition, so it is a version of $\mathbb E(Y\mid X)$. If $Y$ is independent of $X$, the constant $\mathbb E Y$ is measurable with respect to $\sigma(X)$ and $\mathbb E(Y\mathbf 1_A)=\mathbb E Y\,\mathbb P(A)$ for every $A$ in $\sigma(X)$, which is the defining identity.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§4.1)

Suppose $\mathbb E(Y^2)<\infty$ and $g(X)$ is square-integrable. For every square-integrable $W$ that is a function of $X$, the pull-out property gives $\mathbb E\bigl(W(Y-\mathbb E(Y\mid X))\bigr)=0$. Set $W=\mathbb E(Y\mid X)-g(X)$. Then

$$
\mathbb E(Y-g(X))^2=\mathbb E\bigl(Y-\mathbb E(Y\mid X)\bigr)^2+\mathbb E(W^2).
$$


The second term is nonnegative and vanishes when $g(X)=\mathbb E(Y\mid X)$.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§4.1)

For the Gaussian formula, standardise $U=(X-\mu_X)/\sigma_X$ and $V=(Y-\mu_Y)/\sigma_Y$. The joint exponent of the bivariate normal density is

$$
\frac{1}{2(1-\rho^2)}\bigl(U^2+V^2-2\rho UV\bigr)=\frac{(V-\rho U)^2}{2(1-\rho^2)}+\frac{U^2}{2}.
$$


The factor that still depends on $V$ is the density of a normal law with mean $\rho U$ and variance $1-\rho^2$, up to a factor free of $V$. The conditional expectation is the mean of that conditional density, so $\mathbb E(V\mid U)=\rho U$ and $\operatorname{Var}(V\mid U)=1-\rho^2$. Scaling back by $\sigma_Y$ and $\sigma_X$ gives the two displayed formulas. The slope identity is $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y$.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§4.1)

For observed pairs, write $S(a,b)=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-a-bx_i)^2$. The critical point in $a$ is $\bar y=a+b\bar x$. Substituting and differentiating in $b$ gives

$$
b=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2},
$$


provided the denominator is positive. Any common positive divisor in the numerator and denominator cancels, so this is $s_{YX}/s_{XX}$. At this critical point the residual vector is orthogonal to the vectors $(1,\ldots,1)$ and $(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$. Shifting the intercept by $\alpha$ and the slope by $\beta$ therefore adds $\sum_i(\alpha+\beta x_i)^2$ and no cross term, which is the minimum.{{cite:durrett2019}} (Thm. 4.1.15)

## Statistical use
A regression states a conditional mean, and sometimes a whole conditional distribution, of a response given covariates. The conditional mean is the prediction used by squared-error scoring. An interval or a tail probability needs the conditional spread as well. Replacing a missing response by its conditional mean removes the residual variation that a real observation would have carried; the filled value is not a measurement. In a bivariate normal model the conditional mean is the straight line above, and the conditional variance is the same at every $x$. Outside that model the best squared-error predictor need not be linear, and the conditional variance may change with $x$.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§4.1)

## Worked example
Suppose the pairs $(X,Y)$ have probabilities $1/8,2/8,1/8$ on $(0,0),(0,1),(0,2)$ and $1/8,1/8,2/8$ on $(1,0),(1,1),(1,2)$. Then $\mathbb P(X=0)=\mathbb P(X=1)=1/2$. The conditional means are

$$
\mathbb E(Y\mid X=0)=\frac{0\cdot1+1\cdot2+2\cdot1}{4}=1,
\qquad
\mathbb E(Y\mid X=1)=\frac{0\cdot1+1\cdot1+2\cdot2}{4}=\frac54.
$$


Averaging them returns

$$
\mathbb E\bigl(\mathbb E(Y\mid X)\bigr)=\tfrac12\cdot1+\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac54=\tfrac98.
$$


The unconditional mean is $0\cdot(2/8)+1\cdot(3/8)+2\cdot(3/8)=9/8$, the same value. The conditional variances are $1/2$ and $11/16$. Their average is $19/32$, and the variance of the conditional mean is $1/64$. Adding these gives $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=39/64$. The constant prediction $9/8$ therefore has mean squared error $39/64$, while the conditional expectation has mean squared error $19/32=38/64$. The improvement is exactly the variance of the conditional mean.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§4.1)

For a bivariate normal pair with $\mu_X=0$, $\mu_Y=1$, $\sigma_X=2$, $\sigma_Y=3$ and $\rho=1/2$,

$$
\mathbb E(Y\mid X=4)=1+\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac32\cdot4=4,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X=4)=9\bigl(1-\tfrac14\bigr)=\tfrac{27}{4}.
$$


The conditional standard deviation is $\sqrt{27/4}=3\sqrt3/2$, the same at every value of $X$.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§4.1)

**Your turn.** The pairs $(0,0)$, $(0,2)$ and $(1,1)$ have probabilities $1/5$, $2/5$ and $2/5$. Find $\mathbb E(Y\mid X=0)$ and check that the tower property recovers $\mathbb E Y$.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
$\mathbb P(X=0)=3/5$, so $\mathbb E(Y\mid X=0)=(0\cdot1/5+2\cdot2/5)/(3/5)=4/3$. Also $\mathbb E(Y\mid X=1)=1$ and $\mathbb E Y=0\cdot1/5+2\cdot2/5+1\cdot2/5=6/5$. The tower average is $(3/5)(4/3)+(2/5)(1)=6/5$.
:::

## Common misconceptions
The conditional expectation given a continuous value is not obtained by dividing by $\mathbb P(X=x)$, which is usually zero. A conditional mean is not the conditional distribution: the same mean is compatible with very different spreads and shapes. Equality of $\mathbb E(Y\mid X)$ with $\mathbb E Y$ does not imply independence; it says only that this particular mean does not vary with $X$. For a bivariate normal pair the conditional variance does not become smaller at an extreme $x$. A sample least-squares line matching the Gaussian conditional-mean formula does not mean that the two procedures estimate the same object.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§4.1)

## Assumptions and limits
The defining identity needs $Y$ integrable. The mean-square characterisation needs $Y$ square-integrable, and the competitor must be square-integrable as well. Statements about versions hold up to sets of probability zero. The density formula needs a joint density and a positive marginal denominator. The normal formulas need a non-degenerate bivariate normal pair, so $\sigma_X$ and $\sigma_Y$ are positive and $|\rho|<1$ for a positive conditional variance; if $|\rho|=1$ the conditional variance is zero and the conditional mean is the linear relation itself. The least-squares algebra assumes at least two distinct observed $x_i$.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§4.1)
