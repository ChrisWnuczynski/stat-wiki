## Lead context
The useful comparison is between what a model predicts *before looking at a particular cell* and what was actually counted there. A frequency table alone can reveal an imbalance; observed and expected counts turn that impression into a reproducible calculation. The expectations must come from a stated null model, and their construction determines how the resulting discrepancy is interpreted.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, test-statistic subsection) {{cite:nist_contingency2002}} (§7.4.5, “Expected cell frequencies”)

## Motivating example
Suppose a manufacturer expects four colours of a component in equal proportions and inspects 80 independent components. A batch containing 24 red components may sound high, but the reference count is 20, not 40 or 80. The deviation is four components. Whether that is unusual depends on the deviations in *all* four colours and on the sampling model. An excess in one colour must be accompanied by a deficit elsewhere because the total remains 80. The same bookkeeping appears in a clinical table of treatment by outcome, but there the reference proportions usually come from the row and column margins rather than from a four-colour specification.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, test-statistic subsection) {{cite:nist_contingency2002}} (§7.4.5, “Expected cell frequencies”)

## Intuition
An expected count is a mean under a model, not a prediction that a sample will contain exactly that many items. If each of 80 independent components has probability $1/4$ of being red, then the red count has expectation $80/4=20$ even though the observed count can be 19, 24 or another integer. Expected counts can be fractional, because a mean need not be a possible count. For example, under a $1:2:1$ model applied to 93 chickens, each outer category has expectation $23.25$. Round these only for display; rounding them before calculating a test statistic changes the statistic and spoils the exact total.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, test-statistic subsection)

The difference $O_i-E_i$ is a signed residual. Pearson's statistic squares each residual and divides by $E_i$, giving a larger weight to a discrepancy of fixed size in a cell with a smaller expectation. The square prevents excesses and deficits from cancelling. A cell contribution can flag where a table differs most from a model, but it does not by itself give an independent cellwise test: the observed counts share a fixed total and often share estimated margins.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, test-statistic subsection) {{cite:nist_contingency2002}} (§7.4.5, “Test statistic”)

## Definition
For $k$ exhaustive and mutually exclusive cells, write $O_i$ for the observed frequency in cell $i$ and $E_i$ for its mean frequency under the reference model. Both sets of counts sum to the sample size $n$ when the model assigns all observations to those cells. If the cell probabilities $\pi_i$ are fully specified, $E_i=n\pi_i$ and $\sum_i\pi_i=1$. The observed and expected *proportions* are $O_i/n$ and $\pi_i$; multiplying by $n$ puts their difference back on the count scale. For a continuous model binned at boundaries $a_i$, the corresponding expectation is $n\{F(a_{i+1})-F(a_i)\}$.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, test-statistic subsection)

For a two-way table, $O_{ij}$ denotes the count in row $i$, column $j$. If the null hypothesis says the two classifications are independent, estimate their marginal probabilities by $O_{i+}/n$ and $O_{+j}/n$. The fitted expected count is then $E_{ij}=n(O_{i+}/n)(O_{+j}/n)=O_{i+}O_{+j}/n$. Its row sums and column sums match the observed margins. These are *fitted* expectations: they are functions of the same table, unlike $n\pi_i$ for probabilities specified in advance.{{cite:nist_contingency2002}} (§7.4.5, “Expected cell frequencies”)

## Properties
The conservation of totals is an immediate check on arithmetic: $\sum_i(O_i-E_i)=0$. A count in a set of cells can exceed expectation only if the count in its complement falls short by the same amount. In a table of independence, fitted expectations conserve every margin: $\sum_jE_{ij}=O_{i+}$ and $\sum_iE_{ij}=O_{+j}$. This makes a table with correct grand total but incorrect fitted margins a calculation error, not a new statistical finding.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, test-statistic subsection) {{cite:nist_contingency2002}} (§7.4.5, “Expected cell frequencies”)

Provided each $E_i>0$, the Pearson discrepancy $T=\sum_i (O_i-E_i)^2/E_i$ is nonnegative and equals zero precisely when all observed counts equal their expectations. Under a fully specified multinomial null and adequate expected counts, its reference distribution is approximately $\chi^2_{k-1}$. The lost degree of freedom reflects the fixed total. In a two-way independence table, fitting row and column probabilities leaves $(r-1)(c-1)$ degrees of freedom. The statistic measures discrepancy from a model; it does not tell which scientific mechanism created the discrepancy.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, “Critical Region”) {{cite:nist_contingency2002}} (§7.4.5, “Test statistic”; “Degrees of freedom”)

**Proof idea.** Indicator variables make $E_i=n\pi_i$ a direct expectation. The fitted independence formula follows by multiplying estimated margins. Summing that formula along a row or column proves that fitted margins are conserved.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, test-statistic subsection) {{cite:nist_contingency2002}} (§7.4.5, “Expected cell frequencies”)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Let $I_{mi}$ indicate that observation $m$ falls in cell $i$. Under the categorical null, $O_i=\sum_{m=1}^n I_{mi}$ and $\mathbb E(I_{mi})=\pi_i$, so linearity gives $\mathbb E(O_i)=n\pi_i=E_i$. This expectation identity does not need the indicators for different observations to be independent; the multinomial sampling distribution and its chi-squared approximation do require the appropriate independent-trial model. Since exactly one indicator is one for each observation, $\sum_iO_i=n$; since $\sum_i\pi_i=1$, $\sum_iE_i=n$. Subtracting yields $\sum_i(O_i-E_i)=0$.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, test-statistic subsection; “Critical Region”)

For an independence table, put $R_i=O_{i+}$ and $C_j=O_{+j}$. The fitted cell probability is $(R_i/n)(C_j/n)$, so $E_{ij}=R_iC_j/n$. Because $\sum_j C_j=n$, $\sum_jE_{ij}=R_i\sum_jC_j/n=R_i$. Likewise $\sum_iE_{ij}=C_j$. Thus all fitted margins equal the observed margins. In an $r\times c$ table with fixed margins, the first $(r-1)(c-1)$ cells determine the last row and column, which explains the dimension of the independence comparison; the actual large-sample chi-squared approximation also needs its sampling assumptions.{{cite:nist_contingency2002}} (§7.4.5, “Expected cell frequencies”; “Degrees of freedom”)
:::
:::

## Statistical use
To compare a discrete sample with a specified probability model, first define a complete set of cells and their reference probabilities. Compute all $E_i$ from the null, check that they sum to $n$, then calculate each $(O_i-E_i)^2/E_i$. Look at the complete statistic and its reference degrees of freedom before interpreting any one cell. For binned continuous measurements, the probability of a bin is a CDF difference; changing bin boundaries changes observed counts, expected counts and the statistic. Choose and report the bins as part of the analysis.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, test-statistic subsection; “Critical Region”)

For an independence test, write the row and column totals beside the table and calculate each expectation as row total times column total divided by $n$. NIST's worked wafer-defect table shows both observed and expected frequencies in every cell, making the disagreement visible before the contributions are summed. The reference degrees of freedom are $(r-1)(c-1)$ for this independence model. A goodness-of-fit model with parameters estimated from the counts needs a different degrees-of-freedom adjustment: under the usual regular setup, $r$ fitted parameters reduce $k-1$ to $k-1-r$. Counting cells alone is insufficient.{{cite:nist_contingency2002}} (§7.4.5, table and “Degrees of freedom”) {{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, “Critical Region”)

## Worked example
An independent sample of 80 components has observed counts 24, 16, 22 and 18 for four colours. Under equal colour probabilities, each expected count is $80(1/4)=20$. The four residuals are $4,-4,2,-2$; they sum to zero. The Pearson contributions are $16/20$, $16/20$, $4/20$ and $4/20$, giving $T=40/20=2$. The large-sample reference has $4-1=3$ degrees of freedom. This calculation describes a modest departure from equal proportions; a formal conclusion needs a chosen significance level or a reported $p$-value. Notice that replacing the four expectations by the four observed counts would force $T$ to zero and would no longer test the equal-probability model.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, test-statistic subsection; “Critical Region”)

Now suppose the same 80 components were classified by shift and colour. Their four-colour expected counts under independence would depend on *both* the shift totals and the colour totals; the value 20 from the previous null would generally be the wrong comparator for a shift-by-colour cell. The formula $R_iC_j/n$ answers a different question: whether the colour mix varies across shifts. In both settings, write down the model before calculating expectations.{{cite:nist_contingency2002}} (§7.4.5, “Expected cell frequencies”)

**Your turn.** Another independent sample has counts 18, 22, 25 and 15 in the same four colours. Under equal colour probabilities, find the four expected counts, the signed residuals, each Pearson contribution, the total statistic and its reference degrees of freedom.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, test-statistic subsection; “Critical Region”)

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The total is 80, so every expected count is 20. The residuals are $-2,2,5,-5$ and sum to zero. The contributions are $4/20,4/20,25/20,25/20$, hence $T=58/20=2.9$. With four fully specified cell probabilities and no fitted parameter, the reference distribution is approximately $\chi^2_3$ when its conditions hold.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, test-statistic subsection; “Critical Region”)
:::

## Common misconceptions
An expectation of 20 does not mean 20 observations must occur, or even that 20 is the most common result under every count model. An expected count of 23.25 is legitimate despite counts being integers. The zero sum of residuals does not mean the fit is perfect: positive and negative residuals cancel, while their squared contributions do not. And a large contribution in one table cell does not automatically make that cell a separately significant discovery. It belongs to a joint table with shared margins.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, test-statistic subsection) {{cite:nist_contingency2002}} (§7.4.5, “Test statistic”)

An expected count is also not an empirical count that has merely been smoothed or averaged. In goodness of fit it comes from specified or fitted model probabilities; in independence it comes from the product of estimated margins. If those margins were fitted from the data, the fitted expectations and their reference degrees of freedom must reflect that estimation. A tiny $T$ means the observed table lies very close to its expectations; it is not proof that the model generated the data.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, “Critical Region”) {{cite:nist_contingency2002}} (§7.4.5, “Expected cell frequencies”; “Degrees of freedom”)

## Assumptions and limits
The usual Pearson chi-squared reference is a large-sample approximation. NIST gives the familiar rule of at least five expected observations per cell for its goodness-of-fit procedure. Sparse cells may call for defensible pooling or an exact or simulation-based analysis; pooling changes the cells and hence the tested question. A zero expected count makes the displayed Pearson term undefined, and a positive observed count in that cell violates the model. A zero *observed* count with positive expectation is valid and contributes $E_i$ to the statistic.{{cite:nist_dataplot_chisq_gof2002}} (“Description,” expected-frequency rule) {{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, test-statistic subsection)

The simple multinomial model treats observations as independent draws into exhaustive cells with stable probabilities. Repeated observations from the same unit, clustered sampling, or probabilities changing across subgroups can invalidate that reference distribution even though the arithmetic $O_i-E_i$ remains possible. In a two-way table, the fitted independence expectations express the null; they do not establish independence. Examine the sampling design and any structural zero cells before applying the standard degrees-of-freedom formula.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, “Critical Region”) {{cite:nist_contingency2002}} (§7.4.5, “Multiplicative Law of Probability”; “Degrees of freedom”)

## Connections
The [Pearson statistic](pearson-statistic.qmd) combines the cell discrepancies into one quantity; the [Pearson chi-squared approximation](pearson-chi-squared-approximation.qmd) gives its reference law under a specified multinomial null. [Contingency tables](contingency-tables.qmd) use fitted expectations to test independence, while the [test with estimated parameters](pearson-test-with-estimated-parameters.qmd) accounts for parameters fitted in goodness of fit. A [Poisson regression](poisson-regression.qmd) also compares observed counts with fitted means, but its observation-level means come from a regression model rather than from a fixed-total categorical table. The same words *observed* and *expected* therefore name a common comparison across several distinct sampling models.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, test-statistic subsection; “Critical Region”) {{cite:nist_contingency2002}} (§7.4.5, “Expected cell frequencies”)

## History
Karl Pearson's 1900 goodness-of-fit paper developed the chi-squared comparison of observed and expected frequencies. The measure became a standard way to assess whether departures across a full frequency table are consistent with sampling variation. Later work corrected the reference degrees of freedom when model parameters are estimated from the same counts, a distinction that remains essential when constructing expected frequencies.{{cite:gorroochurn2016_pearson}} (Ch. 3, §3.2.2, p. 299; §3.3.1, p. 306)
