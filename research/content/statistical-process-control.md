## Lead context

A process that is left alone wanders. Tools wear, a supplier changes a batch, an operator resets a machine. Statistical process control is the habit of plotting a quality characteristic as production continues, so that a real change is noticed and ordinary variation is not mistaken for one. The chart does not repair the process. It says when someone should go and look, and the limits are set so that those looks are rare when nothing has changed. Quality control as a human practice is old. The statistical form of it, a chart with limits computed from a probability calculation, begins with Shewhart at Bell Laboratories in the 1920s.{{cite:nist_pmc11_2002}} (§6.1.1){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "control chart")

## Intuition

Imagine a machine filling bottles. The amount in each bottle varies even when the machine is doing what it was set to do. That background variation is the chance-cause scatter. A worn nozzle, a new batch of liquid, or a wrong setting adds a shift or a drift on top of the scatter. The chart is a time plot of the fill, or of the mean fill in successive samples, with a horizontal centre line at the in-control mean and two further lines, an upper control limit and a lower control limit. Points inside the lines, scattered without a pattern, are the chance-cause picture. A point outside, or a long run on one side of the centre, is the prompt to look for a cause that has a name and can be removed. Adjusting the machine because one point moved a little, while still inside the limits, treats chance scatter as if it were a signal. That adjustment adds variation. The chart's job is to separate the two.{{cite:nist_pmc31_2002}} (§6.3.1){{cite:nist_pmc13_2002}} (§6.1.3)

## Definition

Statistical process control is the routine monitoring of a process by control charts, and the actions taken when the chart says the process is no longer in the state the limits describe. A control chart is a time-sequence plot of a statistic or of individual measurements, including a central line and one or more statistically derived control limits. Univariate charts track one characteristic. Multivariate charts track a summary of several. The centre line represents the mean of the in-control process. The limits are chosen so that almost all points fall inside them while only chance causes operate.{{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "control chart"){{cite:nist_pmc31_2002}} (§6.3.1)

Process control, as distinct from the monitoring, is the change made once an out-of-control signal appears. Two kinds of intervention are described. A person follows an out-of-control action plan, a flowchart of checks for that process. Or an automated loop adjusts the process by an amount programmed from the size of the departure. Monitoring without that response is a record. Adjustment without a signal is tampering. The subject is a part of quality control, not the whole of it. Acceptance sampling judges a lot that has already been produced. Capability judges whether a stable process can meet a specification. A control chart judges whether the process is still the process the limits were built for.{{cite:nist_pmc13_2002}} (§6.1.3){{cite:nist_pmc31_2002}} (§6.3.1)

## Statistical use

The centre line is a mean for the in-control process. The upper and lower control limits are placed so that nearly all points fall inside them while only chance causes operate. If the limits are 0.001 probability limits, and only chance causes are present, the probability of a point above the upper limit is one in a thousand, and the probability of a point below the lower limit is one in a thousand. Searching for an assignable cause when a point falls outside is then a search that, under chance causes alone, happens about twice in a thousand trials. Where the limits are drawn chooses that risk. Two in a thousand is a convention, not a theorem. It could have been one in a hundred. The choice is the risk the programme is willing to spend on false searches.{{cite:nist_pmc31_2002}} (§6.3.1)

If the plotted statistic is normal, limits at three standard deviations are close to those 0.001 probability limits. From normal tables, the probability beyond three standard deviations in one direction is 0.00135, and in both directions 0.0027. Three-sigma limits are therefore treated as a practical stand-in for 0.001 probability limits when the statistic is normal. In the United States the multiple 3 is used whether or not the statistic is exactly normal. The standard deviation in the multiple may be the process parameter, an estimate, or a standard value adopted for the chart. The context has to say which. British practice has more often preferred limits that are true probability limits rather than a fixed multiple of a standard deviation.{{cite:nist_pmc31_2002}} (§6.3.1)

A point outside the limits is a prompt to look for an assignable cause and to remove it. Points that stay inside the limits can still be out of control if the pattern is systematic. If the first 25 of 30 points lie above the centre line and the last 5 lie below it, the chart is asking why, even though no point crossed a limit. "In control" means both that the points lie between the limits and that they form a random pattern. Shewhart charts for means and ranges, charts for individuals, cumulative-sum charts, and exponentially weighted moving-average charts are variants of the same display: a statistic in time order, judged against limits built from the in-control process. The action after a signal is the process-control step. The chart by itself is the monitoring step.{{cite:nist_pmc31_2002}} (§6.3.1){{cite:nist_pmc13_2002}} (§6.1.3)

Skewness moves the chance of a false signal. If the distribution is skewed upward, the upper three-sigma limit falls short of the upper 0.001 probability limit, and the lower three-sigma limit falls below the lower 0.001 limit. The risk of hunting for a positive assignable cause that is not there is then greater than one in a thousand, and the risk of hunting for a negative one is smaller. The net risk of a chance point outside the limits goes up, by an amount that depends on the skewness. For a Poisson count with mean $np=0.8$, the mean and the variance are both $0.8$, so the upper three-sigma limit is $0.8+3\sqrt{0.8}=3.48$ and the lower limit is taken as 0. The probability of more than three successes is about 0.009, rather than 0.001. The chart is then more willing to declare a high count special than a normal calculation would suggest. That is a reason to compute the tail from the distribution that was assumed, when the normal approximation is poor, and to say which calculation the limits used.{{cite:nist_pmc31_2002}} (§6.3.1)

A confidence interval for a mean answers a different question. It estimates a fixed quantity from a sample, and the endpoints are statements about that quantity. A control limit is a boundary for future points from a process that is supposed to stay put. Crossing it is a decision to look for an assignable cause, with a false-alarm risk chosen by the limit, not an estimate of a parameter. Tightening the limits until ordinary variation looks special spends that risk faster. If the chart says the process is stable and the output is still outside the specification the customer requires, the next step is to change the process. Narrower limits do not make an incapable process capable. They make a stable process look unstable.{{cite:nist_pmc31_2002}} (§6.3.1){{cite:nist_pmc13_2002}} (§6.1.3)

## Assumptions and limits

The probability calculation assumes that, while the process is in control, the plotted points behave like draws from the distribution used to set the limits, independent enough that a run of one-sided points is surprising. Dependence from shift to shift, a slow drift that the chart was not tuned to see, or a limit computed from data that already contained the assignable cause, each breaks that assumption. Three-sigma limits are a multiple of a standard deviation. They match 0.001 tails only approximately, and only for a normal statistic. For the Poisson example the upper-tail risk is about 0.009, nine times the normal figure, and the lower limit has collapsed to zero because $0.8-3\sqrt{0.8}$ is negative and a count cannot be. A chart that prints "three sigma" without the distribution has not stated the false-alarm risk.{{cite:nist_pmc31_2002}} (§6.3.1)

The chart does not identify the cause. It identifies the time at which a search is warranted. The action plan is what names the nozzles, the batches, and the settings to check. An automated loop that corrects every small departure, including departures inside the limits, is a different controller, and it can amplify the chance scatter it was meant to ignore. Monitoring without any action plan produces a record of signals nobody answered. The historical scope of the method is manufacturing and other repeated processes. A single study, with no time order and no repeated operation, does not have a control chart. It may have a confidence interval, which is the other construction.{{cite:nist_pmc13_2002}} (§6.1.3){{cite:nist_pmc31_2002}} (§6.3.1)

## History

Quality comparisons are as old as competing manufacturers. Medieval guilds required long apprenticeships and a demonstration of skill, aimed at the quality of the process as well as of the object. Modern regulation, factory inspection, and professional societies continue that aim. Statistical quality control is much newer. Statistical theory began to be applied effectively to manufacturing quality in the 1920s, after sampling theory had developed. Walter Shewhart of Bell Telephone Laboratories was the first to apply the new methods to the control of a process. His memorandum of 16 May 1924 sketched a modern control chart. He kept working on the scheme, and in 1931 Van Nostrand published his *Economic Control of Quality of Manufactured Product*, which set the pattern for later process control. The distinction the limits still encode, between chance causes and assignable causes, is the distinction that book and the charts made operational.{{cite:nist_pmc11_2002}} (§6.1.1)

Two other Bell Laboratories statisticians, H. F. Dodge and H. G. Romig, led the application of sampling theory to inspection of lots. Sampling inspection is a sister method, not a chart: it judges a batch, rather than watching a process through time. Shewhart, Dodge, and Romig together account for much of what the handbook calls the theory of statistical quality control. Deming's later consulting carried statistical ideas into industrial management. After the Census Bureau he became a consultant in 1946, went to Tokyo in 1947 to advise on sampling, and from 1950 saw Japanese industry take up his management teaching. The chart those factories used remains Shewhart's device. The consulting tour is a separate chapter in how the device spread.{{cite:nist_pmc11_2002}} (§6.1.1){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 155)
