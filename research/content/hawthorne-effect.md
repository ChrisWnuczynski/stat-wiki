## Lead context

People who know they are being watched sometimes work differently from people who do not. The Hawthorne effect is the name for a change in behaviour that comes from being studied, rather than from the condition the study meant to test. The name comes from lighting experiments at Western Electric’s Hawthorne plant. When the original illumination records were recovered, the story that had been told about them did not match the data. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, pp. 1, 3)

## Motivating example

In 1924 the National Research Council began lighting experiments at the Hawthorne works in Cicero, Illinois. The narrow question was whether better lighting raised output. The story that spread afterwards was stronger than that question. Academics and popular writers summarised the results as showing that every change in light, even a change that made the room dimmer, raised productivity, and that productivity kept rising when the original poor light was restored. The data from the illumination experiments were not formally analysed at the time and were later thought to have been destroyed. Levitt and List found them. The remarkable pattern in that summary is not in the records. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, pp. 1, 3)

## Intuition

If output rises after the experimenters arrive, the rise can be the light, the season, a trend that was already under way, or the fact of being measured. The lighting question is identified only if those other contributions are separated from the light. A room that is measured and not experimented on is one separation. A period in which experimentation stops, compared with a period in which it continues, is another. Announcing the change is a third problem: workers who are told that the light will be altered on Monday are not in the same position as workers whose light changes without the announcement. The effect, if it is present, is a response to the study. It is not a property of illumination. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, pp. 9–11)

## Definition

The Hawthorne effect, as the name is used for these experiments, is a change in output associated with being part of the experimental programme rather than with the lighting condition under test. The illumination experiments varied artificial light in rooms where workers assembled relays and wound coils, and recorded output. A relay took about a minute to assemble. The plant employed on the order of 35,000 workers. The lighting work ran, with summers off, from 1924 until April 1927. Artificial light began at 4 foot-candles and was taken as high as 36. In the last phase the windows were blacked out and artificial light was lowered, for one day, to 1.4 foot-candles, then raised to 11. Workers were told about the experimental changes. Lighting changes were made on Mondays, the day after the only day the factory was closed. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, pp. 5, 9)

A later reading of the whole Hawthorne programme, Elton Mayo’s among them, was that people produce more when they know they are being studied. Mayo came in as a consultant in 1928. The later relay, mica, and telephone-assembly experiments continued until June 1932. The illumination records are the part of that history that can now be checked against the original graphs. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, pp. 5, 7)

## Properties

Output in the three rooms followed a similar path in the first year. It rose to a peak roughly 10 percent above the pre-experiment baseline, then declined after experimentation was halted in April but stayed above the baseline. Output per worker in the plant as a whole rose about 1.4 percent a year over the period the illumination experiments were active. The first-year experimental peak is large beside that plant-wide figure, which is why the first year, read alone, looks like a response to experimentation. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 10)

The later years do not repeat that simple picture. Only one room was still under experimental variation in the second round. Output there peaked around 20 percent above the pre-experiment baseline, and the rooms that had left the experiment also rose. When experimentation in the remaining room was suspended again, output fell in all three rooms, which is what a seasonal summer decline looks like. In one regression, periods of active experimentation are associated with output 3 to 4 percent above the pre-experiment baseline after a time trend and the amount of light are allowed for. That contrast disappears once room-by-month and month-by-year terms are added. It is not an abrupt jump at the moment the experiment is switched on or off. Output also moves more with artificial light, which the experimenters set and announced, than with natural light, which they did not. A one-standard-deviation change in artificial light is associated with an output change of less than 1 percent. Mondays on which the light was not changed show the same within-week output pattern as Mondays on which it was, so the raw Monday jump is not evidence of a lighting effect. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, pp. 10–12, 15–16)

OpenIntro’s account of human experiments states the design problem in general terms. Random assignment does not by itself keep knowledge of the treatment from moving the outcome. A volunteer who knows a new drug is being given is not in the same position as a volunteer who knows that no drug is being given. The Hawthorne records are a factory version of that warning: the condition under test was light, and the workers knew when the experimenters were changing it. {{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.4.2) {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 9)

## Worked example

Take the illumination baseline as 100 units per worker. A peak 10 percent above that baseline is 110 units. A plant-wide rise of 1.4 percent on the same baseline is \(100\times 1.014=101.4\) units. The first-year experimental peak sits \(110-101.4=8.6\) units above that plant-wide figure. The 8.6 is a gap between two summaries of the same period. It is not, by itself, an estimate of a Hawthorne effect. Rooms that had left the experiment also rose later, and the 3 to 4 percent regression contrast did not remain after finer time controls. A later peak of about 20 percent on the same baseline is \(100\times 1.20=120\) units, which is \(120-110=10\) units above the first-year peak. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, pp. 10–11)

**Your turn.** On a baseline of 100, a 10 percent peak is 110 and a 1.4 percent plant-wide rise is 101.4. What is the gap between those two levels, and why is the gap not yet an estimate of the effect of being studied?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The gap is \(110-101.4=8.6\) units. Rooms that were no longer in the experiment also rose in later periods, and the regression contrast of 3 to 4 percent did not survive room-by-month terms, so the gap mixes the experiment with trend and season.
:::

## Assumptions and limits

Reading a rise during the experiment as a Hawthorne effect assumes that the rise is a response to scrutiny or to participation, not to the treatment, the season, or a trend that was already under way. At Hawthorne the summer declines lined up across rooms that were and were not still in the lighting experiment. The 3 to 4 percent elevation during active experimentation is identified largely by comparing the room that continued with the rooms that stopped, and it is not robust to interactions of room and month. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, pp. 11, 15–16)

The popular summary — every change in light raised output, including changes that dimmed the room — is not what the recovered graphs show. There is no systematic evidence that productivity jumped whenever the light changed. Hints of a subtler response to experimentation remain, and they are small beside the story. A one-standard-deviation movement in announced artificial light is associated with an output change of less than 1 percent. The effect is not automatic, and these records do not supply one magnitude that can be imported into every other study. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, pp. 1, 15–16)

A [control group](control-group.qmd) that is watched just as closely as the treated group holds the scrutiny fixed. [Blinding and placebos](blinding-and-placebo.qmd) are the usual tools for keeping knowledge of the assignment from becoming part of the treatment. Whether those tools are available depends on the study. A lighting change that workers must be told about cannot be blinded in the way a pill can. [Scientific control](scientific-control.qmd) is the broader discipline. The Hawthorne records are a warning about a causal reading, not a substitute for one. {{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.4.2) {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 9)

## Connections

A [designed experiment](designed-experiment.qmd) assigns conditions so that a later difference can be tied to the assignment. The Hawthorne effect is one reason that assignment is not enough when the participants can tell the conditions apart. A [randomised experiment](randomised-controlled-trial.qmd) still has to reckon with behaviour that responds to being measured. The illumination series is also a lesson in [secondary data](secondary-data.qmd): the graphs were thought lost, and the verbal summary travelled without them. When the graphs were fitted, the strong version of the effect that had been attached to them was not there. [Experimental error](experimental-error.qmd) is the rest of what a measured response contains once the assigned condition is accounted for. Being studied is one of the influences that can sit in that remainder. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, pp. 1, 10–11) {{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.4)
