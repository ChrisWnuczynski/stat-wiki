

## Lead context

People who know they are being watched sometimes work differently from people who do not. The Hawthorne effect is that change: a shift in behaviour that comes from taking part in a study, or from knowing that a study is under way, rather than from the condition the study meant to test. {{cite:mccambridge2014}} (p. 267) {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 7)

## Motivating example

In 1924 the National Research Council began lighting experiments at Western Electric's Hawthorne plant in Cicero, Illinois. The narrow question was whether better lighting raised output. The story that spread afterwards was stronger than that question: each time the light was changed, even when the room was made dimmer, productivity rose, and it kept rising when the original poor light was restored. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 3) The name for a behavioural response to being studied, rather than to the light, comes from that plant. {{cite:mccambridge2014}} (p. 267)

## Intuition

A treatment effect is the change produced by the condition under test. A Hawthorne effect is a change produced by the fact of the test: supervision, a questionnaire, an announced adjustment, the knowledge that output is being counted. The two can point the same way, so a rise in output after a lighting change is not, by itself, evidence that light was the cause. The comparison that separates them is a change the workers can see was arranged, set against a change of the same physical kind that nobody arranged. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 4) {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 16)

## Definition

The Hawthorne effect is a change in behaviour that comes from research participation and from the awareness of being studied. {{cite:mccambridge2014}} (p. 267) In the review that gathered later attempts to measure it, the working definition is any artifact, or any other consequence, of taking part in research, provided the outcome is a behaviour and the study is not also delivering some other intervention. {{cite:mccambridge2014}} (p. 268)

The studies that gave the effect its name were run at the Hawthorne works, the Western Electric factory near Chicago, between 1924 and 1933. Output rose among workers who were supervised intensively as part of a research programme. {{cite:mccambridge2014}} (p. 267) The first series, the illumination experiments, varied the light in rooms where women assembled relays and wound coils, and recorded output as units finished per unit of time. A relay took about a minute to assemble. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 5) A later reading of the whole programme, Elton Mayo's among them, was that people produce more when they know they are being studied. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 7)

The practical warning is for any study in which being measured can change the measurement. If participants know the hypothesis, or know that a condition has just been altered, the contrast between conditions can mix the condition with the attention. Announced lighting changes at Hawthorne were made on Mondays, and the workers were told. Mondays on which the light was not changed show the same within-week output pattern, so the raw Monday jump is not evidence of a lighting effect. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 9) {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 12) Designs that keep the scrutiny the same in every arm, or that compare an arranged change with a naturally occurring one, are attempts to keep this mixture small. The effect is not automatic. Whether it appears, and how large it is, depends on the setting. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 4) {{cite:mccambridge2014}} (p. 276)

The illumination experiments did not show that every change in the lights, including a change that made the room darker, raised output. That summary was in circulation long before the daily sheets were analysed. The daily sheets do not show a systematic productivity jump on the days the light changed. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 3) {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 4) {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 13)

There is also no single effect with one magnitude. Most of the nineteen later evaluation studies report some participation effect, and they do not agree on how large it is or when it appears. Unqualified use of the name, as if it were a constant of observation, is not supported by that evidence. {{cite:mccambridge2014}} (p. 267) {{cite:mccambridge2014}} (p. 276)

The lighting work started in 1924 at a plant of about 35,000 workers and ran, with summers off and with the experimental rooms narrowing to one, until April 1927. Artificial light began at 4 foot-candles and was taken as high as 36. In the last phase the windows were blacked out and artificial light was lowered, for one day, to 1.4 foot-candles. Mayo came in as a consultant in 1928, and the later relay, mica, and telephone-assembly experiments continued until June 1932. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 5) {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 7) {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 9) The phrase itself is later than the factory work. It entered the methodological literature in 1953. {{cite:mccambridge2014}} (p. 267) The illumination records were widely thought to have been lost. When they were recovered and fitted, the strong version of the effect that had been attached to them was not there, and only weaker contrasts, between times of experimentation and times without it, and between artificial and natural light, remained as hints. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 4) {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 17)

## Properties

The popular pattern does not survive the original illumination sheets. There is no systematic jump in output on the days when the lighting was changed. The immediate regression estimates are statistically insignificant: the point estimate is negative on the day of a change and positive the next day, and the two-day total is negative but close to zero. Splitting the changes into increases and decreases of artificial light does not restore the story that any change raised output. The only statistically significant coefficient in that split is a fall in output on the day artificial light was increased. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 4) {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 13) {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 14)

Two quieter patterns are more consistent with a response to the experiment itself. Output in the first year of illumination work rose to a peak roughly 10 percent above the pre-experiment baseline, while output per worker in the plant as a whole rose about 1.4 percent a year over the same era. In one regression, periods of active experimentation are associated with output 3 to 4 percent above the pre-experiment baseline after a time trend and the amount of light are allowed for. That contrast disappears once room-by-month and month-by-year terms are added, so it is not an abrupt jump when the experiment is switched on or off. Output also moves more with artificial light, which the experimenters set and announced, than with natural light, which they did not. A one-standard-deviation change in artificial light is associated with an output change of less than 1 percent. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 10) {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 15) {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 16) {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 17)

Later studies that set out to measure the effect are heterogeneous. A review of nineteen of them, eight randomised trials, five quasiexperiments, and six observational comparisons, found some evidence of a participation effect in most, and no secure estimate of its size, its duration, or its mechanism. Where a follow-up exists, the reported effects are short, with only two of the studies showing a difference beyond six months. {{cite:mccambridge2014}} (p. 267) {{cite:mccambridge2014}} (p. 275)

## Worked example

Take the illumination baseline as 100 units per worker. A peak 10 percent above that baseline is 110 units. A plant-wide rise of 1.4 percent on the same baseline is \(100 \times 1.014 = 101.4\) units. The first-year experimental peak sits \(110 - 101.4 = 8.6\) units above that plant-wide figure. The 8.6 is a gap between two summaries of the same period. It is not, by itself, an estimate of a Hawthorne effect: rooms that had left the experiment also rose later, and the 3 to 4 percent regression contrast did not remain after finer time controls. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 10) {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 15)

**Your turn.** The same baseline of 100 units is used. A later peak in the room that stayed in the experiment is about 20 percent above baseline. What is that peak, and how many units is it above the first-year peak of 110?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The later peak is 120 units. It is 10 units above 110.
:::

## Assumptions and limits

Treating a rise during an experiment as a Hawthorne effect assumes that the rise is a response to scrutiny or to participation, not to the treatment, the season, or a trend that was already under way. At Hawthorne the summer declines lined up across rooms that were and were not still in the lighting experiment, which is what a seasonal pattern looks like. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 11) The 3 to 4 percent elevation during active experimentation is identified largely by comparing the room that continued with the rooms that stopped, and it is not robust to interactions of room and month. {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 15) {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 16) Mechanisms that have been suggested, such as conformity to what the researcher seems to want, have not been isolated by the studies that only ask whether an effect is present. {{cite:mccambridge2014}} (p. 275)

## Connections

A [designed experiment](designed-experiment.qmd) assigns conditions so that a later difference can be tied to the assignment. The Hawthorne effect is one reason that assignment is not enough when the participants can tell the conditions apart. A [control group](control-group.qmd) that is watched just as closely as the treated group holds the scrutiny fixed. [Blinding and placebos](blinding-and-placebo.qmd) are the usual tools for keeping knowledge of the assignment from becoming part of the treatment. [Scientific control](scientific-control.qmd) is the broader discipline of which those tools are a part. The effect is a threat to a causal reading, not a substitute for one. {{cite:mccambridge2014}} (p. 267) {{cite:levitt_list2011}} (NBER WP 15016, p. 17)
