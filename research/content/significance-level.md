## Lead context
That allowance is a ceiling, fixed before the data are used to make the decision. The size of the test is the supremum of the rejection probability over the null. The supremum is an upper envelope, and it need not be attained at any null distribution. Equality of the size and the ceiling means that the envelope equals the chosen level. It does not mean that every distribution in the null rejects with that probability.

## Motivating example
A laboratory standard is supposed to have mean 5. A sample of 195 measurements has mean 9.261460 and standard deviation 0.022789. The measurements sit far from 5, but "far" needs a rule stated in advance. With a two-sided level of 0.05, the critical value printed for the $t_{194}$ law is 1.9723, the $0.975$ quantile rounded to four decimal places. The standardised distance from 5 is about 2611, vastly larger than 1.9723, so the rule rejects. The interval built from that same rounded critical value is $9.261460\pm1.9723\times0.022789/\sqrt{195}$, which is $(9.258241,\ 9.264679)$ and does not contain 5. Under the null that the mean is 5, with independent normal observations, the test that rejects when $|T|$ exceeds the unrounded $t_{194}$ quantile has rejection probability exactly $0.05$. For that rule the size equals the chosen level, because the null distribution of the pivot is one known law. The level describes repeated use of the rule when the mean is 5. It assigns no probability to the hypothesis that the mean equals 5.{{cite:nist_mean_limits2002}} (§1.3.5.2, significance level and critical region){{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 250–251, step 2, the significance level)

## Intuition
A test divides the possible values of a statistic into a rejection region and its complement. The significance level $\alpha$ is fixed after the hypotheses are named and before the critical value is calculated. It is the largest type I error rate the analyst is willing to allow. NIST's normal-mean page uses that order: choose $\alpha$, then the critical point $t_{1-\alpha/2,\,N-1}$, then reject if the statistic falls beyond it.{{cite:nist_mean_limits2002}} (§1.3.5.2, “Definition: Hypothesis Test”){{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 250–251, steps of a test)

The size is the supremum of the rejection probability over the distributions named by the null.{{cite:rudloff_karatzas2010}} (p. 1226, (2.1)) For a simple null the supremum is that single probability. For a composite null it is an upper envelope, and the envelope need not be attained. Let one Bernoulli trial have success probability $\theta$, and test $\theta\in(0,0.05)$ by rejecting when the trial is a success. At each null value the rejection probability equals $\theta$, hence is strictly less than $0.05$, while the supremum of those probabilities is $0.05$. The size is $0.05$, and no point of the null attains it.

A quantile construction makes the rejection probability equal $\alpha$ when the statistic's null distribution is known, or when a pivot has the same distribution at every null parameter. Continuity of a parametric family does not by itself force every point of a composite null to reject with probability $\alpha$. The laboratory $t$ rule is the pivot case: under the null that the mean is 5, the unrounded quantile rejects $5\%$ of samples from that one law. A discrete simple null places probability on isolated values, so a non-randomised region must take each value entirely or leave it out. Neighbouring tails can fall on opposite sides of the ceiling. The serve counts in the exercise below are of that kind. The non-randomised upper tail that respects a nominal level of $0.05$ then has size strictly below the ceiling.

## Definition
When a simple null makes the type I error a single probability, that probability is sometimes itself called the significance level, and a nominal level is then the bound placed on it. The names used here keep the bound and the attained supremum apart. Write $R$ for the rejection region and $P_\theta$ for the distribution of the sample at parameter $\theta$, with null set $\Theta_0$. The size of the test is
$$
\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_0}P_\theta(R).
$$
The significance level $\alpha$ is a number chosen in advance. The test has level $\alpha$ when its size is at most $\alpha$, and the test is exactly calibrated when its size equals $\alpha$. Exact calibration equates the supremum with the ceiling. It does not require $P_\theta(R)=\alpha$ at every $\theta\in\Theta_0$. Rudloff and Karatzas separate the two roles. The significance level is the largest type I error that is to be allowed, and the size is the supremum of the type I error over the null, which the test is required to keep at or below that level.{{cite:rudloff_karatzas2010}} (p. 1224, significance level as the bound; p. 1226, (2.1)) Siegrist identifies the maximum of the power function on the null both with the significance level of the test and with the size of the critical region, and describes the usual construction in which the region is chosen so that the maximum equals a prescribed small value.{{cite:siegrist2022_testing}} (§9.1, Tests of an Unknown Parameter) That identification is the case in which the supremum is attained and equals the prescribed value.

For a two-sided $t$ test of a simple normal mean, the unrounded critical point $t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}$ puts probability $\alpha$ on $\{|T|\ge t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}\}$ under that one null law.{{cite:nist_mean_limits2002}} (§1.3.5.2, critical region at level $\alpha$) A composite null for the same mean can keep a smaller rejection probability at parameters inside the null, even though the normal law is continuous. A discrete simple null misses $\alpha$ for a different reason. The probability of a region is a sum of atoms, and the sum need not equal $\alpha$. The size is then that sum, and $\alpha$ remains the ceiling. When two adjacent non-randomised tails of a simple null straddle $\alpha$, a randomised decision on the boundary atom can remove the slack. If the smaller tail has probability $p_c$ and the tail that adds one atom has probability $p_{c-1}$, with $p_c<\alpha<p_{c-1}$, rejecting with probability one on the smaller tail and with probability $(\alpha-p_c)/(p_{c-1}-p_c)$ on the extra atom makes the type I error equal $\alpha$. For $X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(15,0.6)$ and the boundary count $X=12$, that probability is approximately $0.36105$.{{cite:rudloff_karatzas2010}} (p. 1225, randomisation without slackness)

## Interpretation — Level, critical value, and p-value
The critical value is a cutoff computed from $\alpha$. The $p$-value is computed from the data: it is the smallest level at which this observed statistic would fall in the rejection region. Rejecting when the $p$-value is at most $\alpha$ is the same decision as rejecting when the statistic falls in the level-$\alpha$ region, provided the region was the one used to define the $p$-value. Reporting only "significant at 0.05" throws away how far inside the region the statistic landed. Reporting only the $p$-value, and then choosing a level after seeing it, abandons the procedure whose ceiling was fixed before the data were examined. Where a simple null is exactly calibrated, that precommitted ceiling is also the rejection probability under that one law. Where the null is composite, a size of $\alpha$ can leave smaller rejection probabilities at some null distributions, as in the one-sided test of $\mu\le\mu_0$. Where the statistic is discrete and the rule is not randomised, the size can sit strictly below the ceiling.{{cite:nist_mean_limits2002}} (§1.3.5.2, confidence interval as the set of means not rejected){{cite:siegrist2022_testing}} (§9.1, P-value){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 251, decision after the region is fixed)

## Properties — Where the level goes in a normal-mean test
For independent $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ observations with known $\sigma$ and the simple null $\mu=\mu_0$, the level-$\alpha$ two-sided region for $Z=(\overline X-\mu_0)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)$ is $|Z|\ge z_{1-\alpha/2}$, with $z_{1-\alpha/2}$ the unrounded standard-normal quantile. That known null distribution makes the size equal $\alpha$. The matching region for the sample mean is $|\overline X-\mu_0|\ge z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma/\sqrt n$. With unknown $\sigma$ the normal quantile is replaced by a $t_{n-1}$ quantile and $\sigma$ by $s$, and the equality of size and level again uses the unrounded quantile of that one pivot law. A one-sided level $\alpha$ uses one tail, $t_{1-\alpha,\,n-1}$. NIST's illustration with 194 degrees of freedom prints the two-sided critical value as 1.9723 and the one-sided critical value as 1.6527, each the corresponding $t_{194}$ quantile rounded to four decimal places, at $\alpha=0.05$. For a composite null $\mu\le\mu_0$ the rejection probability of the same upper tail increases with $\mu$, so the size is the probability at the boundary $\mu_0$. Continuity of the normal family does not give the interior null values that same probability.{{cite:nist_mean_limits2002}} (§1.3.5.2, two-sided and one-sided critical values)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Under the simple null $H_0:\mu=\mu_0$ with known $\sigma$, $Z\sim N(0,1)$. The unrounded quantile $z_{1-\alpha/2}$ satisfies $\Pr(|Z|\ge z_{1-\alpha/2})=\alpha$, so the test that rejects for $|Z|\ge z_{1-\alpha/2}$ has size $\alpha$, and therefore level $\alpha$. Multiplying the inequality by $\sigma/\sqrt n$ gives the region for $\overline X$. Replacing $\sigma$ by $S$ and $z_{1-\alpha/2}$ by the unrounded $t_{n-1}$ quantile preserves the equality of size and level when the observations are independent and normal, because then $(\overline X-\mu_0)/(S/\sqrt n)$ has one $t_{n-1}$ law, free of $\mu$ and $\sigma$.

If the null is simple and the statistic is discrete, the size of a region $R$ is $\Pr_{H_0}(T\in R)$. If the null is composite, the size is the supremum of that probability over the null, and the supremum need not equal the probability at any particular null value. A region whose size is at most $\alpha$ has level $\alpha$. When a simple discrete null has adjacent tails $p_c<\alpha<p_{c-1}$, the non-randomised smaller tail has size $p_c$, strictly below the ceiling. Rejecting with probability $(\alpha-p_c)/(p_{c-1}-p_c)$ on the boundary atom raises the type I error to $\alpha$. For the binomial null $\mathrm{Bin}(15,0.6)$ and the boundary count 12, that probability is approximately $0.36105$. A non-randomised threshold cannot spend part of the atom.{{cite:rudloff_karatzas2010}} (p. 1225, randomisation without slackness; p. 1226, (2.1)){{cite:nist_mean_limits2002}} (§1.3.5.2, critical region)
:::

## Statistical use — Reading a reported level
A reported level belongs to a named test and a named null. "Significant at 0.05" without the statistic, the direction, and the sample size is not a description of a region. The same data can reject at 0.05 on a one-sided test and not on a two-sided test, because the one-sided critical value is closer to zero. A confidence set of level $1-\alpha$ obtained by inverting one matching family of level-$\alpha$ tests contains the parameter values those tests do not reject. The duality runs between that family and the set it produces. An arbitrary interval and an arbitrary level-$\alpha$ test need not describe the same decision. The correspondence does not turn $\alpha$ into a posterior probability.{{cite:nist_mean_limits2002}} (§1.3.5.2, interval and the hypothesis test){{cite:siegrist2022_testing}} (§9.1, Equivalence Between Hypothesis Test and Confidence Sets)

## Worked example
Blood pressure in a reference population has standard deviation 7. For a sample of 101 independent normal readings and the simple null $\mu=127$, the level-$0.05$ two-sided region that uses the unrounded standard-normal quantile $z_{0.975}$ has null probability $0.05$. The calculations below use 1.960, that quantile rounded to three decimal places. For $T=(\overline X-127)/(7/\sqrt{101})$ the displayed region is $|T|\ge1.960$. The standard error is $7/\sqrt{101}=0.6965$, and $1.960\times0.6965=1.365$. The sample mean rejects $H_0:\mu=127$ when $\overline X\le125.635$ or $\overline X\ge128.365$.{{cite:nist_mean_limits2002}} (§1.3.5.2, level and critical region)

A discrete comparison shows why a simple null can still miss the ceiling. Let $X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(15,0.6)$, and let the test of $p=0.6$ against $p>0.6$ reject for $X\in\{13,14,15\}$. The probability of this region and the probability of the next larger tail $\{12,13,14,15\}$ lie on opposite sides of $0.05$, because the count 12 is a single atom that a non-randomised rule must take entirely or leave out. The largest non-randomised upper tail whose probability does not exceed the nominal level $0.05$ is therefore $\{13,14,15\}$, and its size is the probability of that smaller tail. The value $0.05$ is the ceiling used to select the region, chosen before the critical value is applied to the data. Rejecting with probability one on $\{13,14,15\}$ and with probability approximately $0.36105$ when $X=12$ spends the rest of the ceiling, so the type I error equals $0.05$. The size of the non-randomised region, and its power at $p=0.9$, are computed in the exercise below.{{cite:rudloff_karatzas2010}} (p. 1226, size bounded by the significance level){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 251, significance level chosen before the critical value)

**Your turn.** Seven observations are used to test $H_0:\sigma^2=1.1$ against $\sigma^2<1.1$ at level 0.05, rejecting for small $s^2$. The critical point supplied for the calculation is 1.635, which is $\chi^2_{6;0.05}$ rounded to three decimal places. Find the largest $s^2$ that still rejects.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
Reject when $6s^2/1.1\le1.635$, so $s^2\le1.635\times1.1/6=0.29975$, which rounds to $0.2998$. Any larger sample variance is outside the region defined by this rounded critical point. The region defined by the unrounded chi-squared quantile has probability $0.05$ under the simple null $\sigma^2=1.1$, so for that exact quantile the size equals the chosen level. The level assigns no probability to the hypothesis that the variance is 1.1.
:::

## Common misconceptions
The significance level is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true after a rejection, and it is not the $p$-value. A nominal level of $0.05$ is a ceiling on the supremum of the null rejection probability. Equality of that supremum with $0.05$ does not mean that every true-null distribution rejects $5\%$ of its samples: the Bernoulli test above has size $0.05$, and no null success probability attains it. A discrete non-randomised test can have size strictly below the same ceiling. A $p$-value of 0.04 and a level of 0.05 are different objects: one is computed from the sample, the other was chosen as a cutoff. A result that is not significant at 0.05 is not evidence that the null is true; the test was built to limit false rejections, not to limit missed effects. And 0.05 is a convention NIST and many textbooks record as common, not a law of nature. A different scientific cost can justify 0.01 or 0.10, provided the choice is made with the region, not after the $p$-value is known.{{cite:nist_mean_limits2002}} (§1.3.5.2, choice of $\alpha$){{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 250–251, level as a step of the procedure)

## Assumptions and limits — When the advertised level is not the error rate
The unrounded $t$ quantile has rejection probability $\alpha$ when the observations are independent and normal and the null is the simple mean used in the statistic. A composite null for that mean can assign a smaller rejection probability to values inside the null. Dependence, or a badly skewed population in a small sample, moves the true rejection probability away from $\alpha$. The binomial region in the exercise is a different limit, visible with no modelling surprise. The statistic is discrete, so a non-randomised threshold on $X$ cannot be given probability exactly $0.05$. Quoting $0.05$ as its size would overstate how often that simple null is rejected. A second limit is optional stopping. If the tester peeks and adds observations until the $p$-value drops below 0.05, the probability of eventually rejecting a true null is larger than 0.05. The level attaches to a region that was fixed, including the sample size.{{cite:nist_mean_limits2002}} (§1.3.5.2, the level is a property of the method){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 251, region fixed before the decision)

## Connections
The [critical region](critical-region.qmd) is the set on which the test rejects. The supremum of its probability over the null is the size, and the significance level is the ceiling placed on that supremum. The supremum need not be attained.{{cite:rudloff_karatzas2010}} (p. 1226, (2.1)) [Type I and type II errors](type-i-and-type-ii-errors.qmd) name a false rejection and a missed rejection. The [p-value](p-value.qmd) is the smallest level at which the observed sample rejects. A confidence set of level $1-\alpha$ obtained by inverting one matching family of level-$\alpha$ tests contains the parameter values those tests do not reject.{{cite:siegrist2022_testing}} (§9.1, Equivalence Between Hypothesis Test and Confidence Sets) The [Neyman–Pearson approach](neyman-pearson-approach.qmd) fixes a ceiling on the type I error and then looks for a region with small type II error.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 250–251, type I and type II errors in the 1933 paper)

### Power of a serve test {#self-check-ex-inf-serve-power}

Serves follow $\mathrm{Bin}(15,p)$. The test of $p=0.6$ against $p>0.6$ rejects for $X\in\{13,14,15\}$. What is the size, and what is the power at $p=0.9$?

[Exercise](../questions/ex-inf-serve-power.qmd)

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The size is $\Pr(X\ge13)$ at $p=0.6$. The sum of the three binomial probabilities is approximately $0.027114$, or $2.7114\%$ when written as a percentage to four decimal places. The next larger tail has probability approximately $0.090502$, and the atom at $X=12$ has probability approximately $0.063388$, so the two tails lie on opposite sides of $0.05$. The power at $p=0.9$ is approximately $0.8159$. The nominal level $0.05$ is the ceiling under which this non-randomised region was selected.
:::

## History
Talk of the significance or insignificance of a batch of observations goes back to the eighteenth century, including Arbuthnot (1710), Daniel Bernoulli (1734) and Laplace (1773), and such calculations became more common in the nineteenth century. Neyman and Pearson's 1928 *Biometrika* paper set a null against an alternative, and their 1933 paper distinguished a type I error from a type II error. In that procedure the significance level is the allowed probability of the type I error, fixed when the rejection region is chosen.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 250–251, History and mathematical steps)
