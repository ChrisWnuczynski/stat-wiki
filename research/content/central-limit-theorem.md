## Lead context
The theorem concerns the *fluctuations* of a sum or average. The law of large numbers says that an average settles near its mean; the central limit theorem describes the shape of the residual error after scaling by the square root of the sample size. This is why normal approximations arise in estimates and tests even when individual measurements have no normal distribution.{{cite:siegrist2022_clt}} (§5.4, “The Central Limit Theorem”; “Normal Approximations”) {{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 9, §§18, 20)

## Statement
Writing $\overline X_n=S_n/n$ gives the equivalent form $\sqrt n(\overline X_n-\mu)/\sigma\Rightarrow Z$, where $Z\sim\operatorname N(0,1)$. The arrow refers to convergence of distributions, not convergence of the random quantity to a particular normal draw. Because $\Phi$ is continuous everywhere, the CDF statement holds at every real $x$. The same theorem applies whether the parent law is discrete or continuous, provided the specified independent and identically distributed variables have $0<\sigma^2<\infty$.{{cite:siegrist2022_clt}} (§5.4, “The Central Limit Theorem”) {{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 9, §20, Thm. 29)

## Motivating example
Suppose 25 independent components each have an exponential lifetime with mean two hours. A single lifetime is strongly right-skewed; the total lifetime of 25 sequential components is much less skewed. Its mean is $25(2)=50$ hours and its standard deviation is $\sqrt{25(2^2)}=10$ hours. The theorem suggests measuring a proposed total relative to 50 in units of 10. The histogram compares standardised sums of uniforms and exponentials: both approach a bell shape, but the exponential sum remains visibly skewed at the displayed sample sizes. The approximation's quality depends on the parent law and the particular probability being requested.{{cite:siegrist2022_clt}} (§5.4, “Moments”; “Normal Approximations”)

## Intuition
For independent summands, means and variances add. The sum therefore has mean $n\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt n$. Dividing its deviation from $n\mu$ by $\sigma\sqrt n$ keeps the scale from either collapsing to zero or expanding without bound. The sample mean has standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt n$; its raw distribution concentrates at $\mu$, while its standardised error retains a nontrivial limiting shape. The normal curve is that limiting shape for the stated model.{{cite:siegrist2022_clt}} (§5.4, “Moments”; “The Central Limit Theorem”)

The shape does not emerge because each observation becomes normal. An individual exponential draw stays exponential as more observations are collected. The statement is about a newly constructed sum or mean at each sample size. Neither does the theorem say that every sufficiently large finite sample has an exactly normal mean. Strong skew, rare events, and probabilities far into a tail can demand a much larger sample than a nearly symmetric parent distribution does. The two figures make these two distinct limits visible: the sum's shape improves with $n$, while a normal CDF can still miss the staircase of a discrete count unless a continuity correction is used.{{cite:siegrist2022_clt}} (§5.4, “The Central Limit Theorem”; “Normal Approximations”) {{cite:siegrist2022_binomial_distribution}} (§11.2, “The Normal Approximation”)

## Key results
The scaling identities are exact: $\mathbb E S_n=n\mu$, $\operatorname{Var}(S_n)=n\sigma^2$, $\mathbb E\overline X_n=\mu$, and $\operatorname{Var}(\overline X_n)=\sigma^2/n$. The normal *distributional* statements are asymptotic. Thus $S_n\approx\operatorname N(n\mu,n\sigma^2)$ and $\overline X_n\approx\operatorname N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)$ are shorthand for the standardised limit when $n$ is large enough for the question at hand. The variance argument itself requires no normal approximation; independence is what removes covariance terms.{{cite:siegrist2022_clt}} (§5.4, “Moments”; “Normal Approximations”) {{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 9, §20, Thm. 29)

For a Bernoulli parent variable with success probability $p\in(0,1)$, the sum $B_n$ is binomial. Its mean and variance are $np$ and $np(1-p)$, so $(B_n-np)/\sqrt{np(1-p)}\Rightarrow\operatorname N(0,1)$. For an integer threshold $k$, the event $B_n\le k$ is the same event as $B_n<k+1/2$; comparing the normal CDF at $k+1/2$ often reduces the finite-sample error. The continuity correction is a numerical improvement to a discrete approximation, not part of the limit theorem's hypothesis.{{cite:siegrist2022_binomial_distribution}} (§11.2, “The Normal Approximation”) {{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 9, §20, Example 30)

## Proof
**Proof idea.** The characteristic function of one centred, variance-one summand looks like $1-t^2/2+o(t^2)$ near zero. Independence raises its value at $t/\sqrt n$ to the $n$th power. That product tends to $e^{-t^2/2}$, the characteristic function of a standard normal. This route needs only a finite second moment; a moment-generating function need not exist.{{cite:siegrist2022_clt}} (§5.4, “The Central Limit Theorem,” proof)

Put $Y_i=(X_i-\mu)/\sigma$. Then $\mathbb E Y_i=0$, $\mathbb E Y_i^2=1$, and the $Y_i$ remain independent and identically distributed. Define $\varphi(t)=\mathbb E e^{itY_1}$. Finite second moment lets us differentiate twice at zero: $\varphi(0)=1$, $\varphi'(0)=i\mathbb E Y_1=0$, and $\varphi''(0)=-\mathbb E Y_1^2=-1$. Consequently $\varphi(u)=1-u^2/2+o(u^2)$ as $u\to0$. No assumption that $\mathbb E e^{sY_1}$ is finite for real $s\ne0$ was used.{{cite:siegrist2022_clt}} (§5.4, “The Central Limit Theorem,” characteristic-function proof)

The standardised sum is $Z_n=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^nY_i$. Independence gives its characteristic function
$$
\varphi_{Z_n}(t)=\{\varphi(t/\sqrt n)\}^n
=\left(1-\frac{t^2}{2n}+o(n^{-1})\right)^n
\longrightarrow e^{-t^2/2}
$$
for each fixed real $t$. The last expression is the characteristic function of $Z\sim\operatorname N(0,1)$. Lévy's continuity theorem therefore gives $Z_n\Rightarrow Z$, which is the displayed CDF limit because the normal CDF has no discontinuities. The proof explains why finite variance is enough in the iid case and why changing the dependence or the scale demands a different theorem.{{cite:siegrist2022_clt}} (§5.4, “The Central Limit Theorem,” characteristic-function proof)

## Generalizations
For independent random vectors with finite covariance matrix $\Sigma$, the multivariate theorem gives $\sqrt n(\overline{\mathbf X}_n-\boldsymbol\mu)\Rightarrow\operatorname N_p(\mathbf0,\Sigma)$. Apply the one-dimensional theorem to every fixed linear combination $\mathbf a^\top\mathbf X_i$ and then use the Cramér–Wold device. A singular $\Sigma$ gives a normal limit concentrated on a lower-dimensional subspace. This is an extension of the same averaging idea, but the sample mean is a vector and the limiting spread is a covariance matrix.{{cite:siegrist2022_clt}} (§5.4, “The Central Limit Theorem”; “Normal Approximations”)

If $\sigma$ is unknown and the sample standard deviation $s_n$ is consistent for positive $\sigma$, Slutsky's theorem permits replacing $\sigma$ in the standardised mean by $s_n$ without changing the standard-normal limit. This is an *asymptotic* statement. The exact Student-$t$ distribution for a finite normal sample uses additional normal-sample structure. Independent variables that are not identically distributed can also obey a central limit theorem, but conditions such as Lindeberg's or Lyapunov's prevent a few terms from dominating the total variance. The iid theorem alone does not justify that extension.{{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 9, §21.2, Thm. 34 and Example 35) 

## Related concepts
The [law of large numbers](law-of-large-numbers.qmd) controls the unscaled average. The [normal distribution](normal-distribution.qmd) is the limiting law after centring and scaling; it is also an exact sampling law for sums of independent normal observations. [Modes of convergence](modes-of-convergence.qmd) distinguish convergence in distribution from stronger notions. [Characteristic functions](characteristic-functions.qmd) give the proof, and [Lévy's continuity theorem](levy-s-continuity-theorem.qmd) translates the limiting characteristic function into distributional convergence. The [multivariate central limit theorem](multivariate-central-limit-theorem.qmd) handles vector averages.{{cite:siegrist2022_clt}} (§5.4, “The Central Limit Theorem”) 

## Statistical use
For a mean or total, calculate the *sampling* mean and standard deviation before consulting a normal table. For the mean, standardise with $\sigma/\sqrt n$; for the total, use $\sigma\sqrt n$. Replacing one with the other gives a factor-of-$n$ error. For a binomial count, check the success and failure expectations and use a continuity correction when a discrete threshold matters. If the exact distribution is available and the sample is small, the exact calculation can be more informative than the approximation; the $\operatorname{Bin}(20,1/2)$ example shows both side by side.{{cite:siegrist2022_clt}} (§5.4, “Normal Approximations”) {{cite:siegrist2022_binomial_distribution}} (§11.2, “The Normal Approximation”)

Normal approximations support large-sample intervals and tests, but the theorem does not validate a badly designed sample. Dependent repeated measurements or data from changing populations need their own variance and limiting arguments. The theorem also does not deliver a universal sample size such as 30: approximation accuracy depends on the underlying law and which tail or interval is being estimated. When observations are exactly normal and independent, their mean is exactly normal for every $n$, by a separate normal-sum result.{{cite:siegrist2022_clt}} (§5.4, “Normal Approximations”) {{cite:siegrist2022_normals}} (§6.8, “The One Sample Model”)

## Common misconceptions
The central limit theorem does not say that the data histogram becomes normal, that $\overline X_n$ converges to a random normal value, or that the normal curve is exact after a named sample size. The law of large numbers sends the *unscaled* average toward the fixed $\mu$; the central limit theorem gives a normal law for the *scaled error*. A parent law with infinite variance is outside this iid finite-variance statement, and a moment-generating function is not needed for the characteristic-function proof.{{cite:siegrist2022_clt}} (§5.4, “The Central Limit Theorem”; “Normal Approximations”)

## History
De Moivre obtained a normal approximation to binomial probabilities in 1733. Laplace developed the approximation further, including its role in the theory of observational errors. These were precursors of the general statement about sums. The modern theorem includes much broader classes of summands, with conditions that make the normal limit precise.{{cite:daw1966_normal_history}} (pp. 3–4) {{cite:oliveira2022_probability_history}} (§9, pp. e20220087-15–16) {{cite:siegrist2022_clt}} (§5.4, “The Central Limit Theorem”)

## Worked example
Let $X_1,\ldots,X_{25}$ be independent exponential lifetimes with mean two hours and variance four square hours. Their total $S_{25}$ has exact mean 50 and standard deviation 10. To approximate $\mathbb P(S_{25}>60)$, standardise the boundary: $(60-50)/10=1$. The central limit theorem gives $\mathbb P(S_{25}>60)\approx1-\Phi(1)\approx0.1587$. This is a probability about the total, not about one exponential lifetime. In fact $S_{25}$ is Gamma with shape 25 and rate $1/2$, so its exact tail is $\Pr\{\operatorname{Poisson}(30)\le24\}\approx0.1572$. The closeness is a feature of this example, not a universal bound for $n=25$.{{cite:siegrist2022_clt}} (§5.4, “Normal Approximations”) {{cite:wang2025_foundations}} (Ch. 2, §3.9.5, Example 65, Thm. 66)

**Your turn.** Suppose instead that 36 independent lifetimes have the same exponential law. Approximate the probability that their *average* exceeds 2.5 hours. State the average's mean and standard deviation before standardising.{{cite:siegrist2022_clt}} (§5.4, “Normal Approximations”)

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The average has mean 2 hours and standard deviation $2/\sqrt{36}=1/3$ hour. Thus its standardised boundary is $(2.5-2)/(1/3)=1.5$, giving $\mathbb P(\overline X_{36}>2.5)\approx1-\Phi(1.5)\approx0.0668$. The average's scale is $2/\sqrt{36}$, while the total's scale would be $2\sqrt{36}$.
:::
