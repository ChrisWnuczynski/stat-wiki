## Motivating example
A classifier can assign a patient a risk of $0.49$ and another a risk of $0.51$, yet a default half-probability cutoff gives them different labels. The dividing set is the **decision boundary**: locations where the preferred class changes. Reading that boundary alongside the probabilities matters because a small change in the decision threshold can move the boundary without changing the fitted model. In logistic regression the geometry is particularly clear: the linear predictor orders cases, and the logistic curve converts its scores into probabilities.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 4, §§4.3.1–4.3.3, pp. 131–134)

## Intuition
Let $p(x)=\Pr(Y=1\mid X=x)$ and write the fitted linear predictor as $\eta(x)=\beta_0+\boldsymbol\beta^\top x$. The logistic model has $p(x)=1/(1+e^{-\eta(x)})$. It is linear in *log odds*, not in probability: moving one unit along coordinate $x_j$ adds $\beta_j$ to $\eta$ and multiplies the odds by $e^{\beta_j}$ when other coordinates are held fixed. The same shift in score produces a smaller probability change near zero or one than near one half, where the curve is steepest. Thus a coefficient describes a conditional association on a specified scale, while the boundary describes the action taken after a threshold is chosen.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 4, §4.3.1, pp. 132–133)

For equal costs of the two errors, a calibrated probability above one half favours class $1$. Since the logistic function increases strictly, this means $\eta(x)\ge0$. In the unexpanded predictors the equality $\eta(x)=0$ is a hyperplane when $\boldsymbol\beta\ne0$. Its normal vector $\boldsymbol\beta$ sets its orientation, and $\beta_0$ sets its location. A zero slope vector leaves the score constant, so there is no separating hyperplane to draw.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 4, §§4.3.1 and 4.4.3, pp. 132–133, 142–144)

## Interpretation — Decision thresholds and costs
A probability threshold $t$ between zero and one puts the logistic boundary at $\eta(x)=\log\{t/(1-t)\}$; moving $t$ translates a linear boundary while preserving its orientation. If a false positive costs $C_{\rm FP}$ and a false negative costs $C_{\rm FN}$, with correct decisions costing zero, classifying as $1$ has conditional risk $C_{\rm FP}(1-p)$ and classifying as $0$ has risk $C_{\rm FN}p$. Choose class $1$ when $p\ge C_{\rm FP}/(C_{\rm FP}+C_{\rm FN})$. This rule assumes calibrated probabilities and costs measured on the same scale. A medical screening rule with a high missed-case cost may therefore use a threshold well below one half without changing the estimated disease probability.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 4, §4.4.1, pp. 138–139, Bayes classification; threshold and cost formula derived below)

Varying a threshold traces a receiver operating characteristic, or ROC, curve. For a fixed fitted score, each point pairs the false-positive rate among true class-$0$ cases with the true-positive rate among true class-$1$ cases. These rates depend on score distributions *within* classes. If only the class prevalence changes while those conditional score distributions stay fixed, the ROC curve remains fixed, although the posterior probabilities, predictive values, and cost-optimal threshold can change. This is a precise meaning of pure prior shift, not a promise under arbitrary dataset shift.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 4, §4.4.1, pp. 138–139, class priors and conditional densities)

## Properties — Why linear discriminant analysis can have the same shape
Logistic regression fits $\Pr(Y\mid X)$ directly. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) instead models $X\mid Y$ as Gaussian in each class, with separate means and a common covariance matrix, then applies Bayes' rule. The common quadratic term in the two normal log densities cancels, leaving posterior log odds affine in $x$. Therefore a two-class LDA model implies a logistic-shaped posterior and a linear boundary at any fixed posterior threshold. It does not imply that the fitted coefficients coincide: LDA estimates means, covariance and priors, whereas logistic regression maximizes a conditional likelihood.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 4, §§4.4.1–4.4.3 and 4.5, pp. 138–144, 151–152)

If the two Gaussian classes have different covariance matrices, their quadratic terms generally do not cancel. A curved or disconnected boundary may then be appropriate, as in quadratic discriminant analysis. Conversely, a logistic model can still produce a curved boundary in the *original* features if squared terms or interactions are included in its linear predictor. “Linear” refers to the chosen feature representation and coefficients; the transformation from raw measurements matters.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 4, §§4.4.4–4.5, pp. 149–152)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
From $p=e^\eta/(1+e^\eta)$, the odds are $p/(1-p)=e^\eta$. Replacing $x_j$ by $x_j+1$ adds $\beta_j$ to $\eta$ and multiplies the odds by $e^{\beta_j}$. Since the logit is the inverse of the strictly increasing logistic function, $p=t$ if and only if $\eta=\log\{t/(1-t)\}$. Comparing $C_{\rm FP}(1-p)$ with $C_{\rm FN}p$ yields the stated cost threshold. The one-half rule follows when the two costs agree.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 4, §§4.3.1 and 4.4.1, pp. 132–133, 138–139)

For $X\mid Y=k\sim N(\mu_k,\Sigma)$ and prior $\pi_k$, Bayes' rule gives $\log\{p_1(x)/p_0(x)\}=\log(\pi_1/\pi_0)+\log\{f_1(x)/f_0(x)\}$. Expanding the normal exponents cancels $x^\top\Sigma^{-1}x$ and leaves $x^\top\Sigma^{-1}(\mu_1-\mu_0)-\tfrac12(\mu_1+\mu_0)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(\mu_1-\mu_0)+\log(\pi_1/\pi_0)$, affine in $x$. With unequal covariance matrices the two quadratic forms need not cancel. If $f_1,f_0$ stay fixed between source and target populations, subtracting the two Bayes log-odds equations leaves $\operatorname{logit}\pi_t-\operatorname{logit}\pi_s$. Finally, at score cutoff $c$, $\mathrm{FPR}(c)=\Pr(S\ge c\mid Y=0)$ and $\mathrm{TPR}(c)=\Pr(S\ge c\mid Y=1)$; stable class-conditional score laws make both unchanged by a prevalence shift.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 4, §§4.4.1–4.4.4, pp. 138–150)

## Worked example
Suppose a binary logistic model uses $\eta(x)=-2+x_1+0.5x_2$. At $x=(1,1)$, the score is $-0.5$, so the probability is $1/(1+e^{0.5})\approx0.3775$ and the odds are $e^{-0.5}\approx0.6065$. Increasing $x_1$ to $2$ gives score $0.5$, probability about $0.6225$ and odds about $1.6487$. The odds ratio is $e\approx2.7183$, while the probability increased by about $0.2449$, not by a fixed coefficient. At threshold $0.5$ the boundary is $x_1+0.5x_2=2$ and the new point is class $1$. At threshold $0.7$ the boundary moves to $x_1+0.5x_2=2+\log(0.7/0.3)\approx2.8473$, so that *same* point is class $0$. The model's probability did not change; the decision rule did.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 4, §4.3.1, pp. 132–133, log odds and classification)

**Your turn.** A model gives a case probability $p=0.2$. A one-unit change in a predictor multiplies its odds by $3$. Find the new probability. If the false-negative cost is four times the false-positive cost, what decision threshold minimizes conditional expected cost, assuming the probabilities are calibrated?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The old odds are $0.2/0.8=1/4$; multiplying by $3$ gives odds $3/4$ and new probability $(3/4)/(1+3/4)=3/7\approx0.4286$. With $C_{\rm FN}=4C_{\rm FP}$, the optimal threshold is $C_{\rm FP}/(C_{\rm FP}+C_{\rm FN})=1/5=0.2$. This case is classified as class $1$ under the cost-based rule, both before and after the predictor change, with ties assigned to class $1$.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 4, §§4.3.1 and 4.4.1, pp. 132–133, 138–139)
:::

## Common misconceptions
The coefficient $\beta_j$ is a change in log odds, not a constant change in probability or a relative risk. A linear boundary does not mean the fitted probability is linear in the predictors: the logistic curve remains nonlinear. A linear LDA boundary does not make LDA and logistic regression the same estimation procedure. An unchanged ROC curve under pure prior shift does not mean unchanged precision or unchanged optimal action, because prevalence enters posterior probabilities. Finally, a conditional odds association does not establish a causal effect from intervening on a predictor.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 4, §§4.3.1 and 4.5, pp. 132–133, 151–152){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 390, odds versus risk distinction)

## Assumptions and limits — When the boundary misleads
If class $0$ is $N(0,1)$ and class $1$ is $N(0,4)$ with equal priors, class $0$ is favoured near zero and class $1$ in both tails. A single straight line in one dimension cannot make those two switches; unequal variances call for a quadratic score. A polynomial feature can repair some curved boundaries, but its coefficient is a contrast in that expanded feature, so a one-unit raw change need not have constant odds multiplier. If a screening population's prevalence changes from its training sample, using the old intercept as though posterior probabilities were unchanged can put the cost boundary in the wrong place. And if probabilities are poorly calibrated, a theoretically optimal cost threshold need not be operationally optimal.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 4, §§4.4.4–4.5, pp. 149–152)

## Connections
The odds multiplier explains logistic coefficients, while the boundary converts probability estimates into labels. LDA reaches a similar boundary through class-conditional normal densities and Bayes' rule. A threshold belongs to decision theory: it reflects error costs and prevalence as well as the model. ROC analysis varies that threshold and reports conditional error rates; it cannot alone supply the cost of a wrong decision. These distinctions let the same fitted score be used for different decisions without claiming that its probabilities or scientific associations changed.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 4, §§4.3–4.5, pp. 131–152)

## History
Joseph Berkson introduced the logistic function for bioassay in 1944, and David Cox developed regression analysis for binary sequences in 1958. Their work made the log-odds scale a practical way to express a binary response as a function of several predictors. The later discriminant-analysis comparison shows why distinct routes—conditional logistic likelihood and Gaussian class modelling—can produce boundaries with the same linear geometry.{{cite:msaouel2022_cox}} ("Editorial", paras. 2–3){{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 4, §4.5, pp. 151–152)
