## Motivating example
The figure compares two ways to report 95% of the same right-skewed $\operatorname{Beta}(3,9)$ posterior. An equal-tailed interval discards 2.5% at each end; a highest-density region keeps the parameter values with the greatest posterior density until their total probability reaches 95%. Both statements use the same posterior and have the same probability content, but their endpoints differ because the density is asymmetric. That choice matters when a boundary, skewness or multiple peaks make “the middle” different from “the most concentrated part.”{{cite:liu_gelman_zheng2015}} (§1, Fig. 1, pp. 809–810){{cite:siegrist2022_bayesian_set_estimation}} (§8.5, Real Parameters)

## Intuition — How posterior mass defines a region
A credible region is a set of parameter values that carries a chosen fraction of the probability distribution *after* observing the data. Probability content alone leaves many sets possible. Tail allocation gives an easily reproduced interval; minimum width gives a compact interval in a chosen parameter scale; a density threshold gives a set of high-density values that may have several components. The choice should match what the summary is meant to convey: quantiles are stable and easy to compare, while a highest-density set emphasizes local concentration.{{cite:liu_gelman_zheng2015}} (§§1–2, pp. 809–811)

## Definition
For a proper posterior distribution $\pi(\theta\mid x)$ and desired content $1-\alpha$, any measurable $C(x)$ satisfying $\mathbb{P}\{\theta\in C(x)\mid x\}=1-\alpha$ is a credible region. For continuous scalar $\theta$ with posterior distribution function $F$, the **equal-tailed interval** is $[F^{-1}(\alpha/2),F^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)]$. A **shortest interval** minimizes $u-l$ among intervals $[l,u]$ with the required content. A **highest-posterior-density (HPD) region** is a superlevel set $\{\theta:\pi(\theta\mid x)\ge c\}$, with $c$ chosen for the required content where possible. For a continuous unimodal posterior, the HPD region and shortest interval coincide; if the posterior is multimodal, the HPD region can be disconnected.{{cite:liu_gelman_zheng2015}} (§§1–2.1, pp. 809–811)

## Properties
For a continuous symmetric unimodal posterior, the equal-tailed and HPD intervals coincide. Under skewness, the HPD interval shifts toward the denser side, so its two tails generally have unequal probabilities. When both HPD endpoints lie inside the support and the density is smooth, they have equal posterior density; this is the condition that balances a small movement of one endpoint against the other. A boundary endpoint need not have a matching-density partner: for a decreasing exponential density, the HPD interval starts exactly at zero.{{cite:liu_gelman_zheng2015}} (§§1–2.1, eq. 1, pp. 809–811)

Equal-tailed quantiles transform naturally under a strictly increasing reparameterisation: applying $g$ to both endpoints gives the equal-tailed interval for $g(\theta)$. A density threshold and minimum length depend on the chosen coordinate. The transformed density includes a Jacobian, which can change where the density is highest; an HPD interval for a variance need not transform into the HPD interval for its logarithm. A meaningful scale for the scientific question should therefore be chosen before seeking “shortest.”{{cite:liu_gelman_zheng2015}} (§1, p. 809)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Let $f$ be a continuous positive posterior density with distribution function $F$. For $l<u$, the interval content is $F(u)-F(l)$. At fixed content $1-\alpha$, $F(u)-F(l)=1-\alpha$ implies $du/dl=f(l)/f(u)$, so $d(u-l)/dl=f(l)/f(u)-1$. An interior shortest interval therefore has $f(l)=f(u)$; otherwise a small endpoint shift shortens it. If $f$ is unimodal, its density superlevel sets are intervals, and choosing the threshold to give the required content produces such an interval. More generally, if $H=\{f\ge c\}$ and another measurable set $A$ has the same posterior mass, then $\int_{H\setminus A}f=\int_{A\setminus H}f$. Since $f\ge c$ on the first difference and $f\le c$ on the second, $|H\setminus A|\le|A\setminus H|$, hence $|H|\le|A|$; ties at the threshold can make the minimizer nonunique. If $Y=g(\theta)$ with $g$ strictly increasing, then $\mathbb{P}\{Y\le g(t)\mid x\}=F(t)$, proving the equal-tail transformation rule. Its density is $f(g^{-1}(y))/g'(g^{-1}(y))$ when $g'>0$, showing why density order, and thus HPD membership, can change.{{cite:liu_gelman_zheng2015}} (§§1–2.1, eq. 1, pp. 809–811)
:::
:::

## Interpretation
The posterior probability statement concerns the unknown parameter conditional on the observed data, prior and likelihood. A frequentist confidence interval instead describes coverage of a random procedure under repeated samples at a fixed parameter. Numerically identical endpoints do not make those probability statements interchangeable. The equal-tail and highest-density worked question illustrates both constructions explicitly; the tank-production example shows a posterior whose support has a data-dependent lower boundary.{{cite:siegrist2022_bayesian_set_estimation}} (§8.5, Confidence Sets and Real Parameters)

## Worked example
Suppose a nonnegative parameter has posterior density $f(\theta\mid x)=2e^{-2\theta}$ for $\theta\ge0$. Its distribution function is $F(t)=1-e^{-2t}$. For 90% content, the equal-tailed interval leaves 5% below and 5% above, giving $[-\log(0.95)/2,-\log(0.05)/2]\approx[0.02565,1.49787]$. The density decreases from the boundary, so the 90% HPD interval starts at zero and ends where $F(u)=0.90$: $[0,-\log(0.10)/2]\approx[0,1.15129]$. Both contain 90% posterior probability; the second is shorter on the $\theta$ scale and includes the mode at zero.{{cite:liu_gelman_zheng2015}} (§1, boundary example, pp. 809–810)

**Your turn.** For a rate-one exponential posterior on $\theta\ge0$, find the 80% equal-tailed and HPD intervals. State why their lower endpoints differ.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
Here $F(t)=1-e^{-t}$. The equal-tailed interval cuts 10% from each tail, so it is $[-\log(0.90),-\log(0.10)]\approx[0.10536,2.30259]$. The density is largest at zero and decreases, so the 80% HPD interval is $[0,-\log(0.20)]\approx[0,1.60944]$. The equal-tail rule removes low-parameter probability even though those values have the highest density.
:::

## Common misconceptions
“95% credible” does not identify a unique interval: the rule for choosing its set is an additional decision. “Highest density” does not require a single interval; two separated peaks can produce two separated components. “Shortest” is not invariant to units or nonlinear transformations, because length is measured on the chosen parameter scale. Nor is the HPD set necessarily more useful: a central interval may communicate quantiles more clearly, and a single interval may conceal a multimodal posterior.{{cite:liu_gelman_zheng2015}} (§§1–2, pp. 809–811, 814–815)

## Assumptions and limits
A proper posterior is needed before “90% posterior probability” is meaningful. A posterior proportional to $1/\theta$ over all $\theta>0$ cannot be normalized, so none of these 90% sets exists. For a two-peak mixture, a shortest *single interval* may include a low-density gap, while an HPD *region* may split; treating the two as synonyms would misdescribe the posterior. For discrete or flat-topped distributions, an exact density-threshold set of a prescribed probability may not exist or may be nonunique; one must specify how ties or excess mass are handled. With only a small effective number of posterior draws, the empirically shortest window can jump between samples, even when equal-tail quantiles are fairly stable.{{cite:liu_gelman_zheng2015}} (§§1–2, pp. 809–811)

## History
Highest-density regions were advocated in the pre-simulation literature, including Box and Tiao's 1973 treatment. As posterior simulation became routine, central intervals gained practical appeal because empirical quantiles are straightforward, while empirical shortest intervals can have high Monte Carlo error. Liu, Gelman and Zheng revisited that computational problem in 2015 and proposed a smoothed shortest-interval estimator. The distinction between the interval rules therefore reflects both their meaning and their computational history.{{cite:liu_gelman_zheng2015}} (§§1–2 and 6, pp. 809–811, 817)
