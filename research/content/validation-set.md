## Motivating example
A researcher trains several classifiers for the same patients. Each can report a tiny training error by adapting to its own cases, so those numbers give little basis for choosing which rule will work on future patients. Reserving cases whose outcomes did not influence fitting provides a trial of each candidate. The trial becomes part of model selection once its results determine the winner. That role explains why a third, untouched sample is needed for a final performance estimate.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (§5.1.1, pp. 176–178){{cite:cawley_talbot2010}} (§1, pp. 2079–2081)

## Intuition — A trial used for choosing is spent
Think of a validation score as a noisy measurement of future error. Its noise comes from the particular cases in the split and, often, from fitting the candidate on fewer cases than will be available later. Trying many settings increases the opportunity to choose a setting whose score is unusually favourable by chance. The best recorded score therefore answers “which candidate looked best on this validation set?”; it does not independently answer “how well will the chosen procedure work on a new sample?” In the *Auto* example, changing the random half-split changed the estimated error curves, even though the broad preference for a quadratic over a linear fit was stable.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (§5.1.1, pp. 176–178){{cite:cawley_talbot2010}} (§1, pp. 2079–2081)

## Statistical use — Keep the roles separate
First divide observations into training, validation and test parts using a plan appropriate to how future cases will arrive; a time-ordered prediction problem calls for a time-respecting split. Fit preprocessing, feature selection and each candidate model using training data alone. Apply those fitted transformations to the validation cases, calculate the prespecified loss there, and select a tuning setting. The selected setting may be refitted using the training and validation cases together. Evaluate that fixed refit once on the untouched test cases, reporting the metric and test sample size. The test cases cannot influence a later threshold, feature choice or choice among reported models without becoming part of selection.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (§5.1.1, pp. 176–178){{cite:cawley_talbot2010}} (§5.3, pp. 2100–2103){{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§5.10, time series cross-validation)

The split must be at the level of independent units. If repeated visits by one patient occur on both sides, a classifier can recognize the patient rather than learn a rule for new patients. If a future forecast is the goal, allowing later observations into training or scaling statistics for an earlier validation period leaks future information. In either case, a small error estimates the wrong prediction task.{{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§5.10, rolling forecasting origin)

## Interpretation — What the estimate means
For a fixed fitted rule $f$ and independent validation observations $(X_i,Y_i)$ from the target population, the mean validation loss $\widehat R_V(f)=m^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^m L(Y_i,f(X_i))$ estimates the conditional risk $R(f)=\mathbb E[L(Y,f(X))\mid f]$. This statement conditions on the rule having been fitted without the validation outcomes. It does not say that the minimum across many validation scores is unbiased for the risk of the selected rule. Nor does it say that a rule refitted on more cases has precisely the same risk as the smaller training fit.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (§5.1.1, pp. 176–178){{cite:cawley_talbot2010}} (§1, pp. 2079–2081)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Conditional on a fixed training-fitted $f$, independence and identical distribution of the $m$ validation cases give $\mathbb E[\widehat R_V(f)\mid f]=m^{-1}\sum_i\mathbb E[L(Y_i,f(X_i))\mid f]=R(f)$. Now take any finite collection of fixed candidates $f_1,\ldots,f_J$. Pointwise, $\min_j\widehat R_V(f_j)\le\widehat R_V(f_k)$ for every $k$. Taking expectations and then the smallest upper bound gives $\mathbb E[\min_j\widehat R_V(f_j)]\le\min_k R(f_k)$. Thus the winning observed score has a downward selection tendency relative even to the best fixed candidate's risk. The inequality can be strict; it does not assert that every realized winning score is below a future test score.{{cite:cawley_talbot2010}} (§1, pp. 2079–2081)
:::
:::

## Worked example — Choosing by twelve cases
Suppose $60$ independent labelled cases are divided into $36$ training, $12$ validation and $12$ test cases. Fit classifiers A and B on the training cases. A makes $3$ validation mistakes, an error rate of $3/12=0.25$; B makes $1$, an error rate of $1/12\approx0.0833$. Choose B. If B then makes $4$ mistakes on the untouched test cases, its observed test error is $4/12\approx0.3333$. The test result is not proof that B's true error exceeds A's, and the two rates need not be ordered this way in every split. It shows why B's unusually good selected validation result should not be reported as a fresh estimate. With only twelve cases in each assessment block, both estimates are coarse.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (§5.1.1, pp. 176–178){{cite:cawley_talbot2010}} (§1, pp. 2079–2081)

**Your turn.** A third classifier C, fitted on the same $36$ training cases, makes $2$ mistakes on the $12$ validation cases. Under the stated lowest-error rule, which candidate is selected, and can you report its validation error as an independent test error?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
C's validation error is $2/12\approx0.1667$, between B's $0.0833$ and A's $0.25$, so B remains selected. Its $0.0833$ validation error was used to make that choice and is not an independent test estimate. The untouched test block is the place to estimate the selected procedure's performance.
:::

## Common misconceptions
Holding validation cases out of the numerical optimization is insufficient if their outcomes guided which predictors, transformations or stopping epoch were chosen; they participated in fitting the overall procedure. Conversely, a validation set is not “bad data” after selection: it can often be used for the final refit once choices are frozen, provided the independent test set remains untouched. A selected validation score is not guaranteed to be lower than a particular future test score; optimism describes a repeated-selection effect, not an ordering for every sample.{{cite:cawley_talbot2010}} (§§1, 5.3, pp. 2079–2081, 2100–2103)

## Assumptions and limits — Unstable splits and small samples
A single split wastes fitting observations and can be unstable when the sample is small; in the *Auto* example, different random splits gave different error estimates. Stratification can preserve rare class counts in classification, but even a stratified validation set may be too small to measure rare-class sensitivity accurately. A random split of observations from a hospital does not establish performance at a new hospital with different patients or equipment. Cross-validation can use training-side data more efficiently, but tuning on its minimum still requires a separate outer assessment. A time-series forecast needs chronological evaluation instead of random case exchangeability.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (§5.1.1–5.1.3, pp. 176–183){{cite:cawley_talbot2010}} (§5.3, pp. 2100–2103){{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§5.10, rolling forecasting origin)

## History
Stone's 1974 paper explicitly joined the choice and assessment of statistical predictions in its title. Cawley and Talbot's 2010 study later demonstrated how optimizing a noisy selection criterion can bias subsequent performance evaluation, and discussed separating the two stages.{{cite:cawley_talbot2010}} (§1, pp. 2079–2081; §5.3, pp. 2100–2103; reference to Stone, p. 2106)
