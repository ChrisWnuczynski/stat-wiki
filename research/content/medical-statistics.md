## Lead context

A hospital trial asks whether a treatment helps the patients who receive it. A public-health question asks what is happening in a population: who becomes ill, where, and after what exposure. Medical statistics is the body of methods aimed at both, and at the biological measurements that feed them. The data are counts of deaths, laboratory values, images, and follow-up times. The conclusions are about diagnosis, treatment, and the safety of a trial, and they are only as sturdy as the design that produced them. A formula applied to a file of patients does not become a medical conclusion until the design, the outcome, and the missingness are part of the report.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 49, Biostatistics){{cite:schulz_altman_moher2010}} (Introduction)

## Intuition

Medicine records people who are not interchangeable. Two patients with the same diagnosis can differ in age, in the other drugs they take, and in how long they can be followed. A comparison that ignores those differences compares the patients, not the treatments. The statistical move is to build the comparison so that the treatment is the thing that differs, or, when that cannot be arranged, to say plainly that the comparison is observational and that an unmeasured common cause can remain. Random allocation is the design that makes the treatment the thing that differs, in expectation, before any model is fitted. A model fitted afterwards does not recreate that design.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 49, Biostatistics){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.4)

The same instinct applies to a single number. A laboratory value is a measurement with scatter and, sometimes, a systematic offset. A survival time is a time that may be cut short because follow-up ended, not because the event occurred. A positive diagnostic test is a classification, not the probability of disease. Each of those objects has a statistical summary that matches its design. Using the summary from a different design answers a different question.{{cite:schulz_altman_moher2010}} (Introduction)

## Definition

Medical statistics, or biostatistics in the narrower biomedical sense, is the application of statistical methods to questions in human biology and medicine. The prefix records the Greek word for life. The main domains are epidemiology, clinical trials, and biological trials, together with the ethical constraints those trials carry. Indirect measurement of how long a substance persists in a living body is one of the older biological uses. The field is the use of estimation, testing, regression, and design on medical data, including the messy parts: [missing data](missing-data.qmd), skewed laboratory measurements, and outcomes that are censored because follow-up ends.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 49, Biostatistics)

A [randomised controlled trial](randomised-controlled-trial.qmd), when it is designed, conducted, and reported properly, is the reference design for comparing health-care interventions. The CONSORT statement exists because a trial that was sound in the protocol can still be unreadable, or misleading, in the paper: readers cannot judge a trial they cannot see. Observational clinical series answer different questions. They can describe who was treated and what happened. They do not inherit the trial's protection against confounding, because the investigators did not assign the treatment.{{cite:schulz_altman_moher2010}} (Introduction){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §§1.2.5 and 1.4)

The methods most often named for the field are resampling, including the bootstrap and the jackknife, multivariate analysis, regression, the evaluation of uncertainty attached to an estimate, and the treatment of missing values. Journals such as *Statistics in Medicine*, *Biometrics*, and *Controlled Clinical Trials* mark the field as a profession with its own literature, next to the clinical journals in which the results are read.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 49, Biostatistics)

## Statistical use

Clinical trials allocate treatments by chance, the idea Fisher developed for agricultural experiments and that the biostatistics sketch treats as the start of trial methodology. [Blinding and placebos](blinding-and-placebo.qmd) keep the comparison from becoming a comparison of expectations. Binary outcomes are summarised by risks and by the [odds ratio](odds-ratio.qmd). Event times use the [survival function](survival-function.qmd), the [hazard rate](hazard-rate.qmd), and the [proportional-hazards model](proportional-hazards-model.qmd). Diagnostic tests are described by [sensitivity and specificity](sensitivity-and-specificity.qmd), which are not the same as the probability of disease given a positive result. Several measurements on the same patient call for multivariate methods. A [bootstrap](bootstrap.qmd) interval is routine when the sampling distribution of a medical estimator has no closed form.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 49, Biostatistics)

A first course meets the same problems in miniature. Comparing a treatment with medical management in patients at risk of a clinical event is already a question about efficacy. The design has to say who was eligible, what was randomised, and which outcome was primary. A significant test of a secondary outcome, chosen after looking, is a different analysis from the trial that was planned. Reporting guidelines ask for the eligibility, the assignment, the outcome, and the numbers lost to follow-up because those facts are what a reader uses to decide whether the estimate applies to the next patient.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.4){{cite:schulz_altman_moher2010}} (Introduction)

Epidemiology, the population side of the same field, estimates incidence, prevalence, and risk from counts of people and counts of events. A rate needs a denominator and a period. A comparison of two groups needs a statement of whether the groups were formed by assignment or by the course of care. Searching for measured confounders helps an observational comparison and does not finish it, because an unmeasured common cause can remain.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §§1.2.5 and 1.3.4){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 49, Biostatistics)

## Assumptions and limits

The reference status of a randomised trial is conditional on the design being carried through. Randomisation that is later undone by differential dropout, by an outcome defined after the data are seen, or by a report that hides the allocation, no longer has the protection the word suggests. CONSORT is a reporting standard, not a proof that every trial that cites it was well conducted. A reader still has to see the flow of participants and the pre-specified outcome.{{cite:schulz_altman_moher2010}} (Introduction)

Observational medical data support associations and hypotheses. A causal sentence requires the conditions of the causal method that is being used, and those conditions are not confirmed by a significant coefficient. Survival methods assume that the censoring mechanism is the one the estimator was built for. A patient who leaves because they became too ill is a different missingness from a patient whose follow-up ended on a calendar date. Multivariate summaries assume a joint model, or at least a joint question. Running a separate test on every laboratory coordinate and ignoring the rest answers a list of one-variable questions, which is a different analysis from a joint one.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 49, Biostatistics){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.4)

Ethical constraints are part of the design, not an afterword. A trial that cannot withhold a treatment, or that must stop early, changes the sample on which the estimate is computed. The statistical report has to carry that change. A laboratory interval, a trial estimate, and a screening test answer three different conditional questions. Mixing them is a failure of the summary, not a small change of vocabulary.{{cite:schulz_altman_moher2010}} (Introduction){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 49, Domains and limitations)

## History

The line usually traced for biostatistics begins in the middle of the seventeenth century, when William Petty and John Graunt analysed the London Bills of Mortality and, with that, death rates, birth rates, and counts of people. Graunt's *Natural and Political Observations* (1662) is the document later summaries name. Gregor Mendel's nineteenth-century experiments on plants added systematic counts of inheritance. Galton and Pearson applied the new correlation methods to physical and behavioural resemblance, and with Weldon they founded *Biometrika*. Fisher's random allocation of treatments at Rothamsted is the design step later read as the opening of the clinical trial.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 49, History)

Late twentieth-century trial reports were uneven enough that consolidated standards were written. The original CONSORT statement appeared in 1996, was revised in 2001, and was updated in 2010 for parallel-group randomised trials, on the view that a well-run trial is the reference method for evaluating health care and that readers cannot judge a trial they cannot see. The same decades made resampling, multivariate analysis, and missing-data methods ordinary tools in the biomedical journals rather than specialist extras.{{cite:schulz_altman_moher2010}} (Introduction){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 49, Biostatistics)
