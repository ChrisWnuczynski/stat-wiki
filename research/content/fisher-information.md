## Motivating example — Why p = 0.7 is not equally precise at every p
Suppose ten independent binary trials yield seven successes. An estimate of their success probability is $0.7$, but its precision depends on the unknown probability: a change from $0.5$ to $0.6$ affects the distribution of the count differently from a change from $0.9$ to $1.0$. The likelihood's local shape gives a way to quantify that distinction. For one Bernoulli trial with success probability $\theta$, the information is $1/[\theta(1-\theta)]$.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 35, Bayesian Statistics) For $n$ independent trials the information about that probability, written $p$, is $n/[p(1-p)]$.{{cite:siegrist2022_best_unbiased}} (§7.5, Bernoulli distribution) Near $p=0$ or $1$ the sample information is large on the probability scale, although the usual normal approximation is then least reliable.

## Intuition — Information is the variability of the score, not bits in one sample
The **score** is the slope of the log-likelihood as a function of a candidate parameter. A large score says that a small change in that parameter would greatly alter the likelihood assigned to the observed data. Across samples generated at the true parameter, positive and negative scores balance. Their variance measures how strongly a typical sample can pull the likelihood away from one candidate value. Equivalently, under regularity conditions, the average negative curvature of the log-likelihood measures the same local sensitivity. Information is therefore a property of the *sampling model at a parameter value*, not a number of bits stored in one observed dataset.{{cite:siegrist2022_best_unbiased}} (§7.5, Information)

## Definition — Expected information, observed information, and the identity
For a scalar parameter $\theta$, write $\ell(\theta;X)=\log f_\theta(X)$ and $U(\theta;X)=\partial\ell(\theta;X)/\partial\theta$. The **expected Fisher information** in $X$ is

$$\mathcal I_X(\theta)=\operatorname{Var}_\theta\{U(\theta;X)\}=\mathbb E_\theta\{U(\theta;X)^2\}.$$

The second equality requires $\mathbb E_\theta U=0$. When differentiation can be passed through the normalising integral a second time, the **information identity** gives $\mathcal I_X(\theta)=-\mathbb E_\theta\{\partial^2\ell(\theta;X)/\partial\theta^2\}$. The observed information $J_X(\theta)=-\partial^2\ell(\theta;X)/\partial\theta^2$ is a function of the actual data; expected information averages it under the model. The two can differ in a sample.{{cite:siegrist2022_best_unbiased}} (§7.5, Information)

For a parameter vector $\boldsymbol\theta$, the information matrix is $\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathbb E[UU^\top]$, or minus the expected Hessian under the corresponding conditions. Its diagonal entries concern individual coordinate directions, while off-diagonal entries record how score components move together. The inverse, when it exists, accounts for uncertainty after the coordinates are estimated jointly; taking reciprocal diagonal entries separately generally gives the wrong marginal variances.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§§1.1, 2.1, pp. 371–373)

## Properties — Additivity, reparameterisation, and the Cramér–Rao bound
For $n$ independent, identically distributed observations, the total score is a sum of independent mean-zero scores. Thus $\mathcal I_n(\theta)=n\mathcal I_1(\theta)$: information grows linearly with sample size, while the corresponding standard-error scale falls like $1/\sqrt n$. If $\phi=h(\theta)$ is a smooth one-to-one reparameterisation, the chain rule gives $\mathcal I_\phi(\phi)=\mathcal I_\theta(\theta)(d\theta/d\phi)^2$. A claim that one model has “more information” therefore needs both a parameter and a scale for that parameter.{{cite:siegrist2022_best_unbiased}} (§7.5, Random Samples)

For an unbiased estimator $T$ of a differentiable target $g(\theta)$, the Cramér–Rao bound is $\operatorname{Var}_\theta(T)\geq [g'(\theta)]^2/\mathcal I_n(\theta)$ under its regularity conditions. Equality is possible when the estimation error is proportional to the score. The bound concerns unbiased estimators under the stated model; it does not say every estimator has variance above $1/\mathcal I_n$. For a regular maximum likelihood estimator in a large sample, the inverse information matrix instead gives its approximate covariance. These are related uses of information with different qualifications.{{cite:siegrist2022_best_unbiased}} (§7.5, Cramér–Rao bounds){{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§2.1, pp. 373–374)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Let the density have parameter-independent support and permit differentiation under the integral. Since $\int f_\theta(x)\,dx=1$, differentiation gives $0=\int \partial_\theta f_\theta(x)\,dx=\mathbb E_\theta U$. Differentiating again and using $\partial_\theta f_\theta=f_\theta U$ gives $0=\mathbb E_\theta(\partial_\theta U+U^2)$. Hence $\mathbb E U^2=-\mathbb E\ell''=\operatorname{Var}(U)$. For independent observations, cross-products of their scores have expectation zero, proving additivity. If $\phi=h(\theta)$ is one-to-one and differentiable, $U_\phi=(d\theta/d\phi)U_\theta$ by the chain rule; taking its variance gives $\mathcal I_\phi=\mathcal I_\theta(d\theta/d\phi)^2$. Finally, differentiating $\mathbb E_\theta T=g(\theta)$ yields $g'(\theta)=\operatorname{Cov}_\theta(T,U)$; Cauchy–Schwarz gives $[g'(\theta)]^2\leq\operatorname{Var}(T)\mathcal I_n(\theta)$.{{cite:siegrist2022_best_unbiased}} (§7.5, Information and Cramér–Rao lower bounds)
:::
:::

## Statistical use — Standard errors in a GLM
In a generalised linear model, the score for the regression coefficients describes how each covariate-weighted residual pushes the fit. For independent responses from an exponential family, Nelder and Wedderburn's expected negative Hessian is a weighted cross-product of the design matrix. Fisher scoring uses this matrix to update the coefficients, giving an iteratively weighted least-squares calculation. The inverse fitted information supplies approximate standard errors when the model is regular and its matrix is nonsingular. A nearly collinear design makes that inverse unstable, even if the response distribution has abundant information about its mean.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§2.1, eqs. 7–9, pp. 373–374)

## Worked example — Twenty Bernoulli trials, fourteen successes
Let $X_1,\ldots,X_{20}$ be independent Bernoulli variables with common probability $p$, and suppose $14$ are successes. The log-likelihood, apart from a constant, is $\ell(p)=14\log p+6\log(1-p)$. For general random count $S=\sum_iX_i$, the score is $U(p)=S/p-(20-S)/(1-p)=(S-20p)/[p(1-p)]$. Because $\operatorname{Var}(S)=20p(1-p)$, $\mathcal I_{20}(p)=20/[p(1-p)]$. At the estimate $\hat p=14/20=0.7$, fitted information is $20/(0.7\cdot0.3)=95.2381$, and its inverse square root is $0.10247$. This is the familiar large-sample standard-error calculation $\sqrt{0.7(0.3)/20}$. It is a local approximation: with only 20 trials, a Wald interval based on it may behave poorly near a boundary.{{cite:siegrist2022_best_unbiased}} (§7.5, Bernoulli distribution)

**Your turn.** In 50 independent Bernoulli trials, 20 are successes. Calculate the fitted Fisher information for $p$ and the inverse-information standard-error estimate. Explain why this is not a posterior standard deviation.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
Here $\hat p=20/50=0.4$, so $\mathcal I_{50}(\hat p)=50/(0.4\cdot0.6)=208.3333$. Its inverse square root is $\sqrt{0.4\cdot0.6/50}=0.06928$. This uses the sampling distribution of an estimator under a fixed $p$; a posterior standard deviation would also require a specified prior and posterior calculation.
:::

## Common misconceptions — High information is not a large observed score
High information does not mean that a particular sample has a large score: the score's *expected* value is zero, and its variance is the information. Expected information and observed information need not agree; the former averages over possible samples, while the latter is the curvature of one fitted likelihood. Nor is $1/\mathcal I$ a universal variance bound for biased estimators: a constant estimator has zero variance but can be badly biased. Finally, information changes under reparameterisation, so comparing its bare numerical values across different scales is misleading.{{cite:siegrist2022_best_unbiased}} (§7.5, Information and lower bounds)

## Assumptions and limits — When the identity fails
If $X\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,\theta)$, the support moves with $\theta$. Inside the support the score is $-1/\theta$, whose expectation is not zero; differentiating the density while ignoring its moving boundary loses a boundary term. The usual score identity and Cramér–Rao argument therefore do not apply. In a Bernoulli model at $p=0$ or $1$, the derivatives and normal approximation that apply at an interior success probability cease to describe a regular estimation problem. In a GLM with perfectly duplicated columns, the information matrix is singular and cannot be inverted to give separate coefficient standard errors.{{cite:siegrist2022_best_unbiased}} (§7.5, fundamental assumption and information bounds){{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§2.1, information matrix)

## Connections — Score, Cramér–Rao, and Fisher scoring
Information is the variance of the [score](score-function.qmd). For an unbiased estimator $T$ of $\theta$, under the regularity conditions, $\operatorname{Var}(T)\ge\mathcal{I}(\theta)^{-1}$; for $n$ iid observations the bound is $1/(n\mathcal{I}_1(\theta))$. [Maximum likelihood](maximum-likelihood.qmd) inverts the information matrix for a large-sample covariance. In a [generalised linear model](generalised-linear-model.qmd) the same expected curvature is the matrix [Fisher scoring](fisher-scoring.qmd) inverts at each step. The three uses share the matrix and differ in the conditions they require.{{cite:siegrist2022_best_unbiased}} (§7.5){{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§2.1)

## History — Fisher, 1922
Fisher's 1922 account put the information retained by statistics at the centre of the problem of estimation and tied efficiency to the precision of competing procedures. His terminology there is broader than the modern score-variance formula.{{cite:fisher1922}} (§§2, 4, 7, pp. 311–316, 330–332) Nelder and Wedderburn's 1972 scoring calculation uses that expected curvature to update a generalised linear model by weighted least squares.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§§1.1, 2.1, pp. 371–374)
