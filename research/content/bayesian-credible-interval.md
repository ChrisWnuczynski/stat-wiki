## Lead context
It reports a range of parameter values that carries a stated share of uncertainty after the data and prior have been combined.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §2.3, pp. 32–34)

## Motivating example
Suppose a treatment succeeds for two of ten independent patients, and a uniform prior was assigned to its success probability $\theta$. The resulting posterior is $\operatorname{Beta}(3,9)$: it favours relatively small probabilities but still leaves a right tail. A clinician needs more than the single posterior mean $0.25$ when judging plausible rates. An interval carrying $95\%$ of the posterior probability states the remaining uncertainty on the rate's original scale. The figure above shows that even with one posterior distribution and one probability level, two reasonable interval rules produce different endpoints.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §§2.1, 2.3, pp. 29–34)

## Intuition
After observing data $x$, the posterior distribution allocates probability across possible parameter values under the chosen model and prior. A credible interval collects a specified portion of that probability on a number line. It does not ask how often a procedure would cover a fixed truth over repeated hypothetical samples; that is a coverage question. To give the range a unique meaning, report how its endpoints were chosen. The equal-tailed rule balances omitted posterior probability on both sides, whereas a highest-density rule keeps the more densely supported values. For a skewed posterior, balancing the tails and minimizing width pull the interval in different directions.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §2.3, pp. 33–34; Ch. 4, §4.4, pp. 90–92)

## Definition
Let $p(\theta\mid x)$ be a proper posterior density. An interval $C(x)=[l(x),u(x)]$ has credibility $1-\alpha$ when $\Pr\{\theta\in C(x)\mid x\}=\int_l^u p(\theta\mid x)\,d\theta=1-\alpha$. The probability conditions on the observed data and on the model and prior used to construct the posterior. For a continuous posterior with cumulative distribution function $F(\,·\mid x)$, the central or equal-tailed interval is $[F^{-1}(\alpha/2\mid x),F^{-1}(1-\alpha/2\mid x)]$. With $\alpha=0.05$, it leaves $0.025$ probability in each tail. Many other pairs of endpoints can enclose the same $0.95$ posterior probability.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §2.3, pp. 33–34)

A highest-posterior-density region at the same level includes all points whose posterior density exceeds a threshold, together with enough points tied at that threshold to attain posterior probability $1-\alpha$. For a continuous unimodal density it is an interval of minimum width, subject to the usual interior and support conditions. For a multimodal density the region can be a union of separate intervals, so “credible region” is the broader term. Its density threshold is a rule for choosing values, not a second definition of posterior probability.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §2.3, bimodal illustration, pp. 33–34)

## Interpretation
The $\operatorname{Beta}(3,9)$ figure compares a central $95\%$ interval and a highest-density $95\%$ interval. The central interval leaves $2.5\%$ on each side, including more of the thin right tail. The highest-density interval shifts left toward the posterior mode and is shorter; its interior endpoint densities match. Both intervals answer “where is $95\%$ of the posterior probability?” but use different rules for which $95\%$ to display. A graph of the posterior, the parameter's units, and the interval rule make the numerical endpoints interpretable together.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §2.3, pp. 33–34)

## Properties
For a continuous posterior, the central interval has probability $1-\alpha$ because the CDF rises from $\alpha/2$ at its lower endpoint to $1-\alpha/2$ at its upper endpoint. A strictly increasing reparameterisation maps this interval to the central interval for the transformed parameter, preserving its tail probabilities. A highest-density interval need not transform that way: a nonlinear change of scale changes density by a Jacobian and can change which values are most dense. Thus it matters whether a density-based interval was chosen for a probability, its log odds, or another transform.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §2.3, pp. 33–34)

For a smooth strictly unimodal posterior, a minimum-width interval whose endpoints lie inside the support has equal density at its endpoints. This explains the figure's highest-density construction. The condition need not hold when the shortest interval touches a support boundary. For a $\operatorname{Beta}(1,2)$ posterior, the density $f(\theta)=2(1-\theta)$ strictly decreases on $[0,1]$, so the shortest $95\%$ interval starts at $0$ and ends at $1-\sqrt{0.05}$. Its CDF is $F(\theta)=1-(1-\theta)^2$, giving exact probability $F(1-\sqrt{0.05})-F(0)=0.95$, but its endpoint densities are $2$ and $2\sqrt{0.05}$, which are unequal. Nor does any of these posterior properties imply exact frequentist coverage for every fixed $\theta$. Large-sample correspondence under regularity conditions is a separate result.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §2.3, pp. 33–34; Ch. 4, §4.4, pp. 90–92)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
For a continuous CDF $F$, set $l=F^{-1}(\alpha/2)$ and $u=F^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)$. Then $\Pr(l\leq\theta\leq u\mid x)=F(u)-F(l)=(1-\alpha/2)-\alpha/2=1-\alpha$. If $z=h(\theta)$ for strictly increasing $h$, the event $\{l\leq\theta\leq u\}$ is the same event as $\{h(l)\leq z\leq h(u)\}$; each tail still has probability $\alpha/2$. This proves the central-interval transformation statement. For a smooth nonlinear $h$, however, $p_z(z\mid x)=p_\theta(h^{-1}(z)\mid x)|d h^{-1}(z)/dz|$, so equal density heights on the $\theta$ scale need not remain equal on the $z$ scale.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §2.3, posterior quantiles and density regions)

For an interval of fixed posterior probability $q$ with interior endpoints, $F(u)-F(l)=q$. Varying $l$ and differentiating gives $f(u)u'(l)-f(l)=0$, or $u'(l)=f(l)/f(u)$. Its width $u(l)-l$ has derivative $f(l)/f(u)-1$. An interior minimum must therefore satisfy $f(l)=f(u)$; with a unimodal density, moving an interval toward the higher-density side until this balance holds reduces its width. This is the equal-height condition used for the Beta figure. For the worked Beta posterior, conjugacy follows by multiplying the uniform prior by the binomial kernel $\theta^2(1-\theta)^8$, giving the $\operatorname{Beta}(3,9)$ kernel. With zero successes and prior kernel $1/\theta$, the posterior kernel behaves like $1/\theta$ as $\theta\downarrow0$; its integral diverges, proving the stated improper-posterior counterexample.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §§2.1, 2.3, pp. 29–34)

## Statistical use — Reporting posterior intervals
Derive the posterior for the particular quantity whose uncertainty matters, choose a probability level and interval rule, and then calculate its posterior quantiles or density threshold. When a closed-form CDF is available, numerical quantiles avoid simulation error. When only posterior draws are available, their empirical quantiles approximate a central interval; assess whether the sampler explores the tails well enough for the requested precision. The posterior-draw property gives one direct order-statistic convention for $1{,}000$ draws. Different software quantile conventions can shift the selected order statistics slightly.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §2.3, pp. 33–34; Ch. 1, §1.9, pp. 22–24)

An interval for a parameter is distinct from a posterior predictive interval for a future observation. The latter averages over parameter uncertainty and includes outcome variation conditional on the parameter; it can be wider or have a different shape. State the parameter, its scale, the prior, and the sampling model when reporting a credible interval so that its conditional probability claim can be understood and checked.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 1, §§1.3–1.4, 1.9, pp. 7–10, 22–24)

## Worked example
Use a uniform $\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)$ prior for a success probability $\theta$ and observe two successes in ten conditionally independent trials. The posterior is $\operatorname{Beta}(3,9)$, with mean $3/12=0.25$ and mode $2/10=0.20$. Numerically inverting its CDF gives the $95\%$ equal-tailed interval $[0.0602,0.5178]$: $F(0.0602)\approx0.025$ and $1-F(0.5178)\approx0.025$. A shortest $95\%$ interval, found by matching endpoint densities while retaining $0.95$ probability, is approximately $[0.0406,0.4837]$. Its width is $0.4432$, compared with $0.4575$ for the equal-tailed interval. The two ranges differ because the posterior is skewed; the shorter one puts more omitted probability on the sparse right side.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §§2.1, 2.3, pp. 29–34)

**Your turn.** Keep the uniform prior but observe three successes in ten trials. Identify the posterior, report its central $95\%$ interval to four decimal places, and find the posterior probability that $\theta>0.5$. State what the interval probability conditions on.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §§2.1, 2.3, pp. 29–34)

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The likelihood contributes $\theta^3(1-\theta)^7$, so the posterior is $\operatorname{Beta}(4,8)$. Its $0.025$ and $0.975$ quantiles give $[0.1093,0.6097]$. The posterior tail probability is $1-F(0.5)=0.1133$. The $95\%$ refers to the posterior probability for $\theta$ conditional on these ten results, the binomial model, and the uniform prior; repeated-sampling coverage concerns a procedure evaluated over possible data sets under a specified sampling law.
:::

## Common misconceptions
A $95\%$ credible interval does not automatically have $95\%$ frequentist coverage for every fixed parameter value; the probability statements condition on different things. A credible interval is not uniquely determined by its probability level: central and highest-density rules give different endpoints for the same skewed posterior. Highest-density does not mean “most likely value plus or minus a fixed distance”; its region can be disjoint when the posterior has separated modes. A parameter interval is also not a prediction interval for the next patient or trial. These are four different readings of what an interval reports.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §2.3, pp. 33–34; Ch. 4, §4.4, pp. 90–92)

## Assumptions and limits — Dependence on the model and prior
Posterior probability requires a proper posterior. For example, under a binomial likelihood with zero successes, the improper prior kernel $1/\theta$ on $(0,1)$ leaves a posterior kernel $(1-\theta)^n/\theta$, whose integral diverges near zero; no $95\%$ posterior interval exists until the prior or model is changed. Even a proper posterior can be misleading if the binomial independence model is false for clustered patients, or if the prior concentrates on implausible rates. A two-mode posterior is a concrete case in which one contiguous interval can include a low-density gap; report the density or separate regions rather than hide that shape. Finally, extreme posterior quantiles estimated from too few effective draws can be unstable even when the posterior is well defined.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §2.3, bimodal illustration, pp. 33–34; Ch. 1, §1.9, pp. 22–24)

## History
Gelman and colleagues trace the inverse-probability treatment of an unknown binomial proportion to Bayes's 1763 essay and Laplace's later work. Their modern discussion recommends posterior quantiles for interval summaries and distinguishes those central intervals from highest-density regions; their bibliographic note points to Box and Tiao's 1973 treatment of such regions. This history explains why a present-day report should name its interval rule rather than treat “Bayesian interval” as one algorithm.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §§2.3, 2.10, pp. 33–34, 56)
