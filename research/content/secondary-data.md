## Lead context

Not every analysis starts by collecting the observations it analyses. Secondary data are records that were made for another purpose and are later used again: a register of births, a file of medical records, a sales ledger, an official series. Primary collection designs the observation for the question. Secondary use inherits the design somebody else chose.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 152){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.4)

## Motivating example

Researchers who want the relation between an earlier illness and a later outcome can review medical records instead of waiting for new cases to occur. The records were written for care, not for the study. The study is retrospective: the events have already taken place, and the data are being read afterwards. A register of births, deaths, and marriages is the same kind of material on a civic scale. Daily temperatures, monthly rainfall, and car sales sit in the same class: series that already exist and are accessible.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.4){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 152)

## Definition

Secondary data are observations originally recorded for a purpose other than the study now using them. A register is the standard form. Collection has three approaches: a register, sampling together with a census, and an experiment. The register is the one that was kept before the new question was asked. Reviewing past events in medical or company records is the retrospective version of the same practice. A prospective study, by contrast, identifies people and collects information as events unfold. The Nurses' Health Study, started in 1976 and expanded in 1989, recruits nurses and then collects questionnaires. A review of old charts does not. A data set can contain both prospectively and retrospectively collected variables.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 152){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.4)

Official statistics are the governmental case. They are statistics from data collected by government establishments, or by private agencies working for government, and the census is the best-known example. Principle 5 of the Fundamental Principles of Official Statistics allows data for statistical purposes to be drawn from statistical surveys or from administrative records, chosen with regard to quality, timeliness, cost, and the burden on respondents. An administrative record reused for a statistical table is secondary data in that sense.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 393){{cite:un_res68261}} (Principle 5)

## Interpretation

There is no general method that turns a register into a sound conclusion. Each series has to be judged on its merits, and inference beyond the recorded units is the delicate step. The coverage of a register is the coverage of whatever list was kept. Units the original system did not record are not in the file, and no reanalysis puts them back. That is coverage error relative to a population wider than the register. The arithmetic can be excellent and still describe the register rather than the population the new question names.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 152){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "coverage error")

Secondary data support description of what was recorded, and they support a wider claim only with an argument that the recorded units and the recorded variables match the question. They are observational. No treatment has been explicitly applied. A causal reading has the usual difficulty of an observational comparison, with the extra difficulty that confounders the original record did not bother to store cannot be adjusted for. A billing file may not contain the clinical detail a later study needs, because the variables are the ones the original purpose required. A code that meant one thing when it was written cannot be redefined by the later analyst without a record of the change. Official series share the limits of the administrative systems that produced them, and political power has historically set which contents those systems hold.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 152, 393–394){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.4){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "census")

## Worked example

A hospital file holds 240 admissions and was kept for billing. A study of falls finds a fall recorded for 30 of them. The recorded risk is $30/240 = 0.125$. The hospital's catchment had 300 admissions in the same period, and 60 were treated elsewhere and are absent from the file. The file covers $240/300 = 0.8$ of the catchment's admissions. The 0.125 describes the billed admissions. It describes the catchment only with a further argument about the 60 admissions the file never held.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 152){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "coverage error")

**Your turn.** Twelve of the 60 missing admissions are later found, and 3 of those 12 had a fall. What are the new number of admissions in the file and the new recorded fall risk?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The file has $240 + 12 = 252$ admissions and $30 + 3 = 33$ falls. The recorded risk is $33/252 = 11/84$.
:::

## History

Registers and censuses are the old form of data collection, from the enumerations of antiquity through the Roman counts and the administrative statistics of the modern state. A position for an official statistician is recorded for the Chow dynasty in China, and Tacitus reports that Augustus ordered a count of soldiers, ships, and wealth. The word statistics itself is traced to the Latin *status*, the state. Official statistics, in the modern sense of governmental series, grew with those administrative counts, and the census is the series a reader is most likely to have met. Retrospective review of existing records, as against identifying people and then watching events unfold, is the distinction a first course still uses to separate the two timings of an observational study.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 152, 393){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.4)
