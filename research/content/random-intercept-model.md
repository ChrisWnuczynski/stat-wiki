## Motivating example
Patients treated at the same hospital may have more similar outcomes than patients at different hospitals because they share staff, procedures and catchment area. A random intercept gives each hospital a shift from the population mean and models those shifts with a common variance. It therefore expresses both the hospital-to-hospital heterogeneity and the dependence among observations from one hospital. That dependence affects uncertainty about a treatment effect even when treatment itself has a fixed coefficient.{{cite:laird_ware1982}} (§§1–2, pp. 963–965, two-stage random-effects model){{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, pp. 2–3, conditional mixed model)

## Intuition
Imagine parallel hospital-specific lines above and below a population line. An individual residual moves one observation away from its hospital line; the random intercept moves the whole hospital line. Two patients at the same hospital share the latter move. Averaging patients reduces residual noise, but does not average away the hospital shift. This is why treating all patients as independent can make a treatment estimate look more precise than it is. The figure shows the parallel lines and their shared intercepts.{{cite:laird_ware1982}} (§2, pp. 964–965, individual effects and covariance){{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, conditional response and random effects)

## Definition — Conditional and marginal views
Using the page’s notation, let $Y_{ji}=\beta_0+\mathbf X_{ji}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\beta+\alpha_j+\varepsilon_{ji}$, where group effects $\alpha_j$ are independent $N(0,\sigma_A^2)$ and residuals $\varepsilon_{ji}$ are independent $N(0,\sigma^2)$, independently of the group effects. Conditional on $\alpha_j$, responses in group $j$ have their own intercept $\beta_0+\alpha_j$. After averaging over the random intercept, every response has mean $\beta_0+\mathbf X_{ji}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\beta$ and variance $\sigma_A^2+\sigma^2$. Distinct responses in one group have covariance $\sigma_A^2$; responses in independent groups have covariance zero. These are the conditional and marginal views of the same model.{{cite:laird_ware1982}} (§2, pp. 964–965, marginal covariance $R_i+Z_iDZ_i^{\mathsf T}$){{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, conditional normal model)

## Interpretation — Correlation and effective information
The intraclass correlation is $\rho=\sigma_A^2/(\sigma_A^2+\sigma^2)$. It is the correlation between two different patients in the same hospital under this model, not the correlation of a patient’s repeated measurements with itself. With $m$ observations from one group, the group mean has variance $\sigma_A^2+\sigma^2/m$, whereas an independence calculation would use $(\sigma_A^2+\sigma^2)/m$. Their ratio is $1+(m-1)\rho$. This *design effect* shows why adding more people to a fixed number of groups eventually yields diminishing gains for a population mean: the shared group variation remains. The formula is for equal-sized groups and a simple mean, not a universal variance multiplier for every regression coefficient.{{cite:laird_ware1982}} (§2, pp. 964–965, within-unit covariance){{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, marginal covariance)

The covariance matrix for $m$ responses from one group is $\sigma^2 I_m+\sigma_A^2\mathbf1\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}$. It has a common covariance off the diagonal, often called compound symmetry. Positive variances make its off-diagonal correlation nonnegative. A general equicorrelation matrix can have a negative common correlation as long as it remains positive semidefinite; a shared random intercept alone cannot generate that pattern. The requirement is a constraint of this model, not a reason to ignore negative dependence when it is observed.{{cite:laird_ware1982}} (§2, pp. 964–965, covariance construction){{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, random-effect covariance)

## Properties — Partial pooling and new groups
Under normality with known fixed effects and variance components, let $\overline r_j$ be the average of $Y_{ji}-\beta_0-\mathbf X_{ji}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\beta$ in a group of size $m$. Its conditional distribution given $\alpha_j$ is $N(\alpha_j,\sigma^2/m)$. The conditional mean of the group effect is $\widehat\alpha_j=w\overline r_j$, with $w=\sigma_A^2/(\sigma_A^2+\sigma^2/m)$. Small or noisy groups have smaller $w$ and are pulled more strongly toward the population line. For a new, unobserved group the mean effect is zero; its future observations still have uncertainty from both $\sigma_A^2$ and $\sigma^2$.{{cite:laird_ware1982}} (§3.1, p. 966, empirical Bayes random-effect prediction){{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, conditional random effects)

The model treats groups as exchangeable after their recorded covariates are accounted for. Estimating $\sigma_A^2$ requires information across groups, so one hospital with many patients cannot reveal a population distribution of hospital intercepts. A zero estimated group variance collapses the model to the ordinary residual-only covariance structure at the boundary of the parameter space. Random slopes allow group lines to tilt as well as shift, and serial residual structures can represent correlations that decay with time. Those are distinct extensions, not properties conferred by an intercept alone.{{cite:laird_ware1982}} (§2, pp. 964–965, general random-effects and residual covariance){{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, general random-effects design; Introduction, residual structures)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Write the centered response as $R_{ji}=\alpha_j+\varepsilon_{ji}$. Independence gives $\operatorname{Var}(R_{ji})=\sigma_A^2+\sigma^2$ and, for $i\ne i'$, $\operatorname{Cov}(R_{ji},R_{ji'})=\operatorname{Var}(\alpha_j)=\sigma_A^2$; averaging $m$ residuals gives $\operatorname{Var}(\overline R_j)=\sigma_A^2+\sigma^2/m$. Dividing the latter by the variance $(\sigma_A^2+\sigma^2)/m$ from a false independence calculation yields $1+(m-1)\rho$. The covariance matrix has eigenvalue $\sigma^2+m\sigma_A^2$ along $\mathbf1$ and $\sigma^2$ in each of the $m-1$ orthogonal directions. More generally, a unit-diagonal equicorrelation matrix has eigenvalues $1+(m-1)\rho$ and $1-\rho$, so it is positive semidefinite for $-1/(m-1)\le\rho\le1$ and positive definite with strict inequalities. For pooling, the normal prior density of $\alpha_j$ is proportional to $\exp\{-\alpha_j^2/(2\sigma_A^2)\}$ and the group-mean likelihood to $\exp\{-(\overline r_j-\alpha_j)^2/(2\sigma^2/m)\}$. Completing the square gives posterior variance $v=(1/\sigma_A^2+m/\sigma^2)^{-1}$ and mean $v(m/\sigma^2)\overline r_j=w\overline r_j$.{{cite:laird_ware1982}} (§§2–3.1, pp. 964–966, covariance and empirical Bayes prediction){{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, Gaussian conditional and random-effects model)
:::
:::

## Statistical use — Estimation and prediction
In R, a Gaussian `lmer` model with `(1 | hospital)` fits a separate latent intercept distribution for hospitals along with its fixed-effect formula. Maximum likelihood and restricted maximum likelihood estimate variance components under the specified model; the displayed group effects are conditional predictions, not independent estimates of unrelated hospital coefficients. For a fitted treatment contrast, report the fixed effect with uncertainty based on the grouped design. For a new hospital, use the population fixed part and include between-hospital variance in predictive uncertainty. For an observed hospital, add its predicted random effect and include uncertainty about that prediction.{{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, pp. 2–3; §1.1, model formula; §3, ML and REML criteria){{cite:laird_ware1982}} (§3.1, p. 966, fixed-effect and individual-effect prediction)

## Worked example
Suppose untreated patients have population mean $100$, between-hospital variance $\sigma_A^2=9$, and residual variance $\sigma^2=16$. Two untreated patients at one hospital have responses $110$ and $114$, so their mean is $112$ and their average departure from the population line is $12$. Its residual sampling variance is $16/2=8$, making the pooling weight $w=9/(9+8)=9/17\approx0.5294$. The predicted hospital intercept is $12(9/17)=108/17\approx6.3529$, and the conditional mean for another untreated patient there is $100+108/17\approx106.3529$. The marginal mean for an untreated patient at a new hospital remains $100$.{{cite:laird_ware1982}} (§3.1, p. 966, conditional random-effect prediction)

The within-hospital correlation is $9/(9+16)=0.36$. A new individual at an unobserved hospital has marginal variance $9+16=25$. Given the two measurements and treating the population parameters as known, the hospital effect has conditional variance $72/17\approx4.2353$, so another patient there has predictive variance $16+72/17\approx20.2353$. The conditional prediction is less uncertain because that hospital has already supplied information; this calculation omits uncertainty in estimated population parameters.{{cite:laird_ware1982}} (§§2–3.1, pp. 964–966, marginal and conditional variances)

**Your turn.** Suppose the population mean is $50$, group-effect variance is $4$, residual variance is $9$, and a group of three has observed mean $53$. Find the pooling weight, predicted group mean and correlation of two distinct observations in that group.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The group mean has residual variance $9/3=3$, so $w=4/(4+3)=4/7\approx0.5714$. The group’s observed departure is $3$ and its predicted effect is $12/7\approx1.7143$. The conditional predicted group mean is $50+12/7\approx51.7143$. Two distinct observations have correlation $4/(4+9)=4/13\approx0.3077$. These numbers describe a Gaussian random-intercept model with the stated variance components treated as known.
:::

## Common misconceptions
Patients in a random-intercept model are conditionally independent given their hospital effect but correlated after that effect is averaged out. A random intercept is a hospital-level model component, not an observed extra response or a claim that a hospital was selected by a physical random-number device. A predicted hospital effect is partially pooled, whereas a fixed hospital indicator estimates a named-group contrast without the same population distribution. Positive intraclass correlation is a consequence of the shared positive variance in this model; it is not a universal rule for grouped data. Finally, the model shifts intercepts only: it does not allow each hospital its own treatment or time slope.{{cite:laird_ware1982}} (§2, pp. 964–965, conditional and marginal model){{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, random-effects design)

## Assumptions and limits
If a hospital’s unrecorded baseline risk influences which treatment its patients receive, the assumption that random intercepts are independent of treatment covariates is suspect; the treatment coefficient may mix treatment and hospital differences. If time measurements are more correlated when close together, a constant same-hospital correlation misses that temporal pattern. If groups compete for a fixed resource and observations become negatively associated, the nonnegative random-intercept covariance is wrong. With only a few hospitals, the between-hospital variance is uncertain even when the patient count is large. A random slope, a residual correlation model, more group-level covariates or a named-hospital analysis can address different parts of these failures, according to the scientific target.{{cite:laird_ware1982}} (§§1–2, pp. 963–965, unbalanced longitudinal designs and covariance structures){{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, pp. 2–3; §1.1, model specification)

## History
Laird and Ware’s 1982 longitudinal-data paper developed a two-stage random-effects formulation for unequally spaced and unbalanced repeated measurements. It separated population parameters, individual random effects and residual variation, then derived marginal covariance and empirical Bayes predictions. The random intercept is the simplest case of that construction. Later software such as `lme4` represents the same conditional normal structure through fixed- and random-effect design matrices and fits its variance parameters by ML or REML.{{cite:laird_ware1982}} (Summary and §§1–3, pp. 963–966){{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, pp. 2–3; §§1.1, 3)
