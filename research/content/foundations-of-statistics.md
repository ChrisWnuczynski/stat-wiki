## Lead context

A government counts births, a laboratory records yields, and a poll asks a few hundred adults about a policy that concerns millions. Statistics is the discipline that turns incomplete numerical records of that kind into statements that can be checked. The foundations are the distinctions that make the programme a method: what was observed, what the observation is supposed to represent, how the records were obtained, and which claims stay inside the file. {{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 518–519) {{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.2)

## Definition

In the modern sense, statistics is a discipline concerned with numerical data and with a scientific system for collecting them, organising them, analysing them, and interpreting them. The word also names a set of available figures about a phenomenon, as in unemployment statistics. The discipline is the larger object: techniques for obtaining knowledge from incomplete data, used when the material can be put in numerical form. {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 518)

Two subsets of technique carry the load. Descriptive statistics represent information in a form that can be used. Inferential statistics generalise that information, which means making statements about populations from samples. A population, in the sampling sense, is the collection one would like to learn about. In one teaching example the population is every graduate of a university in the last five years, and the sample is the graduates actually drawn for review. The sample is studied because the whole collection is not in hand. {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 518) {{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.3)

The empirical starting point is a data matrix. Each row is a case and each column is a variable. A loan file with fifty rows has, in each row, an amount, an interest rate, a term, a grade, a state, an income, and a home-ownership status. Asking what the first row’s grade is, and what units the income is in, is part of reading the matrix, not a later statistical flourish. Without those meanings the arithmetic has nothing to attach to. {{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.2.1)

A model is the theoretical representation that connects the observations. It contains variables and parameters. A stochastic model adds an unobservable error, so the relation is not treated as exact. Bayesian and non-Bayesian procedures share the first two steps of a parametric analysis: name the known observations, and name a family of distributions for them. They part company on whether uncertainty about the parameter is given a distribution before the observations are used. {{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 32–33)

## Interpretation

The foundational split is between a number computed from the cases in hand and a number defined on the collection those cases are meant to represent. A poll that reports a 45 percent approval rating has produced a point estimate of a population proportion. The population proportion stays unknown unless every individual is observed. The gap between the estimate and that proportion is the error of the estimate. Part of the gap is sampling error, the variation from one sample to the next. Part of it is bias, a systematic tilt, as when a question about a stadium is worded so that disagreement sounds like disloyalty to the school. A larger sample shrinks the first part when the draws are comparable. It does not erase the second. {{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, §5.1.1)

That reading is what makes a sampling distribution useful even though it is never seen in a real study. If the population proportion who support an energy policy is 0.88 and a simple random sample of 1,000 adults is drawn, repeated computer repetitions of that draw produced sample proportions such as 0.894, 0.885, 0.878, and 0.859. Across 10,000 repetitions the centre of the histogram sat at 0.880 and the standard deviation was 0.010. The histogram is a picture of the procedure, not of one poll. Inference treats the single observed proportion as one draw from such a picture. {{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, §5.1.2)

Causal questions add a further primitive. An observational study collects records without assigning the condition under study: surveys, medical files, a cohort followed through time. Such a study can show an association. It does not, by itself, show that one variable changed because another did. An experiment is the collection plan in which researchers assign treatments. When the assignment uses a random mechanism, such as a coin toss, the experiment is randomised. OpenIntro’s stent question — whether a device placed in a blood vessel reduces the risk of stroke — is the sort of claim that this design is built to address, because the treatment is assigned rather than chosen by the patient. {{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.2.5; Ch. 1, §1.4) Randomised experiments are central to the theory of causal inference because random assignment supports exchangeability. Many scientific questions are still answered from observational records, and the conditions under which those records identify a causal contrast have to be stated separately. {{cite:hernan_robins2020}} (Ch. 3, §3.1, pp. 27–28)

## Statistical use

Description stays inside the observed file: frequencies, means, a fitted line. Inference steps from the sample to a population quantity, and the ordinary tools are a point estimate, an interval of plausible values, and a test of a claim. The equations change with the parameter. The job does not. Statistical inference, in one statement of the same idea, is primarily concerned with understanding and quantifying the uncertainty of parameter estimates. {{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, introduction preceding §5.1) {{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 263–264)

The same division organises applied work. Fundamental research in statistics is research on probabilities, on statistical methods, and on theory. The applied fields use those methods on their own numerical records: biology and biometry, commerce and inventory, demography, the measurement of production and employment, education, engineering tests and quality control, health and insurance, marketing, management, psychology and the construction of scales, the social sciences, and the reduction of measurements from space missions. A statistician can work in any of those domains. The foundation that travels is the same one: a defined collection, a recorded subset, a model, and a statement of what the calculation is allowed to say beyond the file. {{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 518–519)

Four design principles sit under a randomised experiment that is meant to support that step. Researchers control differences they can control. They randomise to account for differences they cannot. They replicate, by taking enough cases and, separately, by repeating a study. They sometimes block on a variable that is already known to move the response, and randomise inside the blocks. Human experiments still need extra care. Volunteers who know they are receiving a new drug are not in the same position as volunteers who know they are not, and that knowledge can move the outcome the drug was meant to explain. {{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.4.1; Ch. 1, §1.4.2)

## History

The word statistics comes from Latin *status*, the state. Kendall reports its use by the historian Girolamo Ghilini in 1589, and dates the origin of modern statistics to about 1660. Governments needed counts of people, births, migration, and employment, and the word first named those compilations. Methods and distributional models developed principally in the nineteenth century, because experimental and human sciences needed them. In the twentieth century the methods had become numerous enough that statistics became a separate discipline, with specialised encyclopedias: the *International Encyclopedia of Statistics* of 1978 and the *Encyclopedia of Statistical Sciences* begun in 1982. {{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 518–519)

The sampling half of the foundation is more recent than the census. Complete enumeration stayed the usual official practice into the nineteenth century. In 1895, at the congress of the International Statistical Institute in Bern, Anders Nicolai Kiaer compared the structure of a sample with the structure of a census population and used the word “representative.” Most of the congress opposed him, holding that only a complete count would do. By the Rome congress of 1925 the argument had moved. Random sampling and purposive sampling were both on the table, and the choice was among sampling methods rather than between a sample and a complete count. The 1936 *Literary Digest* poll, which predicted a Landon victory from a telephone sample in a year when telephones marked wealth, is the usual picture of a sample that was large and still biased. {{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 468–469)

The inferential half has its own line. Bayes’s essay, published by Price in 1763, de Moivre’s doctrine of chances, Gauss’s theory of errors, and Laplace’s analytic theory of probabilities supplied ways to pass between observations and an unknown quantity. Galton’s treatment of correlation, Pearson’s 1900 criterion for whether deviations could reasonably have arisen by random sampling, and Gosset’s 1908 paper on the probable error of a mean made the sample-to-population step a standard scientific calculation. Neyman and Pearson’s papers of 1928 and 1933 organised tests as a choice between a null hypothesis and an alternative, and distinguished errors of the two kinds. {{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 263–264, 250–251)

## Connections

A [population](population.qmd) and a [sample](sample.qmd) are the sets the inference connects. [Sampling](sampling.qmd) is how the subset is chosen, and only a probability mechanism makes the sampling error calculable. A [statistical model](statistical-model.qmd) is the representation that introduces parameters and error. [Inference](inference.qmd) is the step from the sample to a quantity that was not measured on the whole collection. [Frequentist inference](frequentist-inference.qmd) scores the procedures used in that step by how often they succeed in repeated samples. [Causality](causality.qmd) is the further question of what would have happened under a different action, which a randomised experiment is built to address and which an observational file addresses only under extra conditions. [Data collection](data-collection.qmd) is the decision that determines whether the numbers are a register, a sample, or an experiment. The [philosophy of statistics](philosophy-of-statistics.qmd) asks what those steps assume about probability, models, and causes.
