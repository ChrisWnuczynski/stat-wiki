## Motivating example
For a Poisson count, the mean $\lambda$ must be positive, but the coefficient multiplying the observed count in its log probability is $\log\lambda$. Modeling that coefficient as a linear predictor automatically keeps the fitted mean positive after the exponential transformation. A Bernoulli probability has the same issue at both zero and one; its corresponding coefficient is the log odds. The natural parameter reveals these useful scales by rewriting the probability law, rather than by transforming the observed data.{{cite:fithian2023_exp_families}} (§Exponential family structure, Poisson example){{cite:geyer2022_exp_family}} (§§3.1–3.3, 7.1–7.2)

## Intuition
An exponential-family density can be written $f(x\mid\eta)=h(x)\exp\{\eta^{\mathsf T}T(x)-A(\eta)\}$. The statistic $T(x)$ is the part of the observation that interacts with the parameter, and $\eta$ is its coefficient, called the natural or canonical parameter in this unscaled form. The log normalizer $A$ adjusts all probabilities so they sum or integrate to one. Moving $\eta$ therefore tilts the relative weights of possible observations; it need not move the ordinary mean by the same numerical amount.{{cite:fithian2023_exp_families}} (§Exponential family structure, equations for the density and log partition){{cite:geyer2022_exp_family}} (§§3.1–3.2)

## Definition
For a specified exponential-family representation with statistic $T(x)$ and fixed base factor $h(x)$, the natural parameter is the coefficient $\eta$ in the exponent. Its allowable values are those for which $A(\eta)=\log\int e^{\eta^{\mathsf T}T(x)}h(x)\,dx$ is finite, with a sum replacing the integral for discrete outcomes. This *natural parameter space* can include constraints even when the originally named parameter has a different range. The representation must be stated: an invertible change of scale in $T$ changes the numerical value of $\eta$ while leaving the distribution unchanged.{{cite:fithian2023_exp_families}} (§Exponential family structure, natural parameter space and Poisson example){{cite:geyer2022_exp_family}} (§§3.1–3.4)

One GLM convention places a separate dispersion factor $e(\phi)$ under $y\theta+c(\theta)$. With that scale held fixed, $\theta$ is called canonical in the dispersion-scaled notation, while the coefficient of $y$ in the fully unscaled exponent is $\eta=\theta/e(\phi)$. For a normal family with known variance $\sigma^2$, this distinction permits $\theta=\mu$ in the scaled form but $\eta=\mu/\sigma^2$ when the variance is absorbed into the base measure. For Poisson and Bernoulli with unit dispersion, the familiar log mean and log odds coincide across these conventions.{{cite:geyer2022_exp_family}} (§§3.1–3.4, 7.1–7.2 and 7.5){{cite:zhou2020_glm}} (§Exponential family distribution, Examples 1–3)

## Interpretation — Reading parameters on the right scale
For a Bernoulli response with success probability $p$, the log mass is $y\log\{p/(1-p)\}+\log(1-p)$. Thus $\eta=\log\{p/(1-p)\}$ ranges over the real line as $p$ ranges strictly between zero and one. Its inverse is $p=e^\eta/(1+e^\eta)$. A unit increase in $\eta$ multiplies the odds by $e$; it does not add a fixed amount to the probability. For a Poisson response, $\eta=\log\lambda$ and $\lambda=e^\eta$, so a unit increase multiplies the mean by $e$.{{cite:fithian2023_exp_families}} (§Exponential family structure, Poisson example){{cite:geyer2022_exp_family}} (§§7.1–7.2)

The derivative of the log normalizer gives $E_\eta T(X)=\nabla A(\eta)$, and its Hessian gives $\operatorname{Cov}_\eta\{T(X)\}$ when differentiation under the normalizing integral is valid. This relation makes the natural scale useful for likelihood scores and for understanding why the normalizer is convex. A canonical GLM link sets the linear predictor equal to a natural parameter in the chosen family convention, but a different link can still define a valid GLM.{{cite:fithian2023_exp_families}} (§Differential identities, mean and variance){{cite:geyer2022_exp_family}} (§§4–5)

## Properties — Equivalent representations
Natural-parameter values are not intrinsically unique. For the same Poisson family, taking $T(x)=x$ gives $\eta=\log\lambda$, whereas taking $T^*(x)=x/2$ gives $\eta^*=2\log\lambda$. Both products equal $x\log\lambda$ and produce identical probabilities. More generally, invertible affine changes of the statistic have corresponding changes of the natural parameter and log normalizer. The chosen representation should therefore accompany claims about a “canonical” scale.{{cite:fithian2023_exp_families}} (§Exponential family structure, Poisson example and affine reparameterization){{cite:geyer2022_exp_family}} (§3.4, Uniqueness)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Normalization requires $e^{A(\eta)}=\int e^{\eta^{\mathsf T}T(x)}h(x)\,dx$. Differentiate in a coordinate $\eta_j$ at an interior parameter value where interchange is justified: $e^{A}\partial_jA=\int T_j(x)e^{\eta^{\mathsf T}T(x)}h(x)\,dx$. Divide by $e^A$ to get $\partial_jA=E_\eta T_j(X)$. Differentiate again and subtract the product of first derivatives to obtain $\partial_{jk}A=\operatorname{Cov}_\eta\{T_j(X),T_k(X)\}$. A covariance matrix is positive semidefinite, so $A$ is convex on the interior of its natural parameter space.

For Poisson, $f(x\mid\lambda)=\exp\{x\log\lambda-\lambda-\log(x!)\}$. Thus $T(x)=x$, $\eta=\log\lambda$, $A(\eta)=e^\eta$, and $A'=A''=e^\eta=\lambda$. Replacing $T$ by $T^*=x/2$ and $\eta$ by $\eta^*=2\eta$ leaves their product unchanged, with $A^*(\eta^*)=e^{\eta^*/2}$. For Bernoulli, $f(y\mid p)=\exp\{y\log[p/(1-p)]-\log(1+e^\eta)\}$, where $\eta=\log[p/(1-p)]$; differentiating $A(\eta)=\log(1+e^\eta)$ gives mean $p$ and variance $p(1-p)$.{{cite:fithian2023_exp_families}} (§Exponential family structure and §Differential identities){{cite:geyer2022_exp_family}} (§§3.4, 7.1–7.2)
:::
:::

## Statistical use — Linking a mean to a predictor
In Poisson regression with log link, $\log\mu_i=\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta$ makes the natural parameter a linear function of covariates. The count log-likelihood then contains $y_i\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta$ minus its normalizer. In logistic regression, the same role is played by log odds. This arrangement simplifies differentiation and interprets a coefficient as an additive change on the natural scale, often a multiplicative change after inversion. It does not establish that the chosen mean model or independence assumptions fit the data.{{cite:geyer2022_exp_family}} (§§7.1–7.2, 9.3){{cite:zhou2020_glm}} (§Link function)

Distinguish the natural parameter from a sufficient statistic: one is a model parameter, the other is calculated from observations. Their product is what appears in the exponent. Completeness of the statistic requires additional full-rank and open-parameter conditions; merely writing an exponential-family expression does not establish it. Those conditions matter when using Rao–Blackwell or uniqueness arguments.{{cite:geyer2022_exp_family}} (§§3.1, 3.6, 8–9)

## Worked example
Suppose a Bernoulli success probability is $p=0.20$. Its odds are $0.20/0.80=1/4$, so the natural parameter in the usual representation is $\eta=\log(1/4)\approx-1.386294$. The log normalizer is $A(\eta)=\log(1+e^\eta)=\log(1.25)\approx0.223144$. The exponential-family formula gives $P(Y=1)=\exp\{\eta-A(\eta)\}=0.20$ and $P(Y=0)=\exp\{-A(\eta)\}=0.80$. Its derivatives give $A'(\eta)=0.20$ and $A''(\eta)=0.20(0.80)=0.16$. The mean and variance agree with direct Bernoulli calculation, but neither is numerically equal to $\eta$.{{cite:geyer2022_exp_family}} (§7.1, Binomial Distribution){{cite:fithian2023_exp_families}} (§Differential identities)

**Your turn.** A Poisson response has mean $\lambda=4$. In the representation with $T(y)=y$, find its natural parameter, log normalizer, mean and variance by differentiation. What happens to the natural parameter if instead $T^*(y)=y/2$?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The natural parameter is $\eta=\log4\approx1.386294$, and $A(\eta)=e^\eta=4$. Hence $A'(\eta)=A''(\eta)=4$, the mean and variance. With $T^*(y)=y/2$, the corresponding natural parameter is $\eta^*=2\log4\approx2.772589$; the distribution is unchanged because $\eta^*T^*(y)=\eta y$.{{cite:fithian2023_exp_families}} (§Exponential family structure, Poisson example)
:::

## Common misconceptions
The natural parameter is not generally the mean: Poisson uses a log mean and Bernoulli uses log odds. It is not automatically unique without fixing the statistic and base measure; the Poisson rescaling illustrates why. A canonical link describes the mean–predictor relation and does not transform every observed response. Finally, an exponential-family display alone does not guarantee completeness of its statistic or independence of repeated observations.{{cite:fithian2023_exp_families}} (§Exponential family structure){{cite:geyer2022_exp_family}} (§§3.4, 8–9)

## Assumptions and limits — When the representation needs care
At $\lambda=0$, the Poisson distribution collapses to zero but $\log\lambda$ is not a finite natural parameter; it is a boundary limit of the usual family. With unknown normal variance, a one-dimensional parameter for the mean alone is insufficient: the exponent needs both $y$ and $y^2$ terms with two natural coefficients. In a family whose support moves with its parameter, such as $\operatorname{Uniform}(0,\theta)$, the fixed-support differentiation behind the moment identities cannot simply be applied. These examples distinguish a mathematical representation from an all-purpose modeling guarantee.{{cite:fithian2023_exp_families}} (§Exponential family structure, natural parameter space){{cite:geyer2022_exp_family}} (§§3.6–3.7, 7.5)

## Connections
The [exponential family](exponential-family.qmd) provides the density form, a [sufficient statistic](sufficient-statistic.qmd) carries the data's coefficient, and the [canonical link](canonical-link.qmd) makes a GLM predictor match a chosen natural scale. The [mean and variance identities](mean-and-variance-of-an-exponential-family.qmd) arise by differentiating the log normalizer. These connections explain why Poisson log links and Bernoulli logit links are convenient while leaving room for other links.{{cite:fithian2023_exp_families}} (§Exponential family structure and §Differential identities){{cite:zhou2020_glm}} (§Link function)

## History
The paired terms “natural” and “canonical” are not used uniformly: Geyer's 2022 exposition explicitly chooses “canonical” where many writers say “natural,” following Barndorff-Nielsen's terminology. Recording the representation and any dispersion scaling is more informative than insisting on one word. In generalized linear models, this parameter scale became useful because the log and logit links make Poisson and Bernoulli means valid while retaining linear predictors.{{cite:geyer2022_exp_family}} (§3.2, Terminology; §§7.1–7.2){{cite:zhou2020_glm}} (§Link function)
