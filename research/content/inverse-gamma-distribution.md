## Lead context
It describes uncertainty about a positive variance by putting a gamma law on its reciprocal, the precision. That pairing makes normal-variance updates especially simple, while the shape parameter decides whether familiar summaries such as the mean even exist.{{cite:stan_functions2026}} (§“Gamma distribution”; §“Inverse gamma Distribution”) {{cite:raiffa_schlaifer1961}} (Ch. 11, §11.1.3, pp. 290–291)

## Motivating example
Suppose a sensor's errors are centred at a known zero, but their variance is uncertain. Four measurements close to zero favour a smaller variance than four scattered measurements. An inverse-Gamma prior turns their sum of squared errors into an updated scale parameter: the whole posterior can be calculated from the sample size and that one sum. This is useful when the variance itself, rather than a point estimate of it, must enter a prediction or a decision.{{cite:raiffa_schlaifer1961}} (Ch. 11, §§11.1.2–11.1.3, pp. 289–291)

## Intuition
The reciprocal construction explains the density's shape. If precision is gamma distributed, taking its reciprocal reverses the order: very high precision means very small variance. The inverse-Gamma density approaches zero at zero, rises to its mode, and then falls with a long right tail. Consequently its mode, mean, and variance answer different questions, and the latter two may be infinite. The PDF and CDF plots in the infobox use shape $4$ and scale $1$: the mode is $1/5$ and the mean is $1/3$, so the right tail pulls the mean beyond the peak.{{cite:stan_functions2026}} (§“Gamma distribution”; §“Inverse gamma Distribution”)

## Definition
Write $V\sim\operatorname{InvGamma}(\alpha,\beta)$ for a random variable $V>0$, with shape $\alpha>0$ and **scale** $\beta>0$; a realised variance is $v$. Its density is the one in the infobox, and $\Gamma$ is the gamma function. Equivalently, the precision $T=1/V$ has $\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta)$ law with **rate** $\beta$. Thus the same $\beta$ is the scale in the inverse-Gamma density $e^{-\beta/v}$ and the rate in the reciprocal gamma density $e^{-\beta t}$. This convention fixes the interpretation of both parameters on this page.{{cite:stan_functions2026}} (§“Gamma distribution”; §“Inverse gamma Distribution”)

The inverse-Gamma CDF can be read from the gamma tail: $F_V(v)=\Pr(T\geq1/v)$ for $v>0$. This relation makes the CDF plot and interval calculations interpretable without introducing another special function. For example, a central interval for $V$ is obtained by taking reciprocals of gamma quantiles and reversing their order.{{cite:stan_functions2026}} (§“Gamma distribution”; §“Inverse gamma Distribution”) {{cite:scipy_invgamma2026}} (§“scipy.stats.invgamma”, Notes)

## Properties
The normalising constant and all power moments follow from the same substitution. For any real $r<\alpha$, $\mathbb E(V^r)=\beta^r\Gamma(\alpha-r)/\Gamma(\alpha)$. In particular, the infobox mean requires $\alpha>1$, and its variance requires $\alpha>2$; when those conditions fail, the corresponding positive moment diverges. The mode $\beta/(\alpha+1)$ exists for every positive shape and scale. Multiplying $V$ by $c>0$ multiplies its scale by $c$, so $cV\sim\operatorname{InvGamma}(\alpha,c\beta)$. These are parameter facts, not interchangeable descriptions of a typical draw.{{cite:stan_functions2026}} (§“Inverse gamma Distribution”) {{cite:scipy_invgamma2026}} (§“scipy.stats.invgamma”, Notes)

The three linked results above use different assumptions. The known-mean update concerns one unknown normal variance. The jointly conjugate normal–inverse-Gamma model instead gives the normal mean or regression coefficients a conditional variance proportional to $V$; its joint posterior retains that factorisation. In the linked regression statement, bold $\boldsymbol\beta$ denotes a coefficient vector, while scalar $\beta$ on this page denotes the inverse-Gamma scale. The marginal-variance result shown above uses a reference prior for regression coefficients and variance and integrates the coefficients out. In that setting, with full-column-rank design, $n>p$, and positive residual sum of squares, the marginal shape is $(n-p)/2$. The $p/2$ distinction is the difference between integrating over coefficients and holding them fixed.{{cite:raiffa_schlaifer1961}} (Ch. 11, §§11.1–11.2; Ch. 13, §13.5, pp. 342–344)

**Proof idea.** Transform a gamma precision to obtain the density and moments; then multiply normal kernels and, for the regression marginal, integrate a Gaussian quadratic form.{{cite:stan_functions2026}} (§“Gamma distribution”; §“Inverse gamma Distribution”) {{cite:raiffa_schlaifer1961}} (Ch. 11, §11.1.3)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Let $T\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta)$ in shape–rate form, so $g(t)=\beta^\alpha t^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta t}/\Gamma(\alpha)$ for $t>0$. For $V=1/T$, the Jacobian is $|\mathrm dt/\mathrm dv|=v^{-2}$. Therefore $f_V(v)=g(1/v)v^{-2}=\beta^\alpha v^{-\alpha-1}e^{-\beta/v}/\Gamma(\alpha)$, which integrates to one because it is a transformed density. The same substitution gives $\mathbb E(V^r)=\beta^r\Gamma(\alpha-r)/\Gamma(\alpha)$ when $r<\alpha$; the integral diverges near $t=0$, equivalently in the right tail $v\to\infty$, when $r\geq\alpha$. Taking $r=1,2$ and subtracting the squared mean yields the infobox formulas. Differentiating $\log f_V(v)=\text{constant}-(\alpha+1)\log v-\beta/v$ gives $(\beta-(\alpha+1)v)/v^2$, positive before $v=\beta/(\alpha+1)$ and negative after it. Finally $\Pr(V\leq v)=\Pr(T\geq1/v)$, and replacing $v$ by $w/c$ in the density proves the scale rule.{{cite:stan_functions2026}} (§“Gamma distribution”; §“Inverse gamma Distribution”)

For the known-mean model, put $S_\mu=\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\mu)^2$. Independent $X_i\mid V=v\sim\operatorname N(\mu,v)$ observations give $L(v)\propto v^{-n/2}e^{-S_\mu/(2v)}$. Multiplication by the prior kernel $v^{-\alpha-1}e^{-\beta/v}$ gives $v^{-(\alpha+n/2)-1}e^{-(\beta+S_\mu/2)/v}$, proving the first linked posterior formula and showing why centring at a sample mean would change the likelihood when $\mu$ is known.{{cite:raiffa_schlaifer1961}} (Ch. 11, §§11.1.2–11.1.3, pp. 289–291)

For the jointly conjugate regression result, write $\boldsymbol y\mid\boldsymbol b,v\sim\operatorname N_n(D\boldsymbol b,vI)$, $\boldsymbol b\mid v\sim\operatorname N_p(\boldsymbol m_0,vV_0)$ with positive-definite $V_0$, and $v\sim\operatorname{InvGamma}(a_0,b_0)$. Completing the square in $\boldsymbol b$ gives $V_n=(V_0^{-1}+D^\top D)^{-1}$, $\boldsymbol m_n=V_n(V_0^{-1}\boldsymbol m_0+D^\top\boldsymbol y)$, and residual constant $R=\boldsymbol y^\top\boldsymbol y+\boldsymbol m_0^\top V_0^{-1}\boldsymbol m_0-\boldsymbol m_n^\top V_n^{-1}\boldsymbol m_n$. The joint posterior factors as $\operatorname N_p(\boldsymbol m_n,vV_n)$ times $\operatorname{InvGamma}(a_0+n/2,b_0+R/2)$: the $v^{-p/2}$ from the conditional normal is retained in that factor, while the marginal inverse-Gamma shape gains $n/2$. This proves the second linked statement, with its unspecified $a_n,b_n$ made explicit.{{cite:raiffa_schlaifer1961}} (Ch. 13, §13.5, pp. 342–344) {{cite:scipy_invgamma2026}} (§“scipy.stats.invgamma”, Notes)

For the reference-prior regression result, let $D$ have $p$ independent columns, $n>p$, and $p(\boldsymbol b,v)\propto1/v$. Completing least-squares squares gives $\|\boldsymbol y-D\boldsymbol b\|^2=\operatorname{SSE}+(\boldsymbol b-\widehat{\boldsymbol b})^\top D^\top D(\boldsymbol b-\widehat{\boldsymbol b})$. The joint posterior kernel is $v^{-n/2-1}e^{-\operatorname{SSE}/(2v)}$ times the Gaussian quadratic kernel. Integrating that kernel over $\mathbb R^p$ contributes a factor proportional to $v^{p/2}$, leaving $v^{-(n-p)/2-1}e^{-\operatorname{SSE}/(2v)}$. When $\operatorname{SSE}>0$ this is $\operatorname{InvGamma}((n-p)/2,\operatorname{SSE}/2)$. Its reciprocal multiplied by $\operatorname{SSE}$ is $\chi^2_{n-p}$, and its mean and mode give the values printed above when $n>p+2$. The full conditional at fixed $\boldsymbol b$ keeps shape $n/2$, so it must not be substituted for this marginal.{{cite:raiffa_schlaifer1961}} (Ch. 13, §13.5, pp. 342–344)

## Statistical inference
For a normal sample with known mean, the likelihood statistic is $S_\mu$, the sum of squared deviations from that fixed mean. Its variance MLE is $S_\mu/n$; under the inverse-Gamma prior, the posterior shape and scale become $\alpha+n/2$ and $\beta+S_\mu/2$. If the posterior shape exceeds one, the posterior mean is $(\beta+S_\mu/2)/(\alpha+n/2-1)$; the posterior mode uses $\alpha+n/2+1$ in the denominator. A central credible interval uses inverse-Gamma quantiles, or reciprocal gamma quantiles with reversed endpoints. These are different summaries of the same posterior, chosen for different inferential questions.{{cite:raiffa_schlaifer1961}} (Ch. 11, §§11.1.2–11.1.3, pp. 289–291) {{cite:stan_functions2026}} (§“Gamma distribution”; §“Inverse gamma Distribution”)

For the jointly unknown normal mean and variance, distinguish an independent normal prior on the mean from a normal prior conditional on the variance. Only the latter is the usual jointly conjugate normal–inverse-Gamma construction. With an independent prior, an inverse-Gamma update for $V$ is a *full conditional* given the current mean; it does not make the marginal posterior of $V$ inverse-Gamma. In hierarchical models, conditional conjugacy also leaves the choice of prior scale to assess: Gelman's eight-schools comparison found posterior sensitivity to the small hyperparameter in an inverse-Gamma$(\epsilon,\epsilon)$ prior for a group-level variance.{{cite:raiffa_schlaifer1961}} (Ch. 11, §11.5; Ch. 13, §13.5) {{cite:gelman2006}} (§§3.1, 4.3, 5.1, pp. 519–524)

## Generating random samples
Draw $T$ from a gamma distribution with shape $\alpha$ and rate $\beta$, then return $V=1/T$. In SciPy, `invgamma.rvs(a, scale=b)` uses shape `a` and scale `b`; its default scale is one. A direct gamma generator must instead receive the reciprocal scale $1/\beta$ if it takes a scale argument. The plots on this page use SciPy's `invgamma` with shape $4$ and scale $1$.{{cite:scipy_invgamma2026}} (§“scipy.stats.invgamma”, Notes and Examples) {{cite:stan_functions2026}} (§“Gamma distribution”; §“Inverse gamma Distribution”)

## Worked example
Take three independent readings $x=(1,2,3)$ from $\operatorname N(0,V)$, with known mean zero and prior $V\sim\operatorname{InvGamma}(3,2)$. The squared-deviation sum is $S_0=1^2+2^2+3^2=14$. Thus $V\mid x\sim\operatorname{InvGamma}(3+3/2,2+14/2)=\operatorname{InvGamma}(9/2,9)$. Its posterior mean is $9/(9/2-1)=18/7\approx2.571$, its mode is $9/(9/2+1)=18/11\approx1.636$, and the variance MLE is $14/3\approx4.667$. The prior mean was $1$; the observations shift the posterior toward larger variances, while the right tail keeps its mean above its mode.{{cite:raiffa_schlaifer1961}} (Ch. 11, §§11.1.2–11.1.3, pp. 289–291) {{cite:stan_functions2026}} (§“Inverse gamma Distribution”)

**Your turn.** With known mean zero, take two readings $1,-1$ and prior $V\sim\operatorname{InvGamma}(2,3)$. Find the posterior law, mean, and mode.{{cite:raiffa_schlaifer1961}} (Ch. 11, §§11.1.2–11.1.3, pp. 289–291)

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
Here $S_0=1^2+(-1)^2=2$, so $V\mid x\sim\operatorname{InvGamma}(2+1,3+1)=\operatorname{InvGamma}(3,4)$. The mean is $4/(3-1)=2$ and the mode is $4/(3+1)=1$. The sample is centred exactly on the known mean, but its two nonzero errors still contribute to the variance update.
:::

## Common misconceptions
The second parameter $\beta$ is a scale for $V$ and a rate for its reciprocal precision; putting it in $e^{-\beta v}$ changes the distribution. A proper inverse-Gamma law exists for every $\alpha,\beta>0$, even though its mean requires $\alpha>1$ and variance requires $\alpha>2$. The mode is the highest point of a density, not a probability or a variance formula. Finally, conjugacy for $V$ with a known normal mean does not imply that an independently prior-modelled unknown mean leaves the *marginal* variance posterior inverse-Gamma.{{cite:stan_functions2026}} (§“Gamma distribution”; §“Inverse gamma Distribution”) {{cite:gelman2006}} (§3.1, p. 519)

## Assumptions and limits
The conjugate update shown here assumes conditionally independent normal observations with a specified, known mean and a common positive variance. Replacing that mean by $\bar x$ changes the likelihood calculation. In regression, the reference-prior marginal formula additionally needs a full-rank design, more observations than coefficients, and positive residual sum of squares for a proper inverse-Gamma posterior. An inverse-Gamma prior on a variance is an explicit choice about that scale; small shape and scale parameters alone do not guarantee weak prior influence when the likelihood permits near-zero variance.{{cite:raiffa_schlaifer1961}} (Ch. 11, §§11.1.2–11.1.3; Ch. 13, §13.5, pp. 342–344) {{cite:gelman2006}} (§§4.3, 5.1, pp. 522–524)

## Connections
A [gamma precision](gamma-distribution.qmd) and an inverse-Gamma variance are exact reciprocals under the shape–rate/shape–scale conventions above. The [scaled inverse-chi-squared distribution](chi-squared-distribution.qmd) corresponds to shape $\nu/2$ and scale $\nu s^2/2$. A dependent [normal–inverse-Gamma prior](normal-inverse-gamma-prior.qmd) extends the scalar variance update to an unknown normal mean or regression coefficients; integrating those coefficients gives a marginal variance law, while conditioning on them gives a different full conditional. These relationships explain the three linked results in Properties.{{cite:stan_functions2026}} (§“Gamma distribution”; §“Inverse gamma Distribution”; §“Scaled inverse chi-square distribution”) {{cite:raiffa_schlaifer1961}} (Ch. 11, §11.1.3; Ch. 13, §13.5, pp. 342–344)

## History
Raiffa and Schlaifer's 1961 treatment of a normal process with known mean assigned a gamma conjugate law to its precision and derived the update from the normal likelihood. Expressing that precision as a reciprocal yields the inverse-Gamma variance formulation used here. In 2006, Gelman analysed inverse-Gamma priors for hierarchical variance parameters and showed why small equal shape and scale values could exert substantial influence in near-zero variance settings. The first calculation explains the family's popularity; the latter result makes prior-scale checking part of its use.{{cite:raiffa_schlaifer1961}} (Ch. 11, §§11.1.2–11.1.3, pp. 289–291) {{cite:gelman2006}} (§§3.1, 4.3, 5.1, pp. 519–524)
