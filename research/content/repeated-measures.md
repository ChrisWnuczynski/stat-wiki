## Motivating example
Suppose blood pressure is recorded before treatment and at four later visits for each patient. The five readings from one patient are connected by that patient's physiology and baseline health; five patients would provide a different amount of information from five visits to one patient. The same issue arises for growth measurements, repeated laboratory assays, or trees sharing soil. The analysis must identify the independent sampling units before it can attach a standard error to a time or treatment effect.{{cite:schober_vetter2018}} (pp. 569–570, introduction and repeated-measures overview)

## Definition
**Repeated measures** are multiple observations of a response on the same unit, often at successive times or under distinct conditions. Index the unit by $i$ and the occasion by $j$, writing $Y_{ij}$. Repetition can reveal within-unit change, but $Y_{i1},Y_{i2},\ldots$ generally share an unobserved subject effect or serial process. Thus the number of rows is not the number of independent units. Covariates can explain part of this dependence; remaining errors may still be correlated. Mixed models and marginal models describe it in different ways.{{cite:schober_vetter2018}} (pp. 569–573, overview and regression approaches){{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, conditional linear mixed model)

## Intuition
If one patient tends to have higher blood pressure than another at every visit, their repeated readings move together. A random-intercept model writes $Y_{ij}=x_{ij}^{\mathsf T}\beta+b_i+\epsilon_{ij}$, with subject intercept variance $\tau^2$ and residual variance $\sigma^2$. Under independent intercepts and residuals, two readings on the same subject have covariance $\tau^2$ and correlation $\rho=\tau^2/(\tau^2+\sigma^2)$. A large $\rho$ means that extra visits add less independent information about a population mean than extra patients do. This simple model is useful, but it imposes a nonnegative, constant within-subject correlation.{{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, conditional and random-effect distributions){{cite:schober_vetter2018}} (pp. 572–573, mixed-effect models)

Correlation need not always be positive. Two trees sharing soil may respond similarly to rainfall, yet compete for light or nutrients so that one tree's gain accompanies another's loss. With $m$ exchangeable measurements and a common pairwise correlation $\rho$, the covariance matrix is valid only if $-1/(m-1)\le\rho\le1$. Thus negative dependence is possible but its magnitude is constrained as the cluster grows; a pure random-intercept model cannot create it. Other models can allow negative or time-decaying associations.{{cite:schober_vetter2018}} (pp. 571–573, covariance structures and mixed models)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
For $j\ne k$, independence in the random-intercept model gives $\operatorname{Cov}(Y_{ij},Y_{ik})=\operatorname{Cov}(b_i+\epsilon_{ij},b_i+\epsilon_{ik})=\tau^2$; each variance is $\tau^2+\sigma^2$, proving the stated correlation. If there are $n$ independent subjects and $m$ visits per subject, their grand mean is $\bar Y=\mu+n^{-1}\sum_i b_i+(nm)^{-1}\sum_{ij}\epsilon_{ij}$ for a constant mean, so $\operatorname{Var}(\bar Y)=\tau^2/n+\sigma^2/(nm)$. Treating all $nm$ readings as independent would instead use $(\tau^2+\sigma^2)/(nm)$, underestimating variance when $m>1$ and $\tau^2>0$.{{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, conditional and random-effect distributions)

For $m$ variables with common variance $v$ and pairwise correlation $\rho$, the covariance matrix is $v[(1-\rho)I_m+\rho\mathbf1\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}]$. Along the all-ones direction its eigenvalue is $v[1+(m-1)\rho]$; on the $m-1$ perpendicular directions each eigenvalue is $v(1-\rho)$. Both must be nonnegative, yielding $-1/(m-1)\le\rho\le1$.{{cite:schober_vetter2018}} (pp. 571–572, compound-symmetry working structure)
:::
:::

## Statistical use
When only two occasions matter, differences within each subject remove a stable subject intercept, and a paired test analyzes independent subject differences. For more visits, one can summarize each trajectory by a mean or slope if that summary addresses the scientific question. Repeated-measures ANOVA compares related means under restrictive covariance assumptions. A marginal approach such as generalized estimating equations targets population-average mean effects with a working correlation; a mixed model adds subject-specific random effects and can describe individual trajectories. The choice depends on whether the question concerns average response or differences among subjects, and on how the outcome, timing and missing observations behave.{{cite:schober_vetter2018}} (pp. 570–573, summary statistics, ANOVA, GEE and mixed-effect models){{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.2, sleepstudy example)

## Worked example
Ten patients each have two measurements with a common mean. Suppose the between-patient intercept variance is $\tau^2=9$ and occasion-error variance is $\sigma^2=1$. Each reading has variance $10$, and the within-patient correlation is $9/10=0.9$. The average of all 20 readings has variance $9/10+1/20=0.95$, giving standard error $\sqrt{0.95}\approx0.975$. An independence calculation would use $10/20=0.50$ and standard error $\sqrt{0.50}\approx0.707$. Repeated readings improve precision, but here most variation is between patients, so doubling visits cannot substitute for doubling independent patients.{{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, covariance components)

**Your turn.** Five independent subjects each have two readings under a random-intercept model with $\tau^2=4$ and $\sigma^2=1$. Find the within-subject correlation and variance of the grand mean. What variance would an independence calculation report?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
Each reading has variance $5$, and the within-subject correlation is $4/5=0.8$. With five subjects and ten readings, $\operatorname{Var}(\bar Y)=4/5+1/10=0.9$. Treating the ten rows as independent gives $5/10=0.5$, understating the variance by $0.4$.{{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, covariance components)
:::

## Common misconceptions
Repeated measures are not automatically technical replicates: readings at different visits may measure meaningful change, while repeated assays of one specimen may measure instrument noise. A large row count does not create as many independent subjects. A random intercept does not model every dependence pattern, because it forces the same nonnegative correlation between any two visits. Conversely, allowing within-subject correlation does not by itself resolve informative dropout. Finally, a subject-specific coefficient from a nonlinear mixed model and a population-average coefficient from a marginal model generally answer different questions.{{cite:schober_vetter2018}} (pp. 569–573, study designs and model interpretations){{cite:bell_kenward2013}} (Statistical analysis: what works and what does not work)

## Assumptions and limits — When the simple model fails
If measurements close in time are strongly related but distant ones weakly related, a constant-correlation random intercept leaves serial structure unmodelled. If two trees compete, their within-block association can be negative, which a nonnegative random-intercept variance cannot reproduce. If sicker patients drop out before later visits and the missing outcomes depend on their unobserved condition, simply fitting a mixed model to observed rows need not recover the intended population trajectory. These situations require a covariance or missingness strategy matched to the mechanism.{{cite:schober_vetter2018}} (pp. 571–573, covariance choices and limitations of model specification){{cite:bell_kenward2013}} (Statistical analysis: what works and what does not work)

## Connections
Intraclass correlation measures how strongly rows within a unit cluster in the simple random-intercept setting. Paired samples use within-unit differences; marginal models emphasize the average response; random slopes let subjects change at different rates; and restricted maximum likelihood estimates variance components in Gaussian mixed models. The repeated-measures label describes the data structure, not a unique test.{{cite:schober_vetter2018}} (pp. 570–573, analytic approaches){{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§§1.1–1.2, random effects and repeated sleepstudy measurements)

## History
Repeated-measures ANOVA was a traditional way to compare related means. Later longitudinal analysis made room for flexible regression approaches, including marginal models and mixed models that can express subject-specific baselines or trajectories. The change was driven by the need to describe actual within-unit dependence and irregular observation patterns, rather than forcing every dataset into a balanced grid of measurements.{{cite:schober_vetter2018}} (pp. 570–573, traditional and regression approaches)
