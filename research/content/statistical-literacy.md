## Lead context

A news article says that a risk doubled. A chart starts the axis at a flattering height. A poll reports a percentage with no denominator and no sample size. Statistical literacy is the ability to read those claims as a consumer: to see what was measured, what was compared, and what the number does not say. It is not the ability to derive a sampling distribution. Courses aimed at consumers and courses aimed at future producers of analyses are different jobs, and literacy is the first of them. The college guidelines put the consumer's task in one sentence. Students should become critical consumers of statistically based results reported in popular media, recognising whether a reported result reasonably follows from the study and the analysis that were done.{{cite:gaise2016}} (pp. 7–8)

## Definition

Statistical literacy is the competence of a critical consumer of statistical results, especially results reported in public. The college guidelines distinguish introductory courses that address literacy from courses that address methods, and they describe the distinction as courses for consumers versus courses for producers. The first goal they set for students is the consumer's goal. A further goal is to produce graphical displays and numerical summaries and to interpret what graphs do and do not reveal. Another is to recognise the central role of variability, and another the central role of randomness in designing studies and in drawing conclusions. Literacy, on that account, is about claims in the wild and about the concepts that make those claims readable. It is not fluency with every technique in a methods course.{{cite:gaise2016}} (pp. 7–8)

The scope includes official percentages, medical headlines, and charts in a course. It does not include, by itself, the design of a complex sample or the fitting of a hierarchical model. Those are producer skills. A literate reader knows when a producer is needed, which is itself one of the things a consumer course is for. The guidelines are explicit that there is no single introductory course. Some aim at a general audience, some at life sciences, business, engineering, or mathematics, and class sizes and prerequisites differ. The consumer-versus-producer split is one of those differences, and the six recommendations are meant to apply across it. Mastering specific techniques is described as less important than understanding the concepts that underlie them. Literacy is the part of that understanding that a person uses when the technique was someone else's.{{cite:gaise2016}} (pp. 7–8)

## Properties

The questions a literate reading asks are standard, and each of them is a property of the claim rather than a mood about numbers. Was the study an experiment or an observational study? Studies in which the researchers assign treatments are experiments, and a randomised experiment is the design from which a causal sentence is often reasonable. An observational comparison supports an association. It does not, on its own, support a causal sentence, because a confounding variable can move both the supposed cause and the outcome. Sunscreen use and skin cancer can rise together because sun exposure moves both. A headline that drops the third variable has failed the first GAISE goal: the reported result does not follow from the study that was done.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.4, §1.4){{cite:gaise2016}} (p. 8)

Who was actually measured? A sample is a subset of a population, and the population is the set the question named. Sixty swordfish estimate the mercury content of Atlantic swordfish only as a sample from that population. A convenience sample, or a survey with heavy non-response, describes the people who were reached. A nutrition student picking graduates by hand may over-represent health fields. A 1936 telephone frame described households with telephones, which at the time were the richer households, and the electorate was a different population. The literate question is whether the people in the denominator are the people in the sentence.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.1, §1.3.3, §1.3.5){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 469)

What was the baseline? A relative comparison without the absolute level can turn a rare event into an alarming ratio. Doubling a risk of one in a hundred thousand is a different public fact from doubling a risk of one in twenty, and the ratio hides the difference. The absolute risk, the denominator, and the sample size are the numbers that make the ratio readable. A percentage with none of them is not yet a statistical claim. Variability is the reason a single percentage is not the whole report. Two groups with the same mean can have very different spreads, and a graph is often what shows the spread the mean omitted. Three distributions can share a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 and still look nothing alike. Goal 3 of the guidelines, reading what a graph does not reveal, is the skill that catches an axis that does not start at zero, a bin width that manufactures a mode, and a map of counts that is really a map of population.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 2, §2.1.4){{cite:gaise2016}} (p. 8)

What would the design have done to the conclusion? Random assignment, a known chance of selection, and a comparison group that did not receive the treatment are the features that make a contrast interpretable. A systematic assignment that feels orderly, the first half treated and the second half not, is not randomisation. A control on a condition, such as every patient drinking the same glass of water, is not a control group. A literate reader separates those, because the headline often uses one word for all of them. Randomness in the design is what the guidelines ask students to recognise as central, and it is what a consumer looks for when a causal verb appears in a sentence about an observational file.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.4.1){{cite:gaise2016}} (p. 8){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "randomization, scientific")

## History

Statistical education has long included a hope that citizens could read quantitative arguments. The administrative ancestor of public numbers is the census, from antiquity through the Roman enumerations, and the arguments about what those numbers meant are as old as the counts. The sampling revolution of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries changed which claims a citizen was being asked to trust. In 1895 Kiaer argued for representative samples and was opposed by statisticians who held that only a full count would do. By the 1925 Rome congress the argument had shifted to which sampling method to use. The 1936 telephone prediction, which named the losing candidate because the frame was households with telephones, is the episode that made a bad sample visible to a wide audience. Literacy, in that episode, was the ability to ask who owned a telephone.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 152, 468–469)

The 2005 college guidelines, revised and re-endorsed in 2016, made the consumer goal explicit and separated it from the producer goal. The 2016 report treats literacy courses and methods courses as a standing split in what colleges actually teach. It also states goals that a consumer and a producer share: variability, randomness, graphical interpretation, and an awareness of ethical issues in statistical practice. Between the editions, the report's concern is the introductory course as it is actually offered, across institutions and audiences, rather than a single syllabus. The consumer goal is the first of the nine goals printed for that course. A reader who can ask for the denominator, the design, and the absolute risk is exercising it. A reader who can also build the design is doing something further, which the guidelines call a methods course. Both are legitimate. The first is literacy.{{cite:gaise2016}} (pp. 7–8){{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 468–469)

It is what makes [official statistics](official-statistics.qmd) usable by people who will never see the microdata. Principle 3 of the Fundamental Principles asks agencies to present sources, methods, and procedures so that interpretation is possible, and Principle 4 entitles them to comment on erroneous interpretation and misuse. A literate reader is the person those principles are written for, as well as the agencies that publish. A [statistical survey](statistical-survey.qmd) that reports a percentage without the sampling fraction, the frame, and the non-response has withheld the facts literacy is the ability to request. An [observational study](observational-study.qmd) reported with a causal verb is the claim the confounding-variable question is meant to stop. A [histogram](histogram.qmd) or a [scatterplot](scatterplot.qmd) is the display goal 3 asks a student to read for what it hides. [Sampling bias](sampling-bias.qmd) is the failure the 1936 frame illustrates. The producer skills that go beyond literacy live in the methods course: designing the sample, fitting the model, and computing the interval. Literacy is the ability to tell when those skills have been used, and when a sentence has outrun them.{{cite:gaise2016}} (pp. 7–8){{cite:un_res68261}} (Principles 3, 4){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.4)
