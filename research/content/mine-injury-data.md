## Motivating example
Forty-four mines have injury counts and measurements of inner burden thickness, extraction below the seam and mine age. A log-link count model expresses changes in the fitted mean as multiplicative factors. Its coefficients describe conditional associations in these observations; unequal exposure, selection into operating mines and omitted working conditions can all matter to the interpretation. A count alone is not an injury rate per worker-hour.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§§5.1.1, 5.2.4, regression and exposure offsets)

## Intuition — Change one coordinate while holding the others fixed
With mean $\mu(x)=\exp(\beta_0+\beta_1x_1+\beta_2x_2+\beta_3x_3)$, changing coordinate $j$ by $d$ gives the ratio
$$\frac{\mu(x+d e_j)}{\mu(x)}=e^{d\beta_j}.$$
The other coordinates cancel. This identity explains the units of a log-link coefficient: a percentage-point change in extraction and a year of age are different increments. Centering a covariate at a meaningful value changes the intercept's interpretation without changing fitted means.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§5.1, log-link regression){{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§8.8.3, count-data GLMs)

## Worked example — Recomputing the fitted summaries
Using `mine` from the official `locfit` data package, the specified Poisson fit gives coefficients $(-3.7206821,-0.0014793,0.0627011,-0.0316514)$, with $44-4=40$ residual degrees of freedom. The quasi-Poisson fit gives the same coefficients because dividing the score by a common positive dispersion does not change its roots. Its estimated Pearson dispersion is $0.9049145$. Multiplying the Poisson standard errors by $\sqrt{0.9049145}\approx0.951269$ gives the quasi-Poisson standard errors. R uses normal reference tails for the fixed-dispersion Poisson summary and $t_{40}$ tails for the estimated-dispersion quasi summary. A common dispersion multiplies the quasi-score by $1/\phi$ and does not move its root.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§§5.3.1–5.3.2, residuals, dispersion and Wald statistics){{cite:wedderburn1974}} (pp. 439–447)

For the age coefficient, these choices give $z=-1.94068$, two-sided normal $p=0.05230$, and $t=-2.04007$, two-sided $t_{40}$ $p=0.04798$. Crossing $0.05$ is a small numerical change in evidence, not a discontinuity in a scientific conclusion. The estimated dispersion being below one is a property of residual variation relative to the chosen conditional mean model; it does not identify a cause of underdispersion.

For an illustrative covariate profile $(x_1,x_2,x_3)=(100,70,5)$, the rounded equation gives $\eta=0.3598$ and $\widehat\mu=e^{0.3598}\approx1.433043$. Increasing extraction by ten percentage points changes the mean by $e^{0.627}\approx1.871986$, to about $2.68264$. Increasing age by ten years gives multiplier $e^{-0.317}\approx0.728331$, a decrease of about $27.17\%$ under the fitted equation. These calculations use the rounded coefficients for a transparent comparison, rather than the more precise coefficients of the refit.

**Your turn.** Using the rounded coefficient $0.0627$, what is the fitted mean multiplier for five additional percentage points of extraction? Does it describe a causal effect?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The multiplier is $e^{5(0.0627)}=e^{0.3135}\approx1.368205$. It is a conditional fitted association; a causal interpretation would require additional design and confounding assumptions.
:::

## Properties — Score and dispersion are distinct tasks
For independent Poisson counts with log link, differentiating the log-likelihood gives $U(\beta)=\sum_i x_i(y_i-\mu_i)$. Its negative derivative is $X^\top\operatorname{diag}(\mu_i)X$, so the log-likelihood is concave. For an intercept model at a finite stationary point, the first score component implies $\sum_i\widehat\mu_i=\sum_i y_i$. This balance is algebraic and does not prove that the mean model is correct.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§§5.1.3, 5.2.4, canonical link and balance property)

The Pearson estimate $\widehat\phi=(n-p)^{-1}\sum_i(y_i-\widehat\mu_i)^2/\widehat\mu_i$ estimates residual dispersion after estimating the mean. Quasi-Poisson uses the variance function $V(\mu)=\mu$ with one common multiplier $\phi$, which is the quasi-likelihood specification for that mean–variance pair. It cannot by itself account for an arbitrary variance pattern, dependence or omitted exposure. A model with worker-hours $e_i$ would commonly include $\log e_i$ as an offset when the target is a rate, which changes the interpretation of the response mean.{{cite:wedderburn1974}} (pp. 439–447){{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§§5.2.4, 5.3.1)

## Assumptions and limits
The measurements are observational. Survivor selection is a possible explanation for an age association, but the fitted coefficient and dispersion cannot determine whether such selection occurred. Variable selection also changes the meaning of ordinary coefficient p-values when its randomness is ignored. Scientific interpretation should therefore report the conditioning variables and exposure definition, examine residual patterns and uncertainty, and distinguish a fitted association from a policy intervention.{{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§8.10, further statistical concepts){{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§5.3, model validation)

## Interpretation — Multipliers on the log-link scale
The fitted log mean is linear in the recorded covariates, so a change of $d$ units in one covariate, with the others held at the recorded values, multiplies the mean by $e^{d\beta_j}$. For the rounded extraction coefficient $0.0627$, one percentage point multiplies the mean by $e^{0.0627}\approx1.064707$. Five percentage points multiply it by $e^{0.3135}\approx1.368205$. Ten percentage points, the step used in the profile calculation, multiply it by $e^{0.627}\approx1.871986$. These factors are not $1+d\times0.0627$. The linear approximation $1+0.0627=1.0627$ is close to the one-point factor and is not the ten-point factor. The coefficient is an additive change in the log mean and a multiplicative change in the mean.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§5.1, log-link regression){{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§8.8.3, count-data GLMs)

The age coefficient $-0.0316514$ is a change per year of the recorded age variable. One year multiplies the fitted mean by $e^{-0.0316514}\approx0.968844$ when the other covariates stay fixed. The rounded step of ten years used above, multiplier $e^{-0.317}\approx0.728331$, is the tenth power of a one-year factor only for the rounded coefficient $-0.0317$, since $10\times0.0317=0.317$. The more precise coefficient gives $e^{-0.316514}\approx0.728685$ over ten years. The two displays are the same model read at two rounding levels. Neither display is a percentage change in the age variable. A one-percent change in age would be a multiplicative step in the covariate, which is a different increment.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§5.1, log-link regression)

The profile $(100,70,5)$ has linear predictor $0.3598$ under the rounded equation and fitted mean $e^{0.3598}\approx1.433043$. Adding one extraction percentage point raises the linear predictor to $0.4225$ and the mean to about $1.5258$. The ratio of those means is the one-point multiplier $1.064707$. The absolute increase is about $0.0927$ injuries in the fitted mean, which depends on the baseline $1.433$. The multiplier does not. Quoting the absolute increase as if it applied at every mine confuses a difference of means with a ratio of means.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§5.1, log-link regression)

## Common misconceptions
The Poisson and quasi-Poisson coefficient vectors match because the estimating equation is the same up to $\phi$. The standard errors do not match. The Pearson dispersion $0.9049145$ is below one, and $\sqrt{0.9049145}\approx0.951269$ scales the Poisson standard errors down. The age statistic moves from $z=-1.94068$ with normal tail probability $0.05230$ to $t=-2.04007$ with $t_{40}$ tail probability $0.04798$. The scientific estimate of the age multiplier is the same number in both fits. The reference distribution used to compare that estimate with zero is not. A threshold of $0.05$ can classify the two tail probabilities differently while the coefficients stay fixed to the printed decimals.{{cite:wedderburn1974}} (pp. 439–447){{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§§5.3.1–5.3.2, residuals, dispersion and Wald statistics)

The response in the fit is a count of injuries, not a rate per worker-hour. An offset $\log e_i$ would make the linear predictor a log rate and the mean count $e_i$ times that rate. Without the offset, a mine with more workers can have a larger mean for that reason alone, and the coefficients absorb whatever mix of exposure and risk is present in the recorded covariates. The dispersion estimate does not restore the missing exposure. It rescales residual variance around the fitted mean. Dependence between mines, a variance that is not proportional to the mean, and selection of which mines appear in the table are all outside that rescaling.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§§5.2.4, 5.3.1){{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§8.8.3, count-data GLMs)
