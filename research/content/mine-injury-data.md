## Motivating example
The `mine` data in `locfit` record counts of fractures in the upper coal seam.{{cite:loader1999_locfit}} (pp. 62–63, Ex. 4.2, Fig. 4.1) The [official `locfit` manual](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/locfit/refman/locfit.html#mine) (`mine`, Description and Format) identifies `frac` as the response. The [MPV manual](https://cran.case.edu/web/packages/MPV/refman/MPV.html#p13.7) (`p13.7`, Description and Format) describes 44 observations and defines the measurements: inner burden thickness in feet, percent extraction of the lower seam, lower-seam height in feet, and years of operation. These measurements also appear in the 2010 analysis by Myers and colleagues.{{cite:myers_glm2010}} (p. 181, Ex. 4.13, Tab. 4.35) The fit below uses `inb`, `extrp` and `time`, omitting `seamh`. Its target is the conditional mean fracture count. A record count of 44 does not by itself establish independent sampling of 44 distinct mines. The `locfit` documentation cites Myers's 1990 regression book as its source; the 2010 example cited here is a separate published analysis.

## Intuition — Change one coordinate while holding the others fixed
With mean $\mu(x)=\exp(\beta_0+\beta_1x_1+\beta_2x_2+\beta_3x_3)$, changing coordinate $j$ by $d$ gives the ratio
$$\frac{\mu(x+d e_j)}{\mu(x)}=e^{d\beta_j}.$$
The other coordinates cancel. This identity explains the units of a log-link coefficient: a percentage-point change in extraction and a year of operation are different increments. Centering a covariate at a meaningful value changes the intercept's interpretation without changing fitted means.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (Eq. (5.5), with the log link)

## Worked example — Recomputing the fitted summaries
The three-predictor equation agrees with the selected model in Myers and colleagues, whose $x_4$ is the `time` variable labelled $x_3$ here.{{cite:myers_glm2010}} (pp. 182–183, Ex. 4.13, Tab. 4.36) Using `mine` from the official `locfit` data package, the specified Poisson fit gives coefficients $(-3.7206821,-0.0014793,0.0627011,-0.0316514)$, with $44-4=40$ residual degrees of freedom. The quasi-Poisson fit gives the same coefficients because dividing the score by a common positive dispersion does not change its roots.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (Eq. (5.9)) Its estimated Pearson dispersion is $0.9049145$. This is distinct from the deviance divided by residual degrees of freedom, reported as $0.9508$ by Myers and colleagues.{{cite:myers_glm2010}} (p. 183, Tab. 4.36) Multiplying the Poisson standard errors by $\sqrt{0.9049145}\approx0.951269$ gives the quasi-Poisson standard errors.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (Eq. (5.30)) R uses normal reference tails for the fixed-dispersion Poisson summary and $t_{40}$ tails for the estimated-dispersion quasi summary, as documented in [`summary.glm`](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/summary.glm.html) (Details). This software convention does not establish exact finite-sample coverage for either procedure.

For the coefficient of recorded years of operation, these choices give $z=-1.94068$, two-sided normal $p=0.05230$, and $t=-2.04007$, two-sided $t_{40}$ $p=0.04798$. Crossing $0.05$ is a small numerical change in evidence, not a discontinuity in a scientific conclusion. The estimated dispersion being below one is a property of residual variation relative to the chosen conditional mean model; it does not identify a cause of underdispersion.

For an illustrative covariate profile $(x_1,x_2,x_3)=(100,70,5)$, the rounded equation gives $\eta=0.3598$ and $\widehat\mu=e^{0.3598}\approx1.433043$. Increasing extraction by ten percentage points changes the mean by $e^{0.627}\approx1.871986$, to about $2.68264$. Increasing recorded years of operation by ten gives multiplier $e^{-0.317}\approx0.728331$, a decrease of about $27.17\%$ under the fitted equation. These calculations use the rounded coefficients for a transparent comparison, rather than the more precise coefficients of the refit.

## Properties — Score and dispersion are distinct tasks
For independent Poisson counts with log link, differentiating the log-likelihood gives $U(\beta)=\sum_i x_i(y_i-\mu_i)$, where $x_i$ includes the intercept coordinate. Its negative derivative is $X^\top\operatorname{diag}(\mu_i)X$, so the log-likelihood is concave.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (Eq. (5.9)) For a model containing an intercept, the first score component at a finite stationary point implies $\sum_i\widehat\mu_i=\sum_i y_i$. This balance is algebraic and does not prove that the mean model is correct.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (Cor. 5.7; Rem. 5.8)

The Pearson estimate $\widehat\phi=(n-p)^{-1}\sum_i(y_i-\widehat\mu_i)^2/\widehat\mu_i$ estimates residual dispersion after fitting $p$ mean parameters. It uses squared Pearson residuals, which differ from deviance residuals.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (Ex. 5.16; Eq. (5.30)) Quasi-Poisson specifies $\operatorname{Var}(Y_i\mid x_i)=\phi\mu_i$ with a common positive $\phi$. This permits a common scale adjustment; it does not automatically correct an arbitrary variance pattern or dependence between observations.

## Assumptions and limits
The fitted equation describes observational associations conditional on inner burden, lower-seam extraction and recorded years of operation. Treating the rows as conditionally independent and specifying the conditional mean correctly are modelling assumptions, not consequences of the data having 44 rows. A causal interpretation requires additional evidence about the design and confounding. Neither the coefficient of `time` nor the Pearson estimate establishes a selection mechanism or explains the physical process that generated the fractures. Residual patterns can reveal discrepancies that a single dispersion estimate conceals.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (Ex. 5.16) For these fracture data, Loader compares linear and local fits in extraction percentage and finds evidence against a simple log-linear extraction-only model; that comparison does not directly test the three-predictor equation used here.{{cite:loader1999_locfit}} (pp. 166–167, Ex. 9.2, Tab. 9.2)

## Interpretation — Multipliers on the log-link scale
The fitted log mean is linear in the recorded covariates, so a change of $d$ units in one covariate, with the others held fixed, multiplies the mean by $e^{d\beta_j}$. For the rounded extraction coefficient $0.0627$, the ten-percentage-point step used in the worked example gives $e^{0.627}\approx1.871986$. The factor is exponential, not $1+d\beta_j$: the latter is only a first-order approximation near $d\beta_j=0$. A percentage-point increment adds to the recorded percentage; a relative percent change multiplies it and therefore depends on the starting extraction percentage.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (Eq. (5.5), with the log link)

The coefficient $-0.0316514$ is a change per recorded year of operation. One year multiplies the fitted mean by $e^{-0.0316514}\approx0.968844$ when the other covariates stay fixed. The rounded ten-year multiplier $e^{-0.317}\approx0.728331$ is the tenth power of a one-year factor only for the rounded coefficient $-0.0317$, since $10\times0.0317=0.317$. The more precise coefficient gives $e^{-0.316514}\approx0.728685$ over ten years. The two displays use the same fitted model at different rounding levels. Neither display represents a percent change in the recorded time variable.

At any baseline profile $x$, the fitted absolute difference after an extraction increment $d$ is $\widehat\mu(x)\{e^{d\widehat\beta_2}-1\}$, whereas the ratio is $e^{d\widehat\beta_2}$. Thus the same increment has the same fitted ratio at every profile under this model, but its absolute difference depends on the baseline mean. These statements follow by subtracting or dividing the two fitted means; they do not require interpreting the association as causal.

## Common misconceptions
The Poisson and quasi-Poisson coefficient vectors match because the estimating equations differ only by a common positive factor. The standard errors and reference distributions need not match. Here the Pearson estimate $0.9049145$ scales the Poisson standard errors down by $\sqrt{0.9049145}\approx0.951269$. The statistic for recorded years of operation moves from $z=-1.94068$ with normal tail probability $0.05230$ to $t=-2.04007$ with $t_{40}$ tail probability $0.04798$. The estimated mean multiplier remains the same. A threshold of $0.05$ can classify the two tail probabilities differently while the coefficient is unchanged.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (Eq. (5.9); Eq. (5.30))

The response is a fracture count in the upper coal seam, as specified in the [`locfit` manual](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/locfit/refman/locfit.html#mine) (`mine`, Description). It is not a count of worker injuries. The fitted mean, its multiplicative contrasts and its residual dispersion must all be interpreted for that response. The scale estimate measures residual variation relative to the fitted mean; it does not identify why the variation takes that form or establish that the conditional mean is adequate.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (Ex. 5.16; Eq. (5.30))
