## Statement
The normal update in the displayed statement is **conditional** on the population mean $\mu$, within-group variance $\sigma^2$ and between-group variance $\tau^2$. For group $j$, the data precision is $n_j/\sigma^2$ and the prior precision is $1/\tau^2$. Their sum determines the conditional posterior variance, while their relative sizes determine how far the conditional posterior mean moves from the observed $\bar y_j$ toward $\mu$.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (§2.5, p. 40, eq. 2.10) If $\mu$ and $\tau$ are themselves unknown, a full hierarchical analysis must also learn them from all groups and average over their posterior uncertainty.

## Motivating example
An education office compares average test scores from many tutorial classes. A class with two students may have a striking average because both students happened to score well; a class with one hundred students measures its own class mean much more precisely. A hierarchical model allows each class to have its own mean while describing those means as draws from a population centred at $\mu$. The formula in the statement tells us how much to trust each class average at fixed population parameters. It does not force every class to have the same mean, and it does not treat an extreme two-student average as equally precise as an extreme hundred-student average.

## Intuition — Why noisy estimates shrink more
It helps to write $s_j^2=\sigma^2/n_j$, the sampling variance of the group mean. Then the data weight is $w_j=\tau^2/(\tau^2+s_j^2)$ and the population weight is $1-w_j=s_j^2/(\tau^2+s_j^2)$. If the group mean is accurate because $n_j$ is large, $s_j^2$ is small and $w_j$ is near one. If the true group means are similar, $\tau^2$ is small and the common mean gets more weight. Shrinkage is therefore a comparison between two kinds of variation: uncertainty *within* a group and genuine differences *between* groups. The posterior variance $s_j^2\tau^2/(s_j^2+\tau^2)$ is smaller than either input variance when both are positive, because the posterior combines two sources of information.

## Key results
The conditional mean can also be written $\mu+w_j(\bar y_j-\mu)$: the observed departure from the population mean is retained in the fraction $w_j$. For fixed positive $\sigma^2$ and $\tau^2$, increasing $n_j$ raises $w_j$ and reduces posterior variance. As $n_j\to\infty$, the conditional mean approaches $\bar y_j$; as $\tau^2\downarrow0$, it approaches $\mu$. These are limits of the stated formula, Neither extreme is automatically appropriate for real data. Integrating over uncertain $\mu$ and $\tau^2$ changes both the posterior mean and its uncertainty, so the plug-in weighted average is only the conditional result.

## Proof
At fixed $\mu,\sigma^2,\tau^2$, the factors involving $\theta_j$ are proportional to
$$
\exp\!\left[-\frac12\left\{\frac{n_j}{\sigma^2}(\theta_j-\bar y_j)^2+\frac1{\tau^2}(\theta_j-\mu)^2\right\}\right].
$$
Expanding the squares gives coefficient $A_j=n_j/\sigma^2+1/\tau^2$ on $\theta_j^2$ and linear coefficient $-2B_j$ with $B_j=n_j\bar y_j/\sigma^2+\mu/\tau^2$. Completing the square gives $A_j(\theta_j-B_j/A_j)^2$ plus a term free of $\theta_j$, so the posterior is normal with mean $B_j/A_j$ and variance $1/A_j$. Multiplying both parts of $B_j/A_j$ by $\sigma^2\tau^2$ yields the displayed convex combination with $w_j=n_j\tau^2/(\sigma^2+n_j\tau^2)$. Likewise $1/A_j=\sigma^2\tau^2/(\sigma^2+n_j\tau^2)$. Since $dw_j/dn_j=\sigma^2\tau^2/(\sigma^2+n_j\tau^2)^2>0$, a larger group receives more data weight. Writing $s_j^2=\sigma^2/n_j$ proves the equivalent formulas and the limits stated above.

## Generalizations
If each group has a known but different observation variance $\sigma_j^2$, replace $\sigma^2/n_j$ by that group's sampling variance $s_j^2$. This is especially useful in meta-analysis, where groups can have different precision for reasons beyond sample size. When the population parameters are unknown, the conditional update remains a computational building block, but the marginal posterior is $p(\theta_j\mid y)=\int p(\theta_j\mid\mu,\tau^2,y_j)p(\mu,\tau^2\mid y)\,d\mu\,d\tau^2$. The hyperposterior uses data from every group. Its uncertainty can be important when there are only a few groups, because one estimate of $\tau$ may conceal several plausible amounts of pooling.

### When it fails
If one tutorial class uses a different exam, its mean may differ systematically and be poorly represented by the common normal population. Pulling it toward $\mu$ can hide that structure. If a group contains a single extreme outlier under a heavy-tailed score distribution, a normal likelihood with known $\sigma^2$ can give that outlier too much influence. If $\tau^2$ is treated as exactly zero, all group means are forced equal; if it is fixed at an implausibly large value, borrowing across classes nearly disappears. A class with a known change in teaching method may need its own predictor or population level rather than exchangeability with the rest. These are model failures, not algebraic exceptions to the conditional formula.

## Related concepts
The [normal–normal conjugacy](normal-normal-conjugacy.qmd) result is applied once per group after conditioning on $\mu$ and $\tau^2$. [Complete pooling, no pooling and partial pooling](complete-pooling-no-pooling-and-partial-pooling.qmd) describe the practical range of group estimates: one shared mean, separate raw means, and weighted borrowing. [Empirical Bayes](empirical-bayes.qmd) estimates hyperparameters and plugs them into the shrinkage rule, whereas a full hierarchical posterior averages over their uncertainty. [Ridge regression](ridge-regression.qmd) also pulls estimates toward a reference value through a quadratic normal-prior penalty, but the hierarchy's shared target and pooling strength may be learned from related groups.

## Statistical use
Rat-tumour studies compare raw rates with posterior medians and show the general partial-pooling effect: small studies with extreme raw rates can move far toward a population centre, while large studies move less. Those particular observations are binomial counts with a beta hierarchy, Those studies use a beta hierarchy, so they do not follow the normal formula. For normal outcomes, the formula makes the same precision logic exact when the variances and hyperparameters are fixed. The large and small class example below shows this directly, using a shared score mean of $70$ and markedly different class sizes.

## Common misconceptions
Shrinkage is not a uniform subtraction from every group mean: each weight depends on that group's precision. The weighted average is not the full posterior mean when $\mu$ and $\tau^2$ are uncertain; it is the mean conditional on their values. A smaller conditional posterior variance does not prove the model is well calibrated if the normal or exchangeability assumptions fail. Partial pooling does not say the group means are identical; the prior variance $\tau^2$ explicitly allows differences. Nor does moving an extreme sample mean toward $\mu$ prove that the raw observation was an error: an extreme true group effect remains possible.

## History
Lindley and Smith's 1972 paper *Bayes Estimates for the Linear Model* developed Bayesian estimation with hierarchical prior structure, an early source for pooling related coefficients.{{cite:lancaster2004_bayesian_econometrics}} (Ch. 1, §1.8, pp. 68–69) Efron and Morris's early-1970s papers, including their 1975 *Data Analysis Using Stein's Estimator and Its Generalizations*, helped develop empirical Bayes shrinkage into a data-analysis method.{{cite:casella1985_empirical_bayes}} (§1, p. 83, and references, p. 87)

A normal hierarchical posterior mean and a James–Stein estimator are related through shrinkage, but they are different constructions with different assumptions and risk claims. The conditional normal calculation here follows directly from multiplying the group likelihood by its normal population distribution.

## Worked example
Suppose related laboratories have population mean $\mu=50$, within-laboratory standard deviation $\sigma=12$, and between-laboratory standard deviation $\tau=6$. Two laboratories each report sample mean $62$, but one has $n_1=4$ measurements and the other $n_2=36$. For the first, $w_1=4(36)/(144+4(36))=1/2$, so the conditional posterior mean is $50+(1/2)(62-50)=56$ and variance is $144(36)/(144+4(36))=18$. For the second, $w_2=36(36)/(144+36(36))=0.9$, giving mean $50+0.9(12)=60.8$ and variance $144(36)/(144+36(36))=3.6$. The two observed averages match, yet the better-measured laboratory stays closer to its own data. These are conditional estimates: they do not include uncertainty in the population parameters.

**Your turn.** Keep $\mu=100$, $\sigma=20$ and $\tau=10$ fixed. Two groups each report $\bar y=130$; one has $n=4$ and the other $n=16$. Calculate their data weights, conditional posterior means and conditional posterior variances.

Here $\sigma^2=400$ and $\tau^2=100$. For $n=4$, $w=4(100)/(400+4(100))=1/2$, so the posterior mean is $100+(1/2)(30)=115$ and variance is $400(100)/(400+4(100))=50$. For $n=16$, $w=16(100)/(400+16(100))=0.8$, so the posterior mean is $100+0.8(30)=124$ and variance is $400(100)/(400+16(100))=20$. The larger group's data get more weight, so its posterior mean remains closer to $130$.
