## Lead context

A process engineer who changes one temperature and watches a yield is already running an experiment, whether or not a statistician is in the room. Engineering statistics is the use of experimental design, process comparison, and reliability methods to make those decisions when the units are products, materials, or manufacturing steps. The designs are chosen before the runs, because the runs are expensive and the question is which effects the data will be able to separate.{{cite:nist_engineer_design2002}} (§5.3)

## Intuition

Each run has a cost, and a factor that is not changed cannot show an effect. A design is a plan for which combinations will be run so that the comparisons the engineer named are estimable from those runs. A full factorial runs every combination of the chosen levels, so a main effect is not tied up with an interaction. A fractional factorial runs a subset and accepts that some effects share a column. A response-surface design spends runs on squares and products because the yield may bend. The sequence is the same in each case: state the objective, choose the process variables and their levels, then choose the design that can estimate the effects the objective cares about. Looking at the yield first and inventing the design afterwards answers a different question from the one the runs were built to answer.{{cite:nist_engineer_design2002}} (§5.3; §5.3.3.4; §5.3.3.6)

## Definition

The designs an engineer is expected to know are the designs used across engineering and scientific work. They include completely randomised designs, randomised block designs, Latin squares and their Graeco-Latin extensions, full factorials, fractional factorials, Plackett–Burman and other screening designs, and response-surface designs for second-order surfaces, including central composite and Box–Behnken layouts. Blocking is part of the same list: a known nuisance, such as a batch of raw material or a day, is removed from the comparison of the factors by being balanced into the plan. Centre points can be added to a two-level factorial so that curvature has a degree of freedom of its own.{{cite:nist_engineer_design2002}} (§5.3)

Alongside design sits the comparison of processes that are already running. Tests and confidence intervals are used to decide whether a mean has shifted, whether two processes differ, and whether a trend or an outlier is present. The scope of that comparison is hypothesis tests, confidence intervals, the relation between the two, outlier detection, and the detection of a sequential trend. Reliability methods treat lifetime data, including units that have not failed by the end of the test. The common setting is a measurement that will be used to change a process, a specification, or a test plan.{{cite:nist_process_intro2002}} (§7.1){{cite:nist_censoring2002}} (§8.1.3.1)

## Statistical use

A [designed experiment](designed-experiment.qmd) assigns the factor combinations. In a full factorial every combination appears, so main effects and interactions can be separated. The number of runs grows as a power of the number of levels. Six factors at two levels already specify $2^6=64$ factorial runs, before any centre points. A fractional factorial keeps a fraction such as one half or one quarter of those runs. A half fraction of the six-factor plan has $64/2=32$ runs. The fraction is chosen so that the two-level design stays balanced and orthogonal, and so that the effects the engineer is willing to alias are the ones that share a column. Screening designs push the same idea further when many factors might matter and only a few runs are affordable.{{cite:nist_engineer_design2002}} (§5.3.3.4)

Response-surface designs estimate a curved surface in a few continuous factors so that a yield can be moved toward a maximum. A model with only main effects and interactions is enough when the response at the centre matches the average of the factorial corners, which is the check that pure quadratic curvature is absent. When the surface bends, a quadratic in three factors adds the three squares and the three products to the intercept and the three linear terms. The same handbook writes the cubic terms as well and notes that a quadratic is almost always the industrial stopping point: the full cubic is rarely needed. Central composite and Box–Behnken layouts are the designs that estimate that quadratic. [Least squares](least-squares.qmd) fits the mean model, and an [analysis of variance](anova-sum-of-squares-decomposition.qmd) splits the variation into factors, blocks, and residual. [Hypothesis tests](ingredients-of-a-hypothesis-test.qmd) and [confidence intervals](confidence-interval.qmd) then say whether a change is large relative to the run-to-run noise.{{cite:nist_engineer_design2002}} (§5.3.3.6){{cite:nist_process_intro2002}} (§7.1)

For lifetimes, a fixed test length produces Type I [censoring](censoring-statistics.qmd). Put $n$ non-repairable units on test for a prechosen time $T$. The failure times that fall before $T$ are observed, say $r$ of them, and the other $n-r$ units contribute the information that their lifetimes exceed $T$. Here $T$ is fixed and $r$ is random. If 20 units are run and 7 fail before the cutoff, 13 are right-censored at $T$. Type II censoring reverses the randomness: $r$ is chosen in advance and the test stops at the $r$th failure, so $T$ is the random time. A [Weibull](weibull-distribution.qmd) or lognormal model, or a distribution-free [survival](survival-function.qmd) curve, turns those records into a failure probability. The survivors are not dropped. Dropping them would treat a unit that outlasted the test as if it had never been put on test.{{cite:nist_censoring2002}} (§8.1.3.1)

## Assumptions and limits

A fractional factorial estimates a main effect only together with the interactions that were aliased with it. If one of those interactions is large, the column does not belong to the main effect alone. The design states the aliasing when the fraction is chosen. It does not discover the aliasing after the runs. A response-surface quadratic is a local model inside the region where the factors were actually set. Moving a temperature past that region is an extrapolation of the fitted surface, and the design did not collect the runs that would check it. Centre points detect curvature at the middle of the studied box. They do not map a ridge outside the box.{{cite:nist_engineer_design2002}} (§5.3.3.4; §5.3.3.6)

Process comparisons assume the data are the process the decision will change. A test that a mean has shifted is a statement about the runs in hand and about the sampling model those runs are treated as following. An outlier screen and a trend screen answer different questions from a comparison of two factor means, and a significant factor test does not by itself say that the new setting should be adopted. The cost of a change, the specification limits, and the confirmation runs sit outside the $p$-value.{{cite:nist_process_intro2002}} (§7.1)

Reliability calculations assume the censoring mechanism is the one in the likelihood. Type I censoring, with $T$ fixed, is a different likelihood from Type II, with $r$ fixed. Treating the censored units as failures at $T$, or as if they had failed at some imputed time without a model, changes the failure rate the test was run to estimate. The exact failure times are part of the Type I record the handbook describes. A test that only stores a pass or a fail at time $T$ has a coarser likelihood than the one that stores $t_1,\ldots,t_r$.{{cite:nist_censoring2002}} (§8.1.3.1)

## History

Several standard methods were built on engineering and industrial data, and on the agricultural experiments that the same designs grew out of. R. A. Fisher's designs, stimulated by agricultural field trials, supplied the factorial and blocking language that process engineers still use. John Tukey's numerical spectrum analysis grew out of physical-science and engineering series. George Box's response-surface methods grew out of chemical-process experiments, where the goal was to move a set of controllable factors toward a better yield. Those three lines are the historical argument for keeping method close to the data it was invented on: the designs, the spectrum tools, and the response surfaces each started as an answer to a subject-matter series rather than as a free-standing abstraction. The NIST/SEMATECH handbook collects the designs, the process comparisons, and the censoring rules in the form a working engineer consults.{{cite:cleveland2001}} (p. 22){{cite:nist_engineer_design2002}} (§5.3){{cite:nist_censoring2002}} (§8.1.3.1)
