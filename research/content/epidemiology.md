## Lead context

A city can record many deaths from one illness while a neighbouring city records few, and the difference can be the water, the age mix, or the way the deaths were counted. Epidemiology studies the frequency and distribution of illness, and the parameters and risk factors that define the state of health of a population, in relation to time, place, and groups of people. The name comes from the Greek words for what is upon the people and for study. The aim of the causal side of the subject is to identify how a cause is related to an effect on human health, so that the events can be prevented.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 61, 181)

## Definition

Epidemiology is the study of the frequency and distribution of illness and of the factors that define the health of a population across time, space, and groups. The population is part of the definition. It may be the residents of a country in a given year, or a narrower group such as workers in one plant. Age, sex, and ethnicity differ across those populations, so a comparison that ignores them mixes unlike groups. The events include disease and death, and the factors include exposures such as smoking, polluted water, and microbial agents. A cause, in this setting, is an agent that modifies health. An effect is the change in health associated with that agent.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 61–62, 181)

Distribution and causation are different contrasts, even when both are written as risks. In a population of 20 people of whom 13 were treated, an associational comparison uses the risk among those 13 and the risk among the other 7. Those risks can be $7/13$ and $3/7$. A causal comparison uses the risk that would have been seen if all 20 had been treated and the risk if all 20 had been untreated. Those two counterfactual risks can both be $10/20=0.5$, so the causal risk difference is 0 and the causal risk ratio is 1, while the treated and untreated groups in the actual world still differ. Association is a contrast between two disjoint subsets defined by the treatment people actually received. Causation is a contrast of the same population under two treatment values.{{cite:hernan_robins2020}} (§1.3, p. 7; §1.5, pp. 11–12)

## Properties

Crude rates mix composition with risk. Suppose two regions each have 10,000 inhabitants. Region A records 500 deaths and region B records 1,000, so the crude annual mortalities are $500/10000=0.05$ and $1000/10000=0.10$. Split at age 60 and the age-specific rates match: 5 percent under 60 and 15 percent over 60 in both regions. Region A has all of its population under 60. Region B has half under 60. The mixture $0.5\times 0.05+0.5\times 0.15=0.10$ recovers B's crude rate, and A's crude rate is the under-60 rate because everyone in A is under 60. The crude comparison says B is more dangerous. The age-specific comparison says the two regions have the same mortality at each age, and B has more older people. A recommendation to move from one region to the other does not follow from the crude rates.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 181–182)

Absolute and relative contrasts answer different public-health questions. The absolute effect is the risk in the exposed minus the risk in the unexposed. The relative effect is their ratio, reported as a relative risk or an [odds ratio](odds-ratio.qmd). In a smoking comparison with 1,000 people in each group, 50 smokers and 10 nonsmokers develop lung cancer: the risks are $0.05$ and $0.01$, the ratio is $5$, and the absolute difference is $0.04$. The same proportions in groups of 100 are 5 and 1. Dodge reports a chi-square $p$-value below $0.001$ for the large study and a $p$-value of $0.212$ for the small one, above the $0.05$ threshold used there, so the small study does not establish the association the large study shows. The ratio did not change. The sample did.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 61–62)

A ratio and a difference can also describe one causal contrast on two scales. If 3 in a million people would develop an outcome if treated and 1 in a million if untreated, the causal risk ratio is $3$ and the causal risk difference is $3/10^6-1/10^6=0.000002$. The ratio says how many times the risk multiplies. The difference says how many extra cases the treatment adds. Which one belongs in a decision depends on the decision. A rare outcome can have a large ratio and a negligible count of cases. The causal null is the same on both scales: a risk difference of 0 and a risk ratio of 1.{{cite:hernan_robins2020}} (§1.3, p. 7)

Study design decides what the comparison can support. An [observational study](observational-study.qmd) records the treatment people actually received. A randomised experiment is an experiment because the investigator carries out the action, and it is randomised because a random device, not a prognostic rule, assigns that action. A nonrandom rule, such as treating men and not women, compares groups that critics can argue were already different. A [confounding variable](confounding-variable.qmd) is one name for the discrepancy in which the treated are sicker, or otherwise different, before the treatment. Holding that variable fixed, or breaking the link by random assignment, separates the associational contrast from the causal one. Large samples matter because, as in the smoking tables, the same proportions can be decisive at one sample size and inconclusive at another.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 61–62){{cite:hernan_robins2020}} (§1.5, pp. 11–12; §2.1, p. 13)

## History

The seventeenth-century London bills of mortality gave John Graunt a list of deaths by cause, and William Petty used population figures in political arithmetic. Adolphe Quetelet's population statistics later sat underneath epidemiology as well as under statistics and econometrics. With William Farr, epidemiology was recognised as separate from statistics at large, because he studied causes of death and how death varied with age, sex, season, residence, and occupation. James Lind's demonstration that citrus fruit stopped scurvy, and John Snow's comparison of cholera across London water companies, tied the same questions to prevention. Snow's study of households supplied by polluted Thames water pointed to the water before the organism was known.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 181)

The formal split between a causal contrast and an associational contrast is what makes those historical comparisons readable as designs rather than as lists. A difference in death rates between two water companies is an association until the argument says why the populations would have had the same risk under a common water supply. Random assignment is one such argument. It is not available for a water company chosen in the nineteenth century, which is why that comparison lives on as an observational design with a stated reason for comparability, not as a randomised trial.{{cite:hernan_robins2020}} (§2.1, p. 13){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 181)

## Connections

[Biostatistics](biostatistics.qmd) is the methodological neighbour: regression, survival models, and incomplete data are used here because the questions are biomedical. [Demography](demography.qmd) shares the life table, the [census](census.qmd), and the study of births and deaths, and asks them of whole populations rather than of a particular illness. [Public health](public-health.qmd) is where a rate becomes a programme. Clinical work carries the same designs into the care of individual patients. In each case the statistical object is a rate in a defined group, and the scientific object is what would change that rate. A [randomised controlled trial](randomised-controlled-trial.qmd) is the experiment in which assignment is by a random mechanism. A [survival function](survival-function.qmd) is the tool when the outcome is a time rather than a yes-or-no event at a fixed date. The absolute effect and the relative effect remain the two scales on which a change in that rate is reported.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 62, 181){{cite:hernan_robins2020}} (§1.3, p. 7)
