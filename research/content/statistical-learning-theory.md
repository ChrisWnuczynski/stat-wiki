## Lead context
Statistical learning theory studies how well a rule fitted on one sample will predict a new case, and it ties that future error to the richness of the class of rules the fit was allowed to search. Training error, the mistakes on the sample already seen, is the wrong number to quote once the class is rich enough to fit noise.{{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (pp. 237–239, §7.9){{cite:james_islr2013}} (pp. 175–176, Ch. 5, training error against test error)

## Definition — Scope
A prediction rule is chosen from a class of functions by looking at a training sample. The quantity that matters for a new draw from the same joint distribution is the expected prediction error, the expected loss of the prediction. For a quantitative response and squared error, that expectation is minimised by the conditional mean of the response given the predictors. For classification with zero–one loss, it is the expected rate of misclassification. The rule that attains the minimum in the population is not the same object as the rule fitted on a finite sample.{{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (pp. 18–20, §2.4, expected prediction error)

The gap between training error and this future error is the optimism of the fit. It grows as the class of functions becomes more flexible, and it shrinks as the training sample grows. Cross-validation estimates the future error by scoring observations that were held out of the fit. Capacity measures try to bound the same gap from the geometry of the class, without a second sample.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (pp. 175–176, Ch. 5){{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (p. 237, §7.9, optimism and complexity)

The Vapnik–Chervonenkis dimension is one such measure. For a class of indicator functions, it is the largest number of points that the class can shatter: the points can be labelled in every possible way and some member of the class still separates them perfectly. Lines in the plane shatter three points and not four, so their VC dimension is $3$. A linear indicator in $p$ dimensions has VC dimension $p+1$. The one-parameter family of indicators $I(\sin(\alpha x)>0)$ has infinite VC dimension, which is why counting parameters is not a substitute for the definition. For a real-valued class, the dimension is the VC dimension of the indicators obtained by thresholding the functions.{{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (pp. 237–238, §7.9)

## Statistical use — From a capacity bound to a choice of class
If a class of VC dimension $h$ is fitted to $N$ training points, bounds of VC type say that the future error sits below the training error plus a penalty that grows with $h$ and falls with $N$, and that the inequality holds simultaneously for every function in the class, on a set of training samples of probability at least $1-\eta$. One regression form of the bound is $\mathrm{Err}_T\le \mathrm{err}/(1-c\sqrt{\varepsilon})_+$, where $\varepsilon$ is of order $(h\log(N/h)-\log\eta)/N$. The constants in front of the logarithm are not unique. The bounds are often loose as numerical guarantees. They are still used to compare classes, because only the relative size of the bound is needed to pick a class. Structural risk minimisation fits a nested sequence of classes with increasing VC dimension and chooses the class with the smallest upper bound.{{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (p. 239, §7.9, the bounds and structural risk minimisation)

In ordinary practice the same comparison is made with a resampling estimate instead of a VC bound. [Cross-validation](cross-validation.qmd) estimates test error by refitting on complements of held-out blocks. The [bootstrap](bootstrap.qmd) is used both for the variability of a fitted rule and, in variants built for the purpose, for prediction error. [Overfitting](overfitting.qmd) is the observable symptom the theory is about: training error falls while future error rises. [Loss and risk](loss-and-risk.qmd) supply the scale on which that future error is an expectation, and [zero–one loss](zero-one-loss-and-risk.qmd) is the classification case of the same scale.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (p. 175, Ch. 5){{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (pp. 18–20, §2.4; p. 239, §7.9)

The theory does not remove the need to say which loss is being bounded. A bound on misclassification rate is not a bound on squared error. And a class with a small VC dimension can still be the wrong class for the problem. Linear indicators in the plane have VC dimension $3$, and a mean restricted to a straight line still cannot represent a bend.{{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (pp. 237–239, §7.9)

## History
The expected-prediction-error account of regression and classification is the decision-theoretic setting in which modern prediction methods are compared. VC theory was developed to give a complexity measure that applies to classes, such as a sine wave of free frequency, where the number of free parameters is a misleading count, and to turn that measure into bounds on the optimism of training error. The dimension and the structural risk minimisation programme are set out by Vapnik; a standard exposition is Vapnik (1996). The bounds quoted in survey form are often taken from later numerical recommendations, and they are offered as model-selection devices rather than as tight numerical certificates.{{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (pp. 237–239, §7.9; p. 257, bibliographic note on Vapnik, 1996)

## Connections
[Parametric statistics](parametric-statistics.qmd) is the special case in which the class is indexed by a short parameter and the optimism is often summarised by a parameter count. VC dimension agrees with that count for a linear indicator and disagrees for $\sin(\alpha x)$. [Regularisation](regularisation.qmd) and [ridge regression](ridge-regression.qmd) restrict the class, or penalise its members, so that the fitted rule cannot use the whole flexibility of an unrestricted fit. [Resampling](resampling-statistics.qmd) is the practical estimator of the error that the VC bound only upper-limits. The difficulty shared by all of these devices is that a future error for one particular training sample is hard to know from that sample alone.{{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (pp. 237–239 and 257, §7.9 and the bibliographic notes){{cite:james_islr2013}} (pp. 21–22, §2.1.2, flexibility and overfitting; pp. 175–176, Ch. 5)
