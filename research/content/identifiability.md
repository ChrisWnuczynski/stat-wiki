## Motivating example
A mixture of two normal groups can fit the same data after the groups' names are swapped. The fitted density has not changed, but the numerical vector labelled “component one” has. This is an identification problem: the observations cannot tell those labels apart. Similar ambiguity occurs when an intercept and all factor-level effects are included without a constraint. Identifiability matters because a fitting algorithm's chosen parameter vector can look definite even when several vectors describe exactly the same observable law.{{cite:stephens2000}} (§§1–2, pp. 795–798){{cite:nist_two_way_anova2002}} (§7.4.3.7, factorial model)

## Intuition
Imagine being given unlimited data from a fixed statistical model. If two parameter values still imply the same distribution for every possible observation, no amount of sampling can distinguish them. Identifiability asks whether that ambiguity exists at the level of the *model*, before considering estimation noise. A parameter may be identifiable yet estimated imprecisely from a small sample; those are different issues. Conversely, an optimization program may return a very precise-looking coordinate on a flat likelihood ridge without making that coordinate scientifically determined.{{cite:rothenberg1971}} (§§1–2, pp. 577–579)

## Definition
For a family $\{P_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\}$, the parameterization is globally identifiable if $P_\theta=P_{\theta'}$ implies $\theta=\theta'$. Two different values that yield the same observable law are *observationally equivalent*. Local identifiability at $\theta$ asks only that a sufficiently small neighborhood contain no other equivalent parameter value. Global identification is stronger: two isolated points far apart can remain equivalent even when each is locally identifiable. One can instead identify a function $g(\theta)$ if it has the same value at every observationally equivalent parameter point.{{cite:rothenberg1971}} (§2, definitions of observational equivalence, identification and local identification, pp. 578–579)

## Interpretation — What the data can determine
In a two-component normal mixture with fixed unit component variances, write $f(x)=\pi\varphi(x-\mu_1)+(1-\pi)\varphi(x-\mu_2)$. Replacing $(\pi,\mu_1,\mu_2)$ by $(1-\pi,\mu_2,\mu_1)$ leaves $f$ unchanged for every $x$. The *unlabelled* two-component density can be identified under suitable distinct-component and positive-weight conditions, while the arbitrary component names cannot. If the means coincide or a weight is zero, further ambiguities arise: a redundant component contributes no distinguishable feature. Ordering means can choose one representative in a simple one-dimensional fit, but near-equal means make that convention unstable and it does not solve every mixture-inference problem.{{cite:stephens2000}} (§§1–3, pp. 795–801){{cite:green2019_finite_mixtures}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.1, pp. 13–14, finite-mixture identification)

Linear models show a different kind of redundancy. If a model has an intercept $\mu$ and a coefficient for every level of a factor, the fitted mean at level $j$ is $\mu+\alpha_j$. Adding a constant to $\mu$ and subtracting it from each $\alpha_j$ changes no fitted mean. Fixing a reference effect to zero or imposing a sum-to-zero constraint gives a coordinate system for the same fitted values. In a two-factor ANOVA, analogous constraints are needed for the intercept, main effects and interactions; the scientifically interesting contrasts can remain estimable even before a particular coding is chosen.{{cite:nist_two_way_anova2002}} (§7.4.3.7, two-way factorial model)

## Properties — Global, local and information criteria
An information matrix can diagnose a local flat direction under regularity assumptions, but it is not a general proof of global identifiability. Rothenberg's local criterion connects nonsingular information to local identification at regular parameter points. A mixture with label switching can have separate, well-curved likelihood peaks that are equivalent globally. A model with only the sum $\theta_1+\theta_2$ in its mean has a continuous ridge and a singular information matrix. The two failures require different responses: an arbitrary label convention for the first, and a scientifically meaningful restriction or a redefined estimand for the second.{{cite:rothenberg1971}} (§§2–4, pp. 578–583){{cite:stephens2000}} (§§1–3, pp. 795–801)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
For the normal-sum model $Y\sim N(\theta_1+\theta_2,1)$, writing $s=\theta_1+\theta_2$ shows that every pair with the same $s$ has the same law. The score for one observation is $(Y-s)(1,1)^{\mathsf T}$, so its information matrix is $I(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$, with eigenvalues $2$ and $0$. Thus the sum is identifiable but the individual coordinates are not. In the two-component mixture, commutativity of addition gives $\pi\varphi(x-\mu_1)+(1-\pi)\varphi(x-\mu_2)=(1-\pi)\varphi(x-\mu_2)+\pi\varphi(x-\mu_1)$ for every $x$, proving label equivalence. Finally, for $Y\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\theta^2)$ on $-1<\theta<1$, $\theta$ and $-\theta$ have the same law. At $\theta=1/2$, the one-observation information for this parameterization is $\{2\theta\}^2/[\theta^2(1-\theta^2)]=16/3>0$: local information there does not remove the distinct equivalent point $-1/2$.{{cite:rothenberg1971}} (§§2–4, pp. 578–583, local versus global identification){{cite:stephens2000}} (§2, mixture symmetry)
:::

## Statistical use — Constraints and interpretable targets
Identification checks should begin by asking what feature of the observable distribution changes when a parameter changes. In regression, this often reduces to checking the rank of the design matrix after contrasts are specified. In mixture analysis, one may report permutation-invariant quantities, such as the overall mixture density, rather than attach scientific meaning to arbitrary component numbers. In a generalized additive model, an ordinary smooth can absorb an arbitrary constant from the intercept; `mgcv` usually centres the smooth so its values sum to zero over observations. The constraint makes the displayed intercept and smooth separately interpretable in that chosen convention, without changing the combined fitted predictor.{{cite:stephens2000}} (§§2–4, pp. 796–803)

An identification constraint is a bookkeeping choice only when it selects among parameter vectors with identical observable predictions. It cannot repair a model that lacks information about a scientific feature. For example, observing only the sum of two unknown normal means cannot reveal each mean: declaring $\theta_2=0$ would turn an assumption into an apparent estimate. The distinction matters when reporting coefficients and uncertainty, especially near mixture components that merge or regression columns that are nearly redundant.{{cite:rothenberg1971}} (§§2–4, pp. 578–583)

## Worked example
Take a two-component unit-variance normal mixture with weight $\pi=0.3$ and means $(\mu_1,\mu_2)=(0,2)$. Its density at $x=0$ is $0.3\varphi(0)+0.7\varphi(-2)\approx0.157476$, while at $x=2$ it is $0.3\varphi(2)+0.7\varphi(0)\approx0.295457$. Swap the labels to $(\pi',\mu_1',\mu_2')=(0.7,2,0)$. The two computed densities, and every other density value, stay exactly the same because the summands merely switch places. An optimizer may return either vector. The weight and mean for a *named* first component are therefore not identified by the observations, even though this example's overall density is well defined.{{cite:stephens2000}} (§2, mixture model and label symmetry)

**Your turn.** A two-level factor model has means $\mu+\alpha_1$ and $\mu+\alpha_2$. Starting from $(\mu,\alpha_1,\alpha_2)=(10,-2,3)$, find the two group means. Give a different parameter triple with the same means, then impose $\alpha_1=0$ and find that reference-coded triple.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The group means are $10-2=8$ and $10+3=13$. Adding $5$ to the intercept and subtracting $5$ from each effect gives $(15,-7,-2)$, again yielding $(8,13)$. With the first effect fixed at zero, the same means use $(\mu,\alpha_1,\alpha_2)=(8,0,5)$. The contrast $13-8=5$ is unchanged across these parameterizations.
:::

## Common misconceptions
Identifiable does not mean accurately estimated from any finite dataset. A unique numerical optimizer output does not prove identification when an algorithm selects one point on a ridge or one mixture labelling. Local identifiability does not imply global identifiability: $\theta$ and $-\theta$ in the Bernoulli-square example are separated but equivalent. Finally, a reference-level or centring constraint fixes how coefficients are named; it does not create empirical evidence for an otherwise unobserved component.{{cite:rothenberg1971}} (§§2–4, pp. 578–583){{cite:stephens2000}} (§§1–3, pp. 795–801)

## Assumptions and limits — Where simple fixes fail
If mixture components have the same mean and variance, swapping labels is only part of the problem: splitting one component into two identical pieces can produce more equivalent parameter vectors. If a mixture weight is zero, its unused component's mean is unconstrained by the density. If two regression predictors are identical columns, merely increasing the sample size with the same design does not separate their coefficients. Ordering mixture means may choose labels in a one-dimensional well-separated fit, but Stephens shows that artificial identifiability constraints need not give satisfactory posterior summaries in general. These examples distinguish discrete symmetry, redundant components and continuous design-rank failure.{{cite:stephens2000}} (§§2–3, pp. 796–801){{cite:rothenberg1971}} (§§2–4, pp. 578–583)

## History
Rothenberg's 1971 treatment formulated identification through observational equivalence and distinguished local from global criteria for parametric models. Teicher's 1963 work established finite-mixture identification results that concern the mixing distribution, while the observable symmetry of component labels remained a practical issue in mixture fitting. Stephens's 2000 analysis made that label-switching issue explicit for Bayesian posterior summaries and showed why a simple ordering constraint is not universally satisfactory.{{cite:rothenberg1971}} (§§1–4, pp. 577–583){{cite:green2019_finite_mixtures}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.1, pp. 13–14, Teicher and mixing-distribution identification){{cite:stephens2000}} (§§1–3, pp. 795–801)
