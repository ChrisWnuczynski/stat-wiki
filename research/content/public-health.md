## Lead context
Public health, in the statistical sense used here, is the study of how illness and its exposures are distributed in populations, and of the rates used to describe them. The unit is a population or a subgroup, not a single patient. A rate compares a count of events with the people and the time in which those events could have occurred. A comparison of rates asks whether an exposure tracks the illness. The comparison is not yet a cause. Public-health statistics is the discipline of computing the rates honestly and of saying what a comparison of them is entitled to mean.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 181)

## Intuition
Imagine two regions, each with \(10\,000\) inhabitants. In a year, region A records \(500\) deaths and region B records \(1\,000\). The crude mortality is \(5\%\) in A and \(10\%\) in B. A recommendation that people leave B for A would treat the crude rates as the risk of living in each region. Split the same deaths by age and the picture changes. Among people under \(60\), both regions have annual mortality \(5\%\). Among people over \(60\), both have \(15\%\). Region B has only half its population under \(60\), and region A has all of its population under \(60\). The crude rate in B is higher because B is older, not because an inhabitant of a given age dies faster in B. The intuition public-health statistics trains is to ask which population the rate is about, and which other factor is mixed into the comparison.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 182)

The same instinct applies to an exposure. A higher rate among the exposed can mean the exposure harms people. It can also mean that the exposed differ in age, in a second exposure, or in the way the illness was counted. The rate is a fact about the records once the denominator is fixed. The reason for the rate is a further question.

## Definition
Epidemiology, which supplies the statistical language of public health, is the study of the frequency and distribution of illness and of the factors associated with it. Dodge traces the word to the Greek for the study of epidemics. Risk, in that setting, is the probability that a disease appears in a population, or in a homogeneous subgroup, during a given time interval. Incidence is the frequency of the disease. Risk is the probability. The two are related and they are not synonyms: a frequency in a finite record estimates a probability and is not itself the probability.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 181, 461)

A cause, in the epidemiological sense Dodge records, is an agent that modifies health: a microbe, polluted water, smoking. The effect is the way health changes. The comparison is a risk comparison. The absolute effect is the risk in the exposed minus the risk in the unexposed, the excess risk associated with the exposure. The relative effect is the ratio of those two risks, the strength of the association, reported as a relative risk or through an odds ratio. A relative risk of \(2\) says the exposed have twice the risk of the unexposed. It does not say how many extra cases that is. The absolute effect says how many. Both numbers need a denominator that matches the people and the time being described.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 62)

Public health uses these quantities for populations: a city, an age band, an occupational group. A clinical trial uses some of the same arithmetic on assigned treatments. The population rate and the trial contrast answer different operational questions. One describes who is ill and where. The other asks what a treatment changes. Mixing them produces a sentence that neither design supports.

## Statistical use
A public-health analysis starts by fixing the event, the population, and the time. Deaths in a year, new cases of a disease, or an attack of cholera are different events. The population is the set of people who could have contributed the event. Leaving the elderly out of a mortality comparison, or leaving people who have already had the disease out of an incidence comparison, changes the rate. Age, sex, and ethnicity are the factors Dodge lists as the ones an epidemiological study has to consider, because they change both the risk and the composition of the population. The two-region table is the usual case: the crude rates differ, and the age-specific rates do not.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 182)

The exposure comparison then chooses an absolute effect or a relative effect, and says which. A relative risk near \(1\) is a weak association. A relative risk of \(2\) on a rare disease can be a small absolute effect. Policy that counts cases needs the absolute effect. Aetiology that compares the strength of two exposures often uses the relative effect. Reporting one and deciding with the other is a change of question.

Observational public-health studies are the usual source of these rates, because exposures such as water supply or occupation are not assigned. OpenIntro's rule for observational data is that causal conclusions are treacherous. Sun exposure confounds sunscreen use and skin cancer: the people who use more sunscreen can have more cancer because they spend more time in the sun, even when sunscreen itself is protective. A public-health association has the same structure. It supports a hypothesis and a description of the population. It supports a causal sentence only with the further conditions the design can actually check.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.4){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 62)

Experiments remain available when a treatment can be assigned. Lind's citrus trial for scurvy and the modern randomised trial are that design. The rate in the treated group and the rate in the control group are still rates. The assignment is what makes their difference easier to read as an effect of the treatment rather than as a difference in who received it.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 181)

## Assumptions and limits
The strength of an association is not enough to establish a causal relationship. Dodge lists the temporality criterion first: the supposed cause has to precede the effect. A rate that is higher among people who are already ill does not, by that elevation, show that the illness caused the exposure, or the reverse. Other conditions sit beside temporality. They are design conditions, not decorations on a significant ratio.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 62)

The denominator has to be the population at risk. A mortality rate that uses all residents, including people who could not have had the disease, estimates a different probability from a rate that uses only the susceptible. A rate that ignores age, in the two-region example, estimates the risk of a randomly drawn resident and does not estimate the risk of a resident of a given age. Standardisation and age-specific rates are the repairs. They answer the age-specific question. They do not answer a question about a factor that was never recorded.

A risk is a probability in a model of the population over an interval. A small sample of a rare event estimates it badly. Dodge notes that a significant difference between populations in epidemiological studies usually requires large samples. A precise rate for a common event and a noisy rate for a rare event should not be compared as if the noise were the same.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 62)

Observational confounding is the standing limit. Exhausting the list of confounders is one route OpenIntro names toward a causal reading, and there is no guarantee the list is complete. An unmeasured exposure can still sit under a clean relative risk. The published number remains a description of the measured association.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.4)

## History
Dodge places the origin of epidemiology in the seventeenth century. Petty collected population information and described it in *Political Arithmetic*. Graunt, in 1662, gave an early rigorous analysis of causes of death. Quetelet is described as a founder of modern population statistics, the parent of epidemiology and of other quantitative descriptions of human life. Farr studied how death varied with age, sex, season, place, and profession, and on Dodge's account that work is what separated epidemiology from statistics as a general discipline. Lind showed that citrus fruit stopped scurvy. Snow showed, by a study on the spot, that cholera in London tracked drinking water polluted by sewage. Cornfield, in 1951, stated the notion of risk clearly in a comparison of groups. The rates, the age split, and the refusal to treat a crude association as a cause are the parts of that history that current practice still uses.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 181, 461)
