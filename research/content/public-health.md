## Lead context

A city has a rise in cholera, a country has a rise in lung cancer, a screening programme finds more disease than anyone expected. Public health statistics asks how common the condition is, who bears it, and which exposures travel with it. The unit is a population, or a group within it, rather than a single patient's chart. The answers are rates, comparisons, and maps. They become causes only when the timing and the alternative explanations have been faced.

## Definition — Scope

Public health, in its statistical part, is the study of how illness and health are distributed in populations and of the exposures that travel with that distribution. Epidemiology is the core discipline: the study of the frequency and distribution of illness, and of the parameters and risk factors that describe the health of a population, in relation to time, place, and group. A cause, in that setting, is an agent that changes health, such as a microbe, polluted water, or smoking. The effect is the change in health, read as a comparison of risks.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 62, 181)

The scope includes surveillance, outbreak investigation, and the evaluation of programmes aimed at populations. It is wider than a clinical trial, which asks what a treatment does to the people who are assigned it, and narrower than every social survey. A census count of age is demography. It becomes public health when it is used to understand disease. Groups have to be large when the goal is to detect differences in rates that are not huge.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 62, 181)

## Statistical use

Risk is the probability that disease appears in a population, or in a homogeneous subgroup, during an interval of time. Incidence is the corresponding frequency. The absolute effect is the risk in the exposed minus the risk in the unexposed, the extra cases associated with the exposure. The relative effect is the ratio of those risks, reported as a relative risk or an [odds ratio](odds-ratio.qmd). Neither comparison is yet a cause. The exposure has to precede the disease, and a [confounding variable](confounding-variable.qmd) can produce the same numerical association. In an observational study of sunscreen and skin cancer, sun exposure raises both sunscreen use and cancer risk, so the crude association is not the effect of the cream.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 62, 461){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.4, p. 25)

Maps and [time series](time-series.qmd) are the other workhorses. John Snow's study of cholera and the Thames is the classic spatial comparison of who drank which water. Screening programmes need [sensitivity and specificity](sensitivity-and-specificity.qmd), and those are not the chance that a person who tests positive is ill. Trials of vaccines and other population interventions, when they are randomised, use the same reporting discipline as other [randomised controlled trials](randomised-controlled-trial.qmd).{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 181){{cite:consort2010}} (Introduction)

## History

John Graunt's 1662 analysis of causes of death is the usual start of a numerical epidemiology. William Petty's political arithmetic had already treated population figures as a subject of study. Adolphe Quetelet is credited with shaping the population statistics from which epidemiology, as well as other quantitative social sciences, later split. William Farr studied how death varied with age, sex, season, place, and occupation, and on that basis epidemiology was distinguished from statistics at large. James Lind's trial of citrus against scurvy and John Snow's demonstration that cholera followed contaminated water are the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century set pieces. Jerome Cornfield, in 1951, stated the notion of risk from a follow-up comparison of people with and without disease.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 181, 461)

## Connections

Public health statistics uses [medical statistics](medical-statistics.qmd) whenever the question becomes a trial or a diagnostic test, and it uses [official statistics](official-statistics.qmd) for the denominators: population, births, and deaths. [Spatial analysis](spatial-analysis.qmd) enters when risk is mapped. A rate without a denominator, a relative risk without a baseline risk, and a map that plots cases without plotting population are three ways to mislead with the same data. The absolute effect and the relative effect answer different policy questions, and a large ratio can still be a small number of extra cases.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 62){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.4)
