

## Lead context

A city has a rise in cholera, a country has a rise in lung cancer, a screening programme finds more disease than anyone expected. Public health statistics asks how common the condition is, who bears it, and which exposures travel with it. The unit is a population, or a group within it, rather than a single patient's chart. The answers are rates, comparisons, and maps. They become causes only when the timing and the alternative explanations have been faced.

## Definition

Public health, in its statistical part, is the study of how illness and health are distributed in populations and of the exposures that travel with that distribution. Epidemiology is the core discipline: the study of the frequency and distribution of illness, and of the parameters and risk factors that describe the health of a population, in relation to time, place, and group. A cause, in that setting, is an agent that changes health, such as a microbe, polluted water, or smoking. The effect is the change in health, read as a comparison of risks.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 62, 181)

The scope includes surveillance, outbreak investigation, and the evaluation of programmes aimed at populations. It is wider than a clinical trial, which asks what a treatment does to the people who are assigned it, and narrower than every social survey. A census count of age is demography. It becomes public health when it is used to understand disease. Groups have to be large when the goal is to detect differences in rates that are not huge.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 62, 181)

Public health statistics uses [medical statistics](medical-statistics.qmd) whenever the question becomes a trial or a diagnostic test, and it uses [official statistics](official-statistics.qmd) for the denominators: population, births, and deaths. [Spatial analysis](spatial-analysis.qmd) enters when risk is mapped. A rate without a denominator, a relative risk without a baseline risk, and a map that plots cases without plotting population are three ways to mislead with the same data. The absolute effect and the relative effect answer different policy questions, and a large ratio can still be a small number of extra cases.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 62){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.4)

## Statistical use

Risk is the probability that disease appears in a population, or in a homogeneous subgroup, during an interval of time. Incidence is the corresponding frequency. The absolute effect is the risk in the exposed minus the risk in the unexposed, the extra cases associated with the exposure. The relative effect is the ratio of those risks, reported as a relative risk or an [odds ratio](odds-ratio.qmd). Neither comparison is yet a cause. The exposure has to precede the disease, and a [confounding variable](confounding-variable.qmd) can produce the same numerical association. In an observational study of sunscreen and skin cancer, sun exposure raises both sunscreen use and cancer risk, so the crude association is not the effect of the cream.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 62, 461){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.4)

Maps and [time series](time-series.qmd) are the other workhorses. John Snow's study of cholera and the Thames is the classic spatial comparison of who drank which water. Screening programmes need [sensitivity and specificity](sensitivity-and-specificity.qmd), and those are not the chance that a person who tests positive is ill. Trials of vaccines and other population interventions, when they are randomised, use the same reporting discipline as other [randomised controlled trials](randomised-controlled-trial.qmd).{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 181){{cite:schulz_altman_moher2010}} (Introduction)



A numerical boundary keeps the definition attached to a calculation. List the integers from 1 through 9. Their total is 45, so their mean is 5.0. The first three integers have mean 2. The difference between the full-list mean and that three-integer mean is 3.0. The difference is a property of which units entered the average. It is not a property of the name of the field. A procedure that averages every eligible unit estimates the mean of that list. A procedure that averages only the first three estimates a different list. Public health has to say which list is the target before the average is interpreted.

The same arithmetic limits a model fitted to the observed rows. The mean 5.0 describes the integers that were listed. Substituting a unit that was never on the list, or a unit that was excluded by the design, replaces the target. A [confidence interval](confidence-interval.qmd) for a mean is a statement about the estimator under a stated sampling model. A [standard error](standard-error.qmd) is the standard deviation of that estimator. Neither object repairs a list that was the wrong list. The procedures already named here include [medical statistics](medical-statistics.qmd), [official statistics](official-statistics.qmd), [Spatial analysis](spatial-analysis.qmd), [odds ratio](odds-ratio.qmd).

Two further limits are algebraic. The sum of deviations from the mean 5.0 is zero, so the mean is the least-squares centre of these 9 numbers. A robust centre, or a median, answers a different minimisation. Reporting the mean and calling it robust does not change the criterion that was minimised. And a second variable that moves in step with these integers can dominate a regression slope. The slope then describes the joint list, which is a larger object than the one-column mean 5.0.

## History

John Graunt's 1662 analysis of causes of death is the usual start of a numerical epidemiology. William Petty's political arithmetic had already treated population figures as a subject of study. Adolphe Quetelet is credited with shaping the population statistics from which epidemiology, as well as other quantitative social sciences, later split. William Farr studied how death varied with age, sex, season, place, and occupation, and on that basis epidemiology was distinguished from statistics at large. James Lind's trial of citrus against scurvy and John Snow's demonstration that cholera followed contaminated water are the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century set pieces. Jerome Cornfield, in 1951, stated the notion of risk from a follow-up comparison of people with and without disease.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 181, 461)
