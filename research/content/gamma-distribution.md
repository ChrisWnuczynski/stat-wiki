## Motivating example
A call centre receives calls at a roughly steady rate, and the manager asks how long it will take to receive the third call. The waiting time for one call is exponential under a Poisson-process model; adding three independent interarrival times gives a Gamma waiting time with shape three. This turns the shape parameter into a count of stages and the rate parameter into the pace of arrivals. The same law also describes positive measurements beyond literal waiting times, and its algebra makes it useful as a prior for a Poisson event rate.{{cite:siegrist2022_gamma_waiting}} (§14.3, arrival times and Erlang law){{cite:grinstead_snell1997}} (§5.2, p. 207){{cite:siegrist2022_gamma}} (§5.8, moments and parameterization)

## Intuition — A power near zero and an exponential tail
The density has two jobs. Its power $x^{\alpha-1}$ controls behaviour near zero; its exponential factor $e^{-\lambda x}$ bends the right tail down. When $\alpha=1$, the density starts at its maximum and decreases like an exponential. For $\alpha>1$, it rises to an interior peak at $(\alpha-1)/\lambda$ before falling. A larger rate squeezes times toward zero without changing the standardized shape. A larger shape represents more accumulated stages and makes the distribution less skewed relative to its spread. The infobox records the mode $(\alpha-1)/\lambda$ for $\alpha>1$, coefficient of variation $1/\sqrt\alpha$, and skewness $2/\sqrt\alpha$; the shape–rate proof derives these identities.{{cite:siegrist2022_gamma}} (§5.8, shape, moments and scaling)

## Definition
The parameter convention matters in every computation. The convention $\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha,\lambda)$ uses shape $\alpha$ and **rate** $\lambda$; its scale is $b=1/\lambda$. Thus a source or software call using shape–scale notation writes the same law as $\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha,b)$ only if it explicitly says that the second argument is a scale. The mean is $\alpha/\lambda=\alpha b$, not $\alpha\lambda$. The lower incomplete gamma function in the CDF accumulates the density from zero to $x$, so it is a probability of a value *at most* $x$; survival is $1-F(x)$.{{cite:siegrist2022_gamma}} (§5.8, moments and parameterization){{cite:siegrist2022_gamma}} (§5.8, distribution functions)

## Properties — The MGF, equal-rate sums, and relative spread
The shape–rate MGF is finite exactly for $t<\lambda$, with $M(t)=(1-t/\lambda)^{-\alpha}$. For $t\ge\lambda$, the expectation diverges: its integrand is proportional to $x^{\alpha-1}e^{-(\lambda-t)x}$, which at $t=\lambda$ reduces to $x^{\alpha-1}$ and has a divergent integral at infinity for every $\alpha>0$; for $t>\lambda$, the integrand grows exponentially. On its finite domain, the MGF explains several relationships at once. Multiplying the MGFs of two independent gamma variables with the same rate adds their shapes. Taking shape one recovers the exponential, while shape $\nu/2$ and rate $1/2$ gives the chi-squared law with $\nu$ degrees of freedom. The integer-shape case is sometimes called an Erlang law. These statements need the shared-rate assumption: adding gamma variables with different rates does not, in general, yield a gamma law.{{cite:siegrist2022_gamma}} (§5.8, MGF and sums){{cite:siegrist2022_gamma}} (§5.8, moments and parameterization)

The variance is $\alpha/\lambda^2$, so it grows with the mean when shape is held fixed. The coefficient of variation is $1/\sqrt\alpha$, and skewness is $2/\sqrt\alpha$: large shape makes relative spread and asymmetry smaller. The density may diverge as $x\downarrow0$ when $\alpha<1$, yet its integral still equals one. At $\alpha=1$ the boundary density is finite; at $\alpha>1$ it starts at zero. The shape–rate proof derives these features from the gamma integral, cumulants and derivative of the log density.{{cite:siegrist2022_gamma}} (§5.8, moments, skewness and mode)

## Related distributions — Exponential, Erlang, chi-squared, and inverse-Gamma
The exponential distribution is the one-stage Gamma law, and the Erlang law is its positive-integer-shape extension. The chi-squared distribution is Gamma with shape half its degrees of freedom and rate one half. If $X$ has this Gamma law, $1/X$ is inverse-Gamma with the corresponding shape and reciprocal parameter convention; one must track whether the second parameter is rate or scale when changing variables. Gamma waiting times are linked to Poisson counts: the third arrival occurs after time $t$ exactly when at most two events have occurred by $t$.{{cite:siegrist2022_gamma_waiting}} (§14.3, arrival times and Poisson counts){{cite:siegrist2022_gamma}} (§5.8, moments and parameterization)

## Statistical inference — A duration model, and a conjugate update for a Poisson rate
For positive durations, a Gamma model can accommodate a right-skewed response and variance that grows with its mean. Whether it fits must be checked against the actual sampling process; a density on positive values alone does not imply a Poisson arrival mechanism. For a Poisson intensity $\theta$ observed over $n$ equal unit exposures, a $\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha,\lambda)$ prior and total count $S$ give $\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha+S,\lambda+n)$ posterior. Shape accumulates events and rate accumulates exposure. With unequal exposures $t_i$, the rate update is $\lambda+\sum_i t_i$, while the shape still adds the counts. This distinction matters in incidence-rate calculations. Gelman and colleagues use this update to model varying cancer rates across counties, where the exposure is the population observed over time.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (§§2.6–2.7, pp. 44–45, 47–50){{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§§1–2, pp. 269–272, Poisson–Gamma conjugacy){{cite:siegrist2022_gamma}} (§5.8, positive-valued models)

## Generating random samples
For an integer shape $k$, generate $k$ independent exponential waiting times with common rate $\lambda$ and add them. This construction displays the arrival-time interpretation, but it is not a general algorithm for noninteger shapes. SciPy's `gamma.rvs(a=α, scale=1/λ)` uses shape `a` and a **scale** argument; passing the rate as `scale` would simulate a different distribution. A simulation can be checked by comparing its empirical mean with $\alpha/\lambda$ and its empirical variance with $\alpha/\lambda^2$ as the number of draws grows.{{cite:siegrist2022_gamma_waiting}} (§14.3, exponential sums){{cite:grinstead_snell1997}} (§5.2, p. 207){{cite:scipy_gamma2026}} (§“scipy.stats.gamma”, Methods and Notes)

## Worked example
Suppose calls follow a Poisson process with rate $\lambda=2$ per hour. The waiting time $T_3$ to the third call is $\operatorname{Gamma}(3,2)$ in shape–rate form. Its mean is $3/2=1.5$ hours and variance $3/2^2=0.75$ hours squared. To ask whether the third call arrives after two hours, count arrivals by that time: the mean count is $\lambda t=4$. The event $T_3>2$ means zero, one or two calls by time two, so
$$
\Pr(T_3>2)=e^{-4}\left(1+4+\frac{4^2}{2}\right)=13e^{-4}\approx0.2381.
$$
The mean wait is $1.5$ hours. The tail calculation shows that about $24\%$ of waits exceed two hours; most finish by then, but a longer wait remains plausible. The CDF at two hours is $1-13e^{-4}\approx0.7619$.{{cite:siegrist2022_gamma_waiting}} (§14.3, Poisson count and arrival-time duality){{cite:grinstead_snell1997}} (§5.2, p. 207)

**Your turn.** At rate one event per hour, let $T_2$ be the time to the second event. Find its Gamma shape and rate, mean, variance, and probability of exceeding three hours.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The waiting time is $T_2\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(2,1)$, so its mean and variance are both $2$. The event $T_2>3$ means at most one arrival by hour three, hence $\Pr(T_2>3)=e^{-3}(1+3)=4e^{-3}\approx0.1991$. The second event has an expected wait of two hours, but the right tail leaves about one fifth of runs waiting past three hours.
:::

## Common misconceptions
The second parameter here is a rate, not a scale; confusing them reverses the direction of the mean change. Shape need not be an integer, even though the waiting-time sum has a literal number-of-events interpretation only at integer shape. A Gamma law for a duration does not prove that the underlying events form a Poisson process. Finally, a Gamma prior on a Poisson rate is a distribution *over possible rates*, whereas a Gamma waiting-time model is a distribution *over observed times*; they share a mathematical family but play different roles in a model.{{cite:siegrist2022_gamma}} (§5.8, moments and parameterization){{cite:siegrist2022_gamma_waiting}} (§14.3, integer-shape waiting times)

## Assumptions and limits — When the waiting-time model fails
A negative observation cannot come from the two-parameter Gamma law because its support is positive. If the call rate rises during a busy shift, the time to the third call no longer follows the stationary-Poisson Gamma law used in the worked example. Equal rates also matter for the sum property: independent $\operatorname{Exp}(1)$ and $\operatorname{Exp}(2)$ variables have sum mean $1+1/2=1.5$ and variance $1+1/4=1.25$; a Gamma law with shape two and mean $1.5$ would have rate $4/3$ and variance $2/(4/3)^2=1.125$, so that simple common-rate conclusion fails. These counterexamples concern support, process assumptions and algebra separately.{{cite:siegrist2022_gamma}} (§5.8, support and common-scale sum){{cite:siegrist2022_gamma_waiting}} (§14.3, stationary Poisson waiting times)

## Connections — Poisson processes, special cases, and two different modelling roles
The [Poisson process](homogeneous-poisson-process.qmd) links Gamma waiting times to count probabilities. [Exponential](exponential-distribution.qmd) and [chi-squared](chi-squared-distribution.qmd) laws are parameter choices within the family, while [inverse-Gamma](inverse-gamma-distribution.qmd) is its reciprocal transformation. The [Poisson–Gamma update](generic-exponential-family-conjugate-update.qmd) uses the same algebra as a Bayesian prior construction. [Gamma regression](gamma-regression.qmd) instead places a Gamma distribution on a positive response conditional on predictors.{{cite:siegrist2022_gamma}} (§5.8, special cases and exponential family){{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§§1–2, pp. 269–272, Poisson–Gamma conjugacy)

## History
Laplace’s 1812 treatment of probability developed generating functions as a general tool for analysing chance, part of the analytic setting in which later continuous distributions were studied.{{cite:laplace1812}} (pp. 1–4, Book I preface) Pearson’s 1895 Type III skew-frequency curve has the Gamma form after shifting its lower endpoint; his normalization explicitly uses the Gamma function.{{cite:pearson1895}} (pp. 373–374, Type III curve)

The Erlang name belongs to the integer-shape waiting-time form associated with sums of exponential interarrival times. Modern distribution handbooks retain separate Erlang and Gamma entries, a useful reminder that the arrival-count story is a special case of the continuous-shape family.{{cite:forbes2011}} (Ch. 15 and 22, pp. 84–85 and 109–113) The waiting-time interpretation also connects the family to renewal and queueing theory. Modern uses for positive responses and conjugate priors extend beyond waiting for an integer number of events.{{cite:grimmett_stirzaker2020}} (§§6.8, 8.3–8.4, contents){{cite:siegrist2022_gamma_waiting}} (§14.3, Erlang name and arrival times){{cite:siegrist2022_gamma}} (§5.8, general positive shape)
