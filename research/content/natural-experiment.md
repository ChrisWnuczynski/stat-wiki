## Lead context

Some treatments are handed out by a policy, a price, or a distance, rather than by an investigator with a randomisation list. A natural experiment is a comparison that uses that outside assignment as a candidate for separating people into treated and untreated groups. The data remain observational. The outside factor is useful only when it meets conditions that a real instrument would have met, and only one of those conditions can be checked in the data.{{cite:hernan_robins2020}} (pp. 210–211, §16.1){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.2.5)

## Motivating example

The effect of smoking cessation on weight cannot be read off the people who quit, because they differ from the people who do not. Cigarette price is a candidate for an outside push: a higher price may change the decision to quit, and the claim, if it can be sustained, is that price changes weight only through that decision and shares no causes with weight. A threshold such as a state price above or below \(\$1.50\) turns the push into a binary contrast. In one such split the chance of quitting was \(25.8\%\) on the high-price side and \(19.5\%\) on the low-price side, a risk difference of about six percentage points. That difference is the association of price with quitting. It is the check on whether the outside factor moves the treatment. It is not the effect of quitting on weight.{{cite:hernan_robins2020}} (pp. 210–211, §16.1)

## Definition

A natural experiment is an observational comparison in which the treatment contrast is generated by a process outside the study, and that process is proposed as an instrument or as a stand-in for assignment. Access is one such process: distance or travel time to a facility, calendar period when a treatment's availability changes, or the price of the treatment. The data remain observational. No treatment was explicitly applied or withheld by the investigator. Researchers perform an observational study when they collect data in a way that does not directly interfere with how the data arise.{{cite:hernan_robins2020}} (p. 211, Fine Point 16.1){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.2.5)

The outside factor \(Z\) is a candidate instrument when three conditions are proposed. \(Z\) is associated with the treatment \(A\). \(Z\) does not affect the outcome except through its potential effect on \(A\). \(Z\) and the outcome do not share causes. Of the three, only the association between \(Z\) and \(A\) can be examined in the data. The exclusion restriction and the absence of common causes are assumptions. They can sometimes be falsified, and the falsification tests have no power against most violations.{{cite:hernan_robins2020}} (pp. 209–211, §16.1)

An investigator's coin flip is an experiment because the investigator assigns the treatment. When the assignment uses a randomisation table, the experiment is a randomised experiment, which is the design from which a causal connection is the ordinary conclusion. A natural experiment keeps the logic of a contrast between groups and drops the investigator. Something else, already operating in the world, sorts people. Scientific randomisation, assignment through a random number table, is a different operation from an arbitrary or merely systematic split.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.4){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "randomization, scientific")

## Properties

The observed association between \(Z\) and the outcome is not the effect of the treatment. It is the effect of the outside factor, which is smaller than the treatment effect when the outside factor only partly changes treatment. In the cigarette-price split the quit rates differ by about six percentage points. That gap is the denominator of an instrumental-variable ratio, the association of \(Z\) with treatment, not the numerator that would be the association of \(Z\) with weight. A weak association, a denominator near zero, leaves the ratio too noisy to use. The price split is described as a weak instrument for that reason.{{cite:hernan_robins2020}} (p. 211, §16.1)

A second pattern uses a comparison group that never received the outside change, together with measurements from before and after. The extra change in the affected group is a [difference in differences](difference-in-differences.qmd). Under additive equi-confounding, the effect of treatment in the treated equals the association of treatment with the outcome minus the association of treatment with a pre-treatment measurement of the outcome. The pre-treatment association is the confounding on the additive scale. The identity uses that assumption. It is not an algebraic identity of the two observed contrasts.{{cite:hernan_robins2020}} (p. 106, Technical Point 7.3)

Because the investigator did not randomise, the protection of a randomised experiment is not what the contrast has. An arbitrary or systematic split can be associated with the outcome for reasons that have nothing to do with the treatment.{{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "randomization, scientific"){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.4)

## Statistical use

Natural experiments are analysed as observational studies that hope to borrow the identifying power of an instrument or of a pre-treatment comparison series. Distance to a treatment facility and calendar time are typical access instruments, and price is the access instrument in the smoking example. The resulting estimate is a causal effect only under the conditions named for that method. The data confirm the association of the instrument with the treatment when that association is present, and they do not confirm the exclusion restriction or the absence of common causes. A designed randomised experiment remains the design in which those conditions are created by the assignment rather than argued from subject-matter knowledge.{{cite:hernan_robins2020}} (pp. 210–211, §16.1){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §§1.2.5 and 1.4)

## Worked example

A bridge closure sends 4 recorded trips by the long route, with times 30, 40, 50, and 40 minutes, and 4 trips by the short route, with times 20, 25, 30, and 25 minutes. The investigator did not choose the route. The long-route mean is \(160/4 = 40\). The short-route mean is \(100/4 = 25\). The contrast is \(40 - 25 = 15\) minutes. That number is the association of route with time. It estimates a causal effect of route only if the closure's sorting satisfies the conditions used to interpret it.{{cite:hernan_robins2020}} (p. 211, §16.1){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.2.5)

**Your turn.** One more short-route trip takes 20 minutes. What are the new short-route mean and the new contrast with the long-route mean of 40?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The short-route times sum to 120 over 5 trips, so the mean is 24. The contrast is \(40 - 24 = 16\).
:::

## Common misconceptions

Calling a comparison natural does not make it randomised. The outside factor can be associated with the outcome for reasons that have nothing to do with the treatment, and then the contrast is confounded. Observational data support associations. A causal conclusion from them is the conclusion the identifying assumptions deliver, and those assumptions are not verified by the size of the contrast.{{cite:hernan_robins2020}} (p. 211, §16.1){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.2.5)

A large association between the outside factor and the treatment is not the treatment effect. In the cigarette-price split the quit rates were \(25.8\%\) and \(19.5\%\). The six-point gap is how strongly the candidate instrument moves quitting. The effect of quitting on weight is a different contrast, recovered from an instrumental-variable ratio only when the three instrumental conditions hold.{{cite:hernan_robins2020}} (pp. 210–211, §16.1)

## History

Treating price, distance, and calendar time as substitutes for assignment is a development of instrumental-variable work in observational studies. Access instruments of that kind are reviewed as a standard category alongside genetic variants and physician preference. The insistence that a causal connection is the business of a randomised experiment, in which researchers assign treatments, is the contrasting tradition in introductory design. Difference-in-differences, the before-and-after form of the same idea, is the method that subtracts a pre-treatment contrast under additive equi-confounding, with the econometric references for that method running through Card, Meyer, and Angrist and Krueger.{{cite:hernan_robins2020}} (p. 106, Technical Point 7.3; p. 211, Fine Point 16.1){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.4)
