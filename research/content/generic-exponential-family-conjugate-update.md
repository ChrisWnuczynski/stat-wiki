## Lead context
A conjugate update lets a Bayesian analysis incorporate new data by adding a small set of sample summaries to prior hyperparameters.{{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§§1–2, pp. 269–273)

## Motivating example
Suppose daily counts follow a Poisson model with unknown rate. A Gamma prior for that rate remains Gamma after counts are observed, and its shape and rate change by the observed count total and number of days. This familiar arithmetic is an instance of a wider rule: when a likelihood has an exponential-family form, a prior with a matching kernel can absorb the natural sufficient statistic and sample size into its coefficients. The rule is useful because it avoids starting a new integration from scratch after every observation. It does not by itself tell us whether that prior is scientifically appropriate.{{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§§1–2, pp. 269–273){{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§2, pp. 270–272)

## Intuition
The likelihood's parameter-dependent part is the exponential of a linear pairing between a natural parameter and a data summary, minus a log-normalizer. A conjugate prior has the same two parameter-dependent pieces. Multiplication adds the coefficients of those pieces: data supply a statistic total and one unit of sample size per observation. This is why the update is additive, regardless of whether the posterior receives a familiar name such as Beta or Gamma. “Conjugate” describes closure of a *family* under a given likelihood, not a special virtue of any particular hyperparameter choice.{{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§§1–2, pp. 269–273)

## Key results — The conjugate update
Write one observation as $p(x\mid\eta)=h(x)\exp\{\eta^{\mathsf T}T(x)-A(\eta)\}$. On the natural-parameter scale, a prior density with kernel $\exp\{\eta^{\mathsf T}\alpha-\nu A(\eta)\}$ produces a posterior with $\alpha'=\alpha+\sum_iT(x_i)$ and $\nu'=\nu+n$. The first hyperparameter is a vector with the same dimension as $T$; the second is a scalar. The interpretation of $\nu$ as a prior sample size is useful in some families, but only after the reference measure and hyperparameter range are specified. A symbolic match of kernels does not prove that either prior or posterior integrates to one.{{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§2, eqs. 2.1–2.3, pp. 270–272){{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§§1–2, pp. 269–273)

The parameter scale matters. The theorem is phrased as a density in $\eta$ relative to $d\eta$. If $\eta=c(\theta)$ and a practitioner instead writes a density relative to $d\theta$, transforming that density to $\eta$ introduces $|d\theta/d\eta|$. The additive coefficient rule still follows for a matching kernel on whichever measure is declared, but the named distribution and its shape may shift. In the Poisson example, the prior $\theta^\alpha e^{-\beta\theta}$ is a density kernel in the mean rate $\theta$; its Gamma shape is $\alpha+1$. Writing the same expression as a density in $\eta=\log\theta$ would describe a different prior. This is the reason the “$+1$” cannot be dropped as a notational convenience.{{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§§1–2, pp. 269–273)

## Generalizations — Batch order and propriety
For a fixed proper prior, successive independent batches commute: updating by batch A then B gives $\alpha+S_A+S_B$ and $\nu+n_A+n_B$, the same parameters as updating by B then A. This is Bayesian sequential updating expressed in the family's coordinates. The likelihood also depends on the observed sample through $S=\sum_iT(x_i)$ for inference on $\eta$, so this statistic is sufficient under the usual fixed-support exponential-family conditions. The posterior therefore needs the sample total and sample size, rather than the ordering of observations. The proof below gives the kernel multiplication that establishes both one-batch and sequential updates.{{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§§1–2, pp. 269–273){{cite:dasgupta2011_exponential_family}} (§18.4.1, pp. 512–514)

Conjugacy does not guarantee a closed-form normalizing constant or posterior mean. In a complex exponential family, the posterior may have the matching kernel but still require numerical integration or simulation for a reported probability. Nor does every prior on the parameter share the conjugate form. Properness is a separate condition, and the hyperparameter values that make the normalizer finite depend on the family.{{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§§1–2, pp. 269–273)

### Assumptions and limits
The simple addition assumes conditionally independent observations with a common natural parameter and support handled consistently. If one observation has a different exposure in a Poisson model, the log-normalizer contribution is exposure-weighted rather than simply one; the rate hyperparameter receives total exposure instead of bare $n$. If a supposedly common support depends on the unknown parameter, a dropped indicator can change the posterior's support and invalidate kernel matching. If a prior kernel is not integrable, the posterior needs its own propriety check; the algebraic update alone cannot supply it. And when a likelihood includes dispersion or a noncanonical parameterization, the natural statistic and normalizer must be identified before using the shorthand $\alpha+S,\nu+n$.{{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§§1–2, pp. 269–273)

## Statistical use — Applying the update
To apply the rule, write the sampling density with its support, identify $T(x)$, the natural parameter and $A$, and state the measure used for the parameter prior. Multiply one observation's likelihood by the prior to see which coefficients move. For $n$ observations, add $S$ and $n$, then check the resulting integral and translate the kernel back into a named distribution only after matching its exact exponent convention. This order prevents a one-unit Gamma-shape error and prevents a likelihood support restriction from being silently lost. The Rayleigh, Pareto and Poisson examples show three different ways that kernel recognition works in practice.{{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§§1–2, pp. 269–273){{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§2, p. 271)

## Worked example
### Worked example
Let the Poisson mean $\theta$ have prior $\operatorname{Gamma}(3,2)$ in the shape–rate convention. Its density kernel relative to $d\theta$ is $\theta^{3-1}e^{-2\theta}=\theta^2e^{-2\theta}$, corresponding to the $\alpha=2$ and $\beta=2$ notation. Four observed counts $2,0,3,3$ have $S=8$ and likelihood kernel $\theta^8e^{-4\theta}$. Adding exponents gives posterior kernel $\theta^{10}e^{-6\theta}$, hence $\theta\mid x\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(11,6)$ with mean $11/6=1.8333$. The sample mean is $8/4=2$, while the prior mean is $3/2=1.5$. The posterior mean is a weighted average, $(2/6)(1.5)+(4/6)(2)=1.8333$; the posterior balances the prior's two rate units with four observed days.{{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§§1–2, pp. 269–273)

**Your turn.** Starting again from $\operatorname{Gamma}(3,2)$ on the Poisson rate, observe counts $1,0,4$. Find the updated coefficients $\alpha,\beta$, the posterior Gamma shape and rate, and its mean.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
Here $S=1+0+4=5$ and $n=3$. The coefficients move from $(\alpha,\beta)=(2,2)$ to $(7,5)$, because $\alpha'=2+5=7$ and $\beta'=2+3=5$. The posterior is $\operatorname{Gamma}(8,5)$, with shape $\alpha'+1=8$ and mean $8/5=1.6$. A shape of $7$ would confuse the coefficient of $\log\theta$ with the Gamma shape.
:::

## Common misconceptions
The prior hyperparameters encode a choice made before these data. The update is not “add the sample mean”: the natural statistic is generally a sum, while $n$ updates a separate coefficient. A matching unnormalized kernel is not automatically a proper posterior, since its integral may diverge. Finally, a Gamma exponent $\theta^\alpha$ does not imply Gamma shape $\alpha$: the shape is $\alpha+1$ for a density in $\theta$ under the stated shape–rate convention.{{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§§1–2, pp. 269–273)

## History
Raiffa and Schlaifer's 1961 decision-theory treatment organized applied conjugate analysis around tractable updating. Diaconis and Ylvisaker's 1979 paper studied the general exponential-family prior form and its propriety and expectation properties. The simple coefficient-addition rule is the algebraic part of that broader theory; it remains useful even where normalization or posterior summaries are computationally difficult.{{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§1, p. 269, Raiffa and Schlaifer reference){{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§§1–2, pp. 269–272){{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§§1–2, pp. 269–273)
