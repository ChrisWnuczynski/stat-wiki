## Motivating example
A genetic model predicts that three chicken types should occur in proportions $1:2:1$. A sample of $93$ has counts $23,50,20$. Each observed count differs from its prediction, but differences of the same size matter differently in common and rare categories. Pearson's statistic compares squared deviations with the corresponding expected counts, putting them on a common scale before adding them. A large total is evidence against the proposed category probabilities, provided the sampling and large-sample conditions are credible.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, Test Statistic and Critical Region)

## Intuition — Comparing counts with expected counts
For a category expected to contain $E$ observations under the model, multinomial sampling fluctuations are typically of order $\sqrt E$. Thus $(O-E)^2/E$ is a squared discrepancy measured in approximate sampling units. The statistic adds such terms because departures in any of the categories can count against the null model. It deliberately discards signs: too many observations in one category and too few in another both contribute. Yet its total alone does not tell us *which* cells caused the discrepancy; the individual contributions and table should be inspected as well.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, Test Statistic)

## Definition
For $n$ independent observations classified into $k$ mutually exclusive categories, let $O_i$ be the count in category $i$. Under specified null probabilities $\pi_i>0$ with $\sum_i\pi_i=1$, the expected count is $E_i=n\pi_i$ and the **Pearson statistic** is

$$T=\sum_{i=1}^k\frac{(O_i-E_i)^2}{E_i}=\sum_{i=1}^k\frac{(O_i-n\pi_i)^2}{n\pi_i}.$$

This is a statistic computed from the counts and specified null model; it is not itself a probability or a $p$-value. A larger value means greater aggregate discrepancy on this scale. If a model parameter is estimated from the same grouped counts, replace $\pi_i$ by fitted probabilities $\widehat p_i$, but also adjust the reference degrees of freedom.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, Test Statistic and Critical Region)

## Properties
Under fixed, positive null probabilities and a sufficiently large multinomial sample, $T$ is approximately $\chi^2_{k-1}$. There are $k-1$ rather than $k$ free fluctuations because the observed counts and expected counts both sum to $n$: once the first $k-1$ deviations are known, the last is determined. This approximation, rather than an exact finite-sample identity, supplies the usual upper-tail $p$-value and critical value. It improves as expected counts grow, and its quality depends on the cell probabilities as well as the total sample size.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, Critical Region and sample-size limitation){{cite:pearson1900}} (pp. 157–160, criterion and tail calculation)

If the $k$ probabilities come from a regular model with $r$ free parameters estimated by grouped-data maximum likelihood, the usual reference law is $\chi^2_{k-1-r}$. Estimation consumes directions in which the fitted probabilities can follow the data. For instance, eight categories compared with a binomial family having one fitted probability use $8-1-1=6$ degrees of freedom. The simple subtraction needs an identifiable, interior parameter and enough information in the cells; it is not a universal rule for arbitrary data-dependent fitting.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, estimation from bin counts and Critical Region)

For a two-way table under independence, the fitted count in row $a$, column $b$ is $R_aC_b/n$. The resulting Pearson statistic has approximately $(r-1)(c-1)$ degrees of freedom for an $r\times c$ table. In a $2\times2$ table it equals the square of the pooled two-proportion $z$ statistic, so the chi-squared and two-sided $z$ tests agree. The same Pearson formula thus tests either specified category probabilities or an independence model, but the way expected counts and degrees of freedom are obtained changes.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, fitted probabilities and degrees-of-freedom principle)

The likelihood-ratio goodness-of-fit statistic $G^2=2\sum_i O_i\log(O_i/E_i)$ measures discrepancy differently. A Taylor expansion around $O_i=E_i$ gives $G^2\approx T$ when all relative discrepancies are small; the first-order terms cancel because $\sum_i(O_i-E_i)=0$. The two statistics can disagree noticeably in sparse tables, where neither chi-squared reference approximation should be trusted automatically.{{cite:pearson1900}} (pp. 163–165, fit criterion){{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, sample-size limitation)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Put $\mathbf s=(\sqrt{\pi_1},\ldots,\sqrt{\pi_k})^\top$ and $Z_i=(O_i-n\pi_i)/\sqrt{n\pi_i}$. The multinomial central limit theorem gives $\mathbf Z\Rightarrow N_k(0,I-\mathbf s\mathbf s^\top)$ under the null. Because $\|\mathbf s\|=1$, the covariance is the orthogonal projection onto the $(k-1)$-dimensional space perpendicular to $\mathbf s$. In an orthonormal basis of that space the limit has $k-1$ independent standard-normal coordinates. Since $T=\sum_i Z_i^2$, continuous mapping yields $T\Rightarrow\chi^2_{k-1}$. This derives the fixed-probability approximation; fitted-model degrees of freedom require a further projection onto the complement of the model's score directions.{{cite:pearson1900}} (pp. 157–160, original quadratic criterion and tail calculation)

For the relation to likelihood ratio, write $O_i=E_i+\delta_i$ and expand $2O_i\log(O_i/E_i)=2\delta_i+\delta_i^2/E_i+O(\delta_i^3/E_i^2)$. Summing removes $2\sum_i\delta_i=0$ and leaves $T$ to second order.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, grouped likelihood and test statistic)

## Statistical use — Expected counts before the tail
First specify the categories and null model, then calculate each $E_i$ using that model. Review small expected counts before using a chi-squared tail, and decide whether categories can be combined for a substantive reason or whether an exact or simulated null distribution is preferable. Compare the observed $T$ with the *upper* tail of its reference distribution, because large deviations in either direction contribute positively. A non-rejection means the data do not show a discrepancy large enough for this test; it does not establish that the proposed distribution is exactly true. The result can change if continuous data are binned differently.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, Purpose, Test Statistic and sample-size limitation){{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 73–75, Chi-square Goodness of Fit Test)

## Worked example
For the $1:2:1$ chicken model, the expected counts from $n=93$ are $23.25,46.5,23.25$. The contributions from the observed counts $23,50,20$ are approximately $0.003,0.263,0.454$, so $T=0.72043\approx0.72$. Three fixed categories give $3-1=2$ degrees of freedom; the $5\%$ upper critical value is $5.991$, and the approximate $p$-value is $0.698\approx0.70$. The observed differences are small on the model's sampling scale, so the test does not reject the $1:2:1$ proportions. The conclusion concerns compatibility with this model, not proof of the genetic theory.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, Test Statistic and Critical Region)

A second table shows how to calculate the same statistic with more categories. In $120$ rolls of a die, the face counts $14,22,16,25,19,24$ sum to $120$. Fairness gives six expected counts of $20$ and $T=(36+4+16+25+1+16)/20=4.90$. With $6-1=5$ degrees of freedom, $4.90<11.07$, the $5\%$ critical value; the upper-tail $p$-value is about $0.43$. This is again a failure to reject, not a guarantee that every face probability is $1/6$.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, Test Statistic and Critical Region)

**Your turn.** Four specified categories have expected counts $10,10,10,10$ and observed counts $7,13,8,12$. Calculate $T$, its reference degrees of freedom, and the direction of evidence.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The squared deviations are $9,9,4,4$, so $T=(9+9+4+4)/10=2.6$. With four fixed probabilities the reference law is approximately $\chi^2_3$. Because $2.6$ is a modest value rather than a large upper-tail departure, it offers little evidence against the specified probabilities; the conclusion still depends on the chosen significance threshold.
:::

## Common misconceptions
An observed count is not divided by its observed size in a Pearson term: the denominator is the *null expected* count. The statistic is not exactly chi-squared in a finite multinomial sample; the reference law is an approximation. A small $T$ is not a posterior probability or proof that the null is true. And estimating parameters from the same counts does not leave the degrees of freedom unchanged. These errors affect different steps: calculation, calibration, interpretation and model fitting.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, Test Statistic, Critical Region and sample-size limitation)

## Assumptions and limits — Small or zero expected counts
If expected counts are $0.2$ in several cells, a single observation in one of them creates a large Pearson contribution, while the chi-squared approximation can be poor. If a category has null probability zero but is observed once, the corresponding denominator is zero and the usual finite statistic is not defined; the null is already contradicted by that observation. If $100$ measurements are classified into bins chosen *after* examining their peaks, the nominal reference law does not account for the search over bin boundaries. Dependent observations, such as repeated measurements from one subject treated as independent counts, likewise violate the multinomial sampling argument. These cases call for a model or calibration matched to the design.{{cite:nist_chi_square_gof2002}} (§1.3.5.15, binned-data and sample-size limitations)

## History
Pearson's 1900 paper set out a quadratic discrepancy criterion for assessing whether a system of observed frequency deviations could plausibly arise through random sampling. It also applied the criterion to grouped frequency data, including dice experiments. The modern multinomial statement isolates the count model and its $k-1$ constraint more clearly than the original account. Subsequent work on fitting parameters clarified that the reference degrees of freedom must reflect the model fitted to the same data.{{cite:pearson1900}} (pp. 157–160, 165–169, criterion and dice examples){{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 74–75, Chi-square Goodness of Fit Test; p. 79, history)
