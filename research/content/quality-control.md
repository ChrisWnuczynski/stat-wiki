## Lead context
Quality control, in industry, is the use of sampling, control charts, and designed changes to keep a product or a process within its specification. The statistical part is younger than the craft of making things well. Guilds and inspections aimed at quality long before anyone plotted a chart with limits computed from the process's own variation. Statistical quality control begins when that variation is measured and the decision to intervene is tied to a probability calculation rather than to a single craftsman's judgement.{{cite:nist_pmc11_2002}} (§6.1.1)

## Definition
Process monitoring watches a quality characteristic over time. Process control is the active change made when the monitoring says the process has left the state it was designed to hold. The NIST handbook separates the two. Monitoring detects. Control changes the process, either by a person following an out-of-control action plan or by an automated loop that corrects in proportion to the measurement. A chart that is never allowed to change the process is a monitoring tool. A change that is made with no chart is not statistical control.{{cite:nist_pmc13_2002}} (§6.1.3)

A control chart is the usual monitoring display. For one quality characteristic it plots the statistic, or the individual measurement, against sample number or against time. A centre line marks the mean of the in-control process. An upper control limit and a lower control limit mark the band inside which almost all of the points should fall while only common-cause variation is present. A point outside the limits is treated as evidence of an assignable cause, and it starts a search. Where the limits are placed sets the risk of that search when no assignable cause is present. Limits drawn as \(0.001\) probability limits, under chance causes alone, put probability \(0.001\) on a point above the upper limit and the same probability on a point below the lower limit.{{cite:nist_pmc31_2002}} (§6.3.1)

A univariate chart watches one characteristic. A multivariate chart watches a statistic that summarises several characteristics at once. The logic of the limits is the same. The statistic is different, because a shift that is small in each variable can be large in a combination of them.{{cite:nist_pmc31_2002}} (§6.3.1)

Sampling inspection is the companion tool for lots that have already been made. Dodge and Romig, at Bell Laboratories with Shewhart, developed the sampling-inspection side of the theory. A sample of a lot is measured, and the lot is accepted or rejected according to a rule whose risks can be computed. The chart and the sampling plan are different decisions. One asks whether the process, as it runs, is still the process that was stable. The other asks whether a finished lot meets a specification.{{cite:nist_pmc11_2002}} (§6.1.1)

## Statistical use
Shewhart's 1924 memorandum sketched the modern control chart, and his 1931 book, *Economic Control of Quality of Manufactured Product*, set the pattern for applying statistical methods to process control. The practical sequence since then is to bring a process into a stable state, estimate the centre and the spread from data taken in that state, draw the limits from that estimate, and plot new points against the limits. A point beyond the limits, or a pattern the rule treats as a signal, triggers the action plan. The action plan is part of the method. A limit with no plan is a picture.{{cite:nist_pmc11_2002}} (§6.1.1){{cite:nist_pmc13_2002}} (§6.1.3)

The centre and the limits are estimated from the process, not copied from the specification. A specification says what the customer will accept. A control limit says what the process produces when it is stable. A process can be inside its control limits and outside its specification, if the natural spread is wider than the tolerance. It can be outside its control limits and inside its specification, if a shift is real and still too small to make the product unacceptable. The two comparisons answer different questions, and a quality system that uses one number for both will intervene when it should not, or fail to intervene when the product is already bad.

Designed experiments sit beside the chart. Once an assignable cause is suspected, or once a stable process is still not capable, an experiment changes factors on purpose and estimates their effects. The chart is a poor tool for that exploration. It is built to detect a departure from a stable mean, not to compare several settings. Dodge's list of statistical applications in engineering includes design and efficiency testing, and in commerce it includes industrial planning. Those are the experimental and the operational neighbours of the chart.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 519){{cite:nist_pmc13_2002}} (§6.1.3)

The probability attached to a limit is a risk calculation. With \(0.001\) limits, a stable process produces a false search about one time in a thousand on each side. Tightening the limits raises that risk and shortens the time until a real shift is seen. Widening them does the opposite. The choice is a cost comparison between needless searches and missed shifts. The \(0.001\) figure is one placement of that cost, not a property of every chart. Moving the limits changes the probability of a false search and the probability of missing a shift, in opposite directions.{{cite:nist_pmc31_2002}} (§6.3.1)

## Common misconceptions
A control limit is not a specification limit. Crossing a control limit is evidence about the stability of the process. Crossing a specification limit is evidence about the acceptability of the product. A chart that draws the specification in place of the control limits will ignore shifts that matter to stability and will alarm on variation the process always had, or the reverse, depending on which band is wider.

A point inside the limits does not prove that no assignable cause is present. The limits are a rule with a false-negative risk. A small shift can leave most points inside a wide band for a long time. Runs and other patterns are additional rules precisely because a single point is a coarse signal. Using only the points outside the limits, and ignoring a drift that stays inside, misses the shifts those extra rules were added to catch.{{cite:nist_pmc31_2002}} (§6.3.1)

A chart does not improve a process by being plotted. Improvement is the change made when the chart signals, or the redesign made when a stable process is not capable. Plotting a stable, incapable process documents the incapability. It does not shrink the spread. And a chart computed from data that were already unstable estimates a centre and a spread that mix the common-cause variation with the assignable causes. The limits are then too wide, and later assignable causes hide inside them.

Sampling inspection of lots is not a substitute for process control. Rejecting bad lots removes bad product from the customer and leaves the process that made the lots untouched. Shewhart's contribution was to move attention to the process. A plant that only inspects finished lots is using the older half of quality control.{{cite:nist_pmc11_2002}} (§6.1.1)

## Connections
[Statistical process control](statistical-process-control.qmd) is the running system of charts, rules, and action plans. [Designed experiments](designed-experiment.qmd) are the tool for estimating the effect of a change once monitoring has posed the question, or once a stable process needs a new setting. A [standard error](standard-error.qmd) is the scale on which a mean's variability is judged, and a control limit for a mean is a multiple of that scale. The risk of a false search is a probability of the same kind as the [risk](risk-assessment.qmd) computed in other applied settings: the probability of a harmful or costly event, here the event of hunting for a cause that is not there. [Random error](random-error.qmd) is the chance scatter the limits are built to contain. A systematic shift is what the limits are built to detect.{{cite:nist_pmc31_2002}} (§6.3.1){{cite:nist_type_a2002}} (§2.5.3)

## History
Quality control as a commercial practice is as old as competition among makers. The NIST history points to medieval guilds, which required long apprenticeships and a demonstration of skill, and to modern factory inspection and regulatory bodies. Statistical quality control is much newer. Statistical theory itself developed over two or three centuries, first in astronomy, physics, and the biological and social sciences. Application to manufacturing waited until sampling theory was far enough along, in the 1920s. Shewhart, at Bell Telephone Laboratories, issued the 16 May 1924 memorandum with a sketch of a control chart, and published the 1931 book that set the tone. Dodge and Romig built the sampling-inspection theory beside him. Those three accounts, chart, book, and sampling plans, are the core the handbook still points to. Later industrial statistics added experiments for improvement and multivariate charts for several characteristics at once. The definition of a signal, a point outside limits whose placement is a chosen risk, has not changed.{{cite:nist_pmc11_2002}} (§6.1.1){{cite:nist_pmc31_2002}} (§6.3.1)
