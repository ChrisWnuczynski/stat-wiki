## Lead context
Reproducibility is the property that a published result can be regenerated from the data and the code that produced it. Replication is a different property: independent investigators collect new data and ask whether the finding returns. The two are easy to collapse in ordinary speech, and they answer different questions. A result can be regenerated exactly and still be a bad description of a new sample. A result can be true of a new sample and still be impossible to regenerate, because the code was never kept. Computational science made the gap practical. Data sets and analyses became too large for a methods paragraph to specify, and the analysis itself became a program.{{cite:peng2011}} (pp. 1226–1227){{cite:osc2015}} (Introduction)

## Definition
Peng defines the reproducibility standard as the requirement that the data and the computer code used to analyse the data be available to others. The standard falls short of full replication, because the same data are analysed again rather than independently collected data. Under the standard, a reader can explore the data and the code far enough to check the quality of the claims. The aim is to fill the gap between full replication of a study and no replication at all. Between those endpoints there is a spectrum. A study is more or less reproducible according to what data and what code are shared. A review of microarray gene-expression analyses, which Peng cites, found studies that were not reproducible, studies that were partially reproducible with discrepancies, and studies that were reproducible, and the range was largely explained by the availability of data and metadata.{{cite:peng2011}} (pp. 1226–1227)

The standard has a mechanical basis. A computational experiment has, in theory, a detailed log of every action the computer took. Publishing that log gives a more precise description of the analysis than a methods paragraph in natural language. The barrier Peng emphasises is that the code is often no longer available. Interactive software used for exploratory analysis typically does not record the user's actions. Even when the analysis is a script, several packages are used and the code that joins their results is not saved. Changing the software, or persuading people to leave tools they know, is slow. Non-open-source software can be changed only by its owners, who may not treat reproducibility as a priority.{{cite:peng2011}} (pp. 1226–1227)

Replication, in the sense used by the Open Science Collaboration, is the attempt to recreate the conditions believed sufficient for a previously observed finding, and to establish that finding with new data. A direct replication can miss because a difference between the studies changes the effect, because the original result was a false positive, or because the replication was a false negative. Those are facts about a new sample. They are not facts about whether the original table can be rebuilt. The Collaboration's introduction treats debates about evidence as empty when the evidence being debated is not reproducible. Reproducibility of the record is a condition for a useful argument about replication. It is not the replication.{{cite:osc2015}} (Introduction)

## Interpretation
A kite-mark that says an analysis was regenerated is not a mark that the result is correct. Peng is explicit: reproducibility does not guarantee the quality, correctness, or validity of the published results. The "R" mark used by the journal *Biostatistics* means that a knowledgeable person ran the code on the data and produced the results the authors claimed. When a result is questionable, that regeneration is how a computational error is found. When a result is interesting, regeneration is how the next analysis is built. The mark is a statement about the record. The scientific claim still has to be judged.{{cite:peng2011}} (pp. 1226–1227)

The *Biostatistics* policy, started in July 2009, is a concrete reading of the spectrum. Authors of accepted papers may submit code or data as supporting material. They may also request a reproducibility review, in which an associate editor runs the code and checks that it produces the published results. A "D" marks data. A "C" marks code. An "R" marks a passed review. As of July 2011, \(21\) of \(125\) articles had a kite-mark, and five had an "R." The articles covered biostatistical methods, epidemiology, and genomics. In that small set the journal had not yet met a case in which the data existed and the code failed to run, but the counts already show that a policy of encouragement does not make regeneration universal. Most accepted articles in those two years carried no mark.{{cite:peng2011}} (pp. 1226–1227)

The replication project's numbers are about a different failure. Original psychological effects had mean about \(0.403\), and replication effects had mean about \(0.197\). About \(36\%\) of replications were significant in the same direction. Those figures can be computed only because the original studies were described well enough to repeat and because the replications were themselves recorded. A literature in which the code cannot be run cannot produce a comparable audit of effect sizes. A literature in which every analysis regenerates can still have a low replication rate, because regeneration uses the old sample. The two rates measure different things, and a field can be high on one and low on the other.{{cite:osc2015}} (Results){{cite:peng2011}} (pp. 1226–1227)

Ioannidis's positive predictive value is a third, again different, quantity. It is the probability that a significant claim is true, as a function of prior odds, power, and the Type I error rate. Reproducibility of the code does not appear in the formula. A false claim can be regenerated perfectly. A true claim can be impossible to regenerate. The formula explains why many significant claims fail when new data are collected. It does not explain why a script is missing.{{cite:ioannidis2005}} (p. 0696)

## Statistical use
Peng's practical order is by increasing cost. Anyone doing computation should publish the code, even if it is not clean. Code without the data can still show problems and can still carry an idea. Editors and reviewers can require this now, at almost no extra cost, because public repositories already exist. The next steps share data and metadata, then build the habit of a reproducibility review of the kind *Biostatistics* piloted. The last step he proposes is shared infrastructure, a place for data and a place for code, linked to each other and to the papers, analogous to a central archive for articles. Fields that already have repositories would keep them and connect them. Fields that do not would gain a place to put the record. Waiting for that infrastructure before publishing any code leaves the current papers unregenerable.{{cite:peng2011}} (pp. 1226–1227)

For a study that will be replicated rather than only regenerated, the statistical use is to publish the materials that define the finding: the outcome, the exclusion rules, the analysis that produced the quoted effect. The Collaboration required replication materials and data to be archived, and it required replications to be powered, at least \(80\%\), on the original effect size. Power on an inflated original effect is power against the wrong target, which is one reason high average power, \(0.92\), still produced a \(36.1\%\) significance rate. The archive is what lets a later reader see the target that was used.{{cite:osc2015}} (Method, Replication Protocol, and Results)

A report should say which standard it meets. "The analysis regenerates" means the code and data produce the published numbers. "The finding replicates" means a new sample, under the stated conditions, produces a comparable effect. "The claim is true" means something stronger than either, and neither standard delivers it alone. Peng's warning covers the first. The replication project covers the second. Ioannidis's formula is one model of the third, inside the assumptions of that model.{{cite:peng2011}} (pp. 1226–1227){{cite:ioannidis2005}} (p. 0696)

## History
Replication has the longer history. Peng calls it the ultimate standard by which scientific claims are judged, and he describes a culture of replication that weeds out spurious claims when independent investigators can repeat the work. That culture becomes difficult when the object of study is a large public survey, a climate model, or a cohort that takes years to follow. The Sloan Digital Sky Survey is his example of a data set that others can analyse without repeating the observation. In those settings he argues for reproducibility as a minimum standard, a substitute that is attainable when full replication is not. The argument is from the constraints of the data, not from a preference for computation over new samples.{{cite:peng2011}} (pp. 1226–1227)

The *Biostatistics* kite-mark policy of 2009 is an institutional step. The counts from the first two years, \(21\) marked articles out of \(125\), and five fully reviewed, are a baseline rather than a success rate for science as a whole. The Open Science Collaboration's 2015 replications are the empirical step on the replication side, in psychology, and they presuppose that the original studies were reproducible enough to attempt. The two dates mark different standards becoming explicit. They do not mark the invention of checking one's work.{{cite:peng2011}} (pp. 1226–1227){{cite:osc2015}} (Introduction)
