## Motivating example
A dataset can contain a recording error, a mixture of groups, a nonlinear association or a trend that is invisible in a single summary. Exploratory data analysis begins by examining those possibilities through complementary summaries and displays. The goal is to learn what needs explanation and which assumptions deserve scrutiny before committing to a narrow model. The question, the sampling mechanism and the measurement units still guide the exploration.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 192, Exploratory Data Analysis)

## Definition — Exploration is a way of reasoning
Exploratory data analysis combines graphical investigation, numerical summaries, transformations and diagnostic checks to investigate a dataset's structure. It is broader than making a standard collection of plots: useful exploration follows up discrepancies and revises the questions being asked. A histogram describes a distribution, a scatterplot investigates a relationship, and a plot against observation order investigates change. Their different roles make agreement or disagreement between displays informative.{{cite:nist_eda2002}} (§1.1.1)

A practical starting point is to establish what an observation represents, which fields are missing, what units are used and whether repetitions are legitimate. A value outside a plausible range could indicate a unit mismatch, a coding convention or a scientifically interesting extreme. The response should depend on evidence about the measurement, rather than an automatic rule to delete unusual values. Validation and interpretation are distinct stages, and both are necessary.{{cite:downey2014_thinkstats2}} (Ch. 1, “Validation” and “Interpretation”)

## Interpretation — Resistance reveals sensitivity
A resistant summary changes comparatively little when a small fraction of observations is moved far away. The sample median often plays this role, whereas the mean responds directly to the magnitudes of all values. Comparing them can reveal asymmetry or sensitivity. Such disagreement is a clue, not a complete account of the data-generating process. Likewise, a transformation can make a multiplicative pattern easier to study, but it changes the scale on which differences are interpreted.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 192, resistance and re-expression)

The choice of a graphical resolution also matters. A histogram depends on bin boundaries and widths, and a scatterplot can obscure overlaps. Looking at several defensible resolutions helps distinguish stable structure from a display artefact. Splitting observations by a scientifically relevant group can expose a relationship hidden in the aggregate; indiscriminately splitting into many small groups can instead create unstable patterns. The analysis must keep track of which comparisons were considered.{{cite:downey2014_thinkstats2}} (Ch. 2, histograms and outliers; Ch. 7, relationships between variables)

## Worked example — One value changes the story
Consider measurements $(2,3,3,4,5)$. Their mean is $3.4$, median is $3$ and range is $3$. If the final value is recorded as $50$, the mean rises to $12.4$, while the median stays $3$ and the range becomes $48$. A sorted-value display makes the isolated high measurement immediately visible. The calculations do not decide whether $50$ is wrong; they show how strongly the mean depends on investigating that measurement. A plot against time or group may then reveal whether it belongs to a different measurement regime.{{cite:downey2014_thinkstats2}} (Ch. 2, “Outliers” and “Summarizing distributions”)

**Your turn.** If that final measurement is corrected to $8$ rather than $50$, what are the mean and median?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The total is 20, so the mean is 4. The middle ordered value is still 3.
:::

## Assumptions and limits — Discovery needs a route to confirmation
An exploratory pattern can motivate a subsequent model or experiment, but a hypothesis chosen after inspecting many relationships has a different evidential status from one specified in advance. The same data can support development and diagnosis, while independent data or an explicit selection-aware procedure is useful for checking generalisation. Exploration also cannot repair a biased sample merely by improving the graph. Representativeness, missingness and dependence remain questions about how observations were obtained.{{cite:downey2014_thinkstats2}} (Ch. 1, “A statistical approach”; Ch. 9, “Replication”)

## Connections — Use several views for one question
Graphical summaries and quantitative methods can reinforce one another. A residual plot may suggest curvature, after which a fitted comparison quantifies the improvement. A robust summary may suggest a sensitivity analysis with and without a demonstrably erroneous record. The useful output is an interpretable account of structure, anomalies and limitations, together with questions that can be checked.{{cite:nist_eda2002}} (§1.1.1)

## Intuition
A single number is a projection. The mean of $(2,3,3,4,5)$ is $3.4$, the median is $3$, and the range is $3$. Those three summaries are compatible with a tight pile of integers. Replacing the last value by $50$ moves the mean to $12.4$ and the range to $48$, and it leaves the median at $3$. The mean followed the extreme value. The median stayed with the middle of the ordered list. Neither number says whether $50$ was a transcription error, a different unit, or a real observation from a heavier tail. The gain from computing both is that their disagreement forces the next question, which a lone mean of $12.4$ would have buried inside one average.

Resistance is the property the median is exhibiting. Moving a small fraction of the sample arbitrarily far does not move the sample median until the extreme values cross the middle of the ordered sample. The mean has no such buffer: it is the sum divided by $n$, so every unit added to one observation adds $1/n$ to the mean. Replacing $5$ by $9$ in the original list $(2,3,3,4,5)$ moves the mean from $3.4$ to $4.2$ and leaves the median at $3$. The arithmetic is the definition. The exploratory act is to notice the disagreement and then to look at the measurement, the units, and the design, rather than to delete the point because a rule said the mean and the median differ.

## Properties — Displays have tuning parameters
A histogram's shape depends on the bin width and on the alignment of the bin edges. A coarse binning can hide a second mode. A very fine binning can turn a modest sample into a collection of spikes that will not repeat. Looking at more than one width is part of the exploration. A scatterplot has a related defect when many points share coordinates: overplotting hides the count. Jitter, transparency, or a two-dimensional binning shows the multiplicity the first scatter hid. A plot against time or against collection order asks a different question, about drift and about runs of similar values. A relationship that is invisible in the marginal histogram can be obvious against time, and a trend that is invisible in a shuffled scatter can be obvious once the time order is restored.{{cite:downey2014_thinkstats2}} (Ch. 2, histograms and outliers; Ch. 7, relationships between variables)

Splitting the sample by a factor that the design actually recorded, such as a site or a treatment, can reveal two regressions whose mixture looked like a cloud. Splitting by every available label until a pattern appears creates patterns that the next sample will not share. The record of which splits were examined is part of the analysis. A later confirmation sample, or a model specified from the first sample and scored on data that did not choose it, is what turns the discovered pattern into a checkable claim.{{cite:downey2014_thinkstats2}} (Ch. 1, “A statistical approach”; Ch. 9, “Replication”)

## Common misconceptions
Exploration is sometimes treated as a licence to draw a causal conclusion from a striking plot, and sometimes treated as a stage that serious analysis skips on the way to a regression table. A plot is evidence about the measurements that were plotted. A causal claim needs a design or a set of assumptions about how the treatments were assigned. The plot can show that the association is curved, that one site dominates the fit, or that the outcome is a mixture. Those facts constrain the regression. They do not by themselves name a cause.

Automatic deletion of points beyond a multiple of the interquartile range is a similar shortcut. The rule can flag the value $50$ in the example. Flagging is a reason to inspect the data record. Deletion is a change of the target population. If $50$ is the observation of scientific interest, deleting it estimates the mean of a different distribution from the one that was sampled. A sensitivity analysis that reports the mean with the point, without the point, and with a Winsorised version makes the dependence visible. A single cleaned mean hides it.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 192, resistance and re-expression)

### Connections
Tukey's program of exploratory data analysis assembled these habits: resistant summaries, re-expression, residual displays, and an insistence on looking before choosing a narrow model. Residual analysis in a regression is the same habit applied to $y-\widehat y$ rather than to $y$. A curved residual plot is an exploratory finding about the conditional mean. A formal comparison of a linear fit with a quadratic fit is one way to quantify it. Robustness studies the behaviour of the summaries under contamination. Exploratory analysis uses that behaviour as a diagnostic, comparing a resistant summary with a sensitive one on the sample in hand. The two subjects share examples and diverge in their claims. One claims a property of a procedure over a neighbourhood of distributions. The other claims a description of this sample and a list of questions the description raises.{{cite:nist_eda2002}} (§1.1.1)
