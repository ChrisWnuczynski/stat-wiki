## Motivating example
A researcher measures training hours and completion time for each runner. A single correlation says how strongly a *straight-line* pattern runs through the pairs, but the individual points might instead trace a curve or split into two groups. Plotting each runner's two measurements first shows which summary is worth computing and whether an unusual runner needs investigation.{{cite:nist_scatterplot2002}} (§1.3.3.26, Purpose and Questions)

## Intuition
A scatterplot retains the pairing: one mark represents one unit with two measurements. Its horizontal position records one variable and its vertical position the other. As the eye moves across the plot, it can see direction, curvature, changing vertical spread, clusters, empty regions and isolated points. A number such as correlation deliberately compresses this information into the strength and direction of a *linear* relationship. The plot and the number therefore answer related, but different, questions.{{cite:nist_scatterplot2002}} (§1.3.3.26, Questions and Examples){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 475, Scatterplot)

## Definition
For paired observations $(x_i,y_i)$ on units $i=1,\ldots,n$, draw the point $(x_i,y_i)$ in a common coordinate system for every unit. Usually $x$ is on the horizontal axis and $y$ on the vertical axis. When $y$ is a response and $x$ an explanatory variable, this convention makes the response's behavior across $x$ easy to read; it does not make $x$ a cause. Both axes should name their variable and its unit, and the plot should retain a common scale when panels are compared. The mathematical object is the set or collection of paired coordinates, not a fitted line through them.{{cite:nist_scatterplot2002}} (§1.3.3.26, Definition and Causality Is Not Proved By Association){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 475, Mathematical Aspects)

If several units share the same pair of recorded values, their marks coincide. A conventional point plot then hides multiplicity: a coordinate with many observations may look like a coordinate with one. Transparency, point size proportional to count, or small *display-only* jitter can reveal the pile. The jittered positions are not new measured values and should not be used to compute a correlation.{{cite:nist_scatterplot2002}} (§1.3.3.26, plot of observed pairs; display convention)

## Interpretation — Read structure before a summary
First identify the unit represented by a point and the ranges on the axes. Then ask whether the cloud rises, falls, bends or stays diffuse; whether its vertical spread changes with $x$; whether separate groups need different descriptions; and whether isolated observations are recording errors, rare valid cases or a sign of another process. A straight cloud supports a linear summary, whereas a curve asks for a nonlinear account. A funnel of increasing spread suggests that constant-error-variance assumptions deserve scrutiny. An isolated point can alter a fitted line, but removal needs a reason grounded in the data collection.{{cite:nist_scatterplot2002}} (§1.3.3.26, Questions; §§1.3.3.26.5 and 1.3.3.26.10){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 475, Domains and Limitations)

When the point cloud is dense, use a display that preserves density rather than interpreting blank-looking overlap as absence of data. When groups are meaningful, color or facet by group so a pooled trend is not mistaken for a within-group trend. A scatterplot matrix lays out pairwise plots for several variables and helps identify which pair or group warrants closer study. In every case the plot is a description of the recorded pairs; sampling, design and subject knowledge are needed before generalizing or making a causal claim.{{cite:nist_scatterplot2002}} (§1.3.3.26, Combining Scatter Plots and Causality; §1.3.3.26.11, Questions)

## Properties — What correlation can conceal
Take $x=-2,-1,0,1,2$ and $y=4,1,0,1,4$. Every point lies exactly on $y=x^2$, yet the sample correlation is zero. The mean of $x$ is $0$, the mean of $y$ is $2$, and the centered cross-products sum to $-4+1+0-1+4=0$. Correlation correctly records no *linear* association; the scatterplot shows a perfect nonlinear one. A zero correlation is therefore not a statement of independence. This example also shows why testing a linear slope without viewing the points can miss a strong relationship.{{cite:nist_scatterplot2002}} (§1.3.3.26, nonlinear relationship; §1.3.3.26.5, quadratic relationship)

An observation far to the right of all other $x$ values can pull a least-squares line toward itself. Its effect depends on its vertical position as well as its distance along the horizontal axis. A point far from the line at an ordinary $x$ may have a large residual without the same leverage. The graph identifies candidates for closer analysis; influence calculations and record checking determine what the points mean.{{cite:nist_scatterplot2002}} (§1.3.3.26.10, outlier example)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
For the five symmetric $x$ values above, $\bar x=0$ and $\bar y=(4+1+0+1+4)/5=2$. The centered product sum is
$\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)=(-2)(2)+(-1)(-1)+0(-2)+1(-1)+2(2)=0$.
The centered squared sums are $\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2=10$ and $\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2=14$, both positive. Thus the sample correlation is $0/\sqrt{10\cdot14}=0$. The equality $y_i=x_i^2$ holds for every point, so zero correlation and an exact nonlinear relationship coexist. The same cancellation holds whenever symmetric $x$ values are paired with an even function of $x$ and both centered variances are positive.
:::
:::

## Statistical use — A first look before modeling
Start with the raw pairs, not the fitted values. If the cloud is roughly straight with comparable spread at each $x$, a linear regression may be a useful first model. If it bends, consider a transformation or a curved mean function; if spread changes, reconsider the error model. After fitting, a scatterplot of residuals against fitted values or a predictor can reveal structure left behind. Dodge specifically notes this role in checking regression models. A plot is a diagnostic invitation, not an automatic rule to add a term or delete a point.{{cite:nist_scatterplot2002}} (§1.3.3.26, Purpose and Questions){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 475, Domains and Limitations)

The fish-species exercise linked below uses two scatterplots of counts against geographical covariates. Comparing their shapes and spreads is more informative than assuming that both covariates should enter a Poisson regression in the same way. If variables are recorded on a coarse grid, show repeated pairs clearly before treating their apparent density as a biological pattern.{{cite:nist_scatterplot2002}} (§1.3.3.26, interpretation questions)

## Worked example
Suppose five paired measurements are $(1,3),(2,5),(3,7),(4,9),(5,11)$. They lie exactly on the rising line $y=2x+1$; $\bar x=3$ and $\bar y=7$. Their centered cross-product sum is $20$, while the two centered squared sums are $10$ and $40$, so $r=20/\sqrt{10\cdot40}=1$. The picture and the correlation tell the same linear story here. If the last response is recorded as $21$ instead, the plot shows one high point at the largest $x$; the mean response becomes $9$, the cross-product sum $40$, and the response squared sum $200$, giving $r=40/\sqrt{10\cdot200}\approx0.8944$. Even a still-strong correlation conceals the particular shape and the influential observation that produced it.{{cite:nist_scatterplot2002}} (§1.3.3.26, linear relationship and outlier examples)

**Your turn.** Plot $(1,1),(2,4),(3,9),(4,16),(5,25)$ mentally or on paper. Is a straight-line summary sufficient? Find the mean of each variable, and explain what you would examine before fitting a linear regression.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The means are $\bar x=3$ and $\bar y=11$. The points lie exactly on $y=x^2$, so the pattern rises but bends; a single straight line does not describe the rule. Inspect the curvature and residuals from any proposed line, then consider a quadratic mean model if that is scientifically sensible. The monotone rise can still yield a high positive correlation, but correlation alone cannot tell that the rule is curved.{{cite:nist_scatterplot2002}} (§1.3.3.26.5, quadratic relationship)
:::

## Common misconceptions
A steep-looking cloud does not automatically imply a strong correlation: rescaling axes can change the visual steepness without changing the correlation. A strong correlation does not imply causation; an unmeasured variable or the sampling design may explain the pattern. Zero correlation need not mean no relationship, as the exact parabola shows. An isolated point is also not necessarily an error to discard: it may be a genuine rare observation. These are distinct questions about scaling, causal interpretation, functional form and data provenance.{{cite:nist_scatterplot2002}} (§1.3.3.26, Questions and Causality; §§1.3.3.26.5 and 1.3.3.26.10)

## Assumptions and limits — When the picture misleads
If each variable is rounded to whole units, dozens of identical pairs may cover one another; a plain scatterplot understates how many observations are there. If two populations have different baselines, pooling their points can create a trend absent within either group. If an extreme $x$ value is a data-entry error, leaving it unexamined can distort a line; if it is real, deleting it solely because it looks inconvenient distorts the evidence instead. A very large sample can turn the panel into a solid mass, calling for transparency or binned summaries. Plot design and data context determine which of these problems matters.{{cite:nist_scatterplot2002}} (§1.3.3.26, display and interpretation questions; §1.3.3.26.11, grouping)

## History
The scatterplot has become a standard first step in regression analysis because it puts the paired observations in view before a model compresses them. Dodge's 2008 encyclopedia describes it as a way to judge a relationship before choosing a model and as a tool for residual analysis. The NIST/SEMATECH handbook likewise places it among exploratory graphical techniques and illustrates linear, curved and outlying patterns rather than treating a correlation as the whole description.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 475, Scatterplot){{cite:nist_scatterplot2002}} (§1.3.3.26, Purpose and Examples)
