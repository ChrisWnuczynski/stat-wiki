## Lead context
Risk assessment, in the statistical settings collected here, estimates the probability of a harmful or costly event and puts that probability next to the comparison that gives it meaning. In epidemiology the event is disease in a population over an interval of time. On a control chart the event is a false search for an assignable cause, or a missed shift. The probability is not a complete assessment by itself. A rare disease with a large relative risk and a common disease with a small relative risk can demand different actions, and a chart limit that almost never false-alarms can miss a shift for a long time. Assessment is the probability together with the denominator, the comparison, and the decision the probability is being asked to support.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 461){{cite:nist_pmc31_2002}} (§6.3.1)

## Intuition
Two numbers describe an exposure. Suppose the risk of a disease is $0.02$ without the exposure and $0.04$ with it. The relative risk is $0.04/0.02=2$: the exposed have twice the risk. The absolute difference is $0.02$: two extra cases in every hundred people. Both numbers come from the same pair of risks. A sentence that says only "twice the risk" has dropped the two cases. A sentence that says only "two extra cases" has dropped the doubling. Risk assessment keeps both, because a doubling of a tiny risk and a doubling of a large risk are different burdens, and the same doubling can be either.

A control chart is the same idea in a factory. A point outside a limit starts a search. If the process is stable, the search is a false alarm, and the limit is a choice of how often that false alarm is tolerated. A limit with probability $0.001$ in each tail tolerates a rare false alarm and waits longer to notice a small shift. The probability is the risk of the search. It is not the risk that the product is defective.

## Definition
In epidemiology, Dodge defines risk as the probability that a disease appears in a population, or in a homogeneous subgroup, during a given time interval. Homogeneous here means that the individuals are exposed to a factor suspected of causing the disease under study. Incidence is the frequency of the disease. Risk is the probability. A frequency in a finite record estimates the probability. The estimate and the probability are different objects, and a short record of a rare disease estimates the probability badly.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 461)

The comparisons built from two risks are the absolute effect and the relative effect. The absolute effect is the risk in the exposed minus the risk in the unexposed. It is the excess risk, the extra cases associated with the exposure. The relative effect is the ratio of the two risks. It is the strength of the association, reported as a relative risk or through an odds ratio. Dodge's statement of the limit is that the strength of an association is not enough to establish a cause. The supposed cause has to precede the effect, and other conditions have to be considered. A risk ratio is an assessment of association. A causal assessment adds those conditions.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 62)

On a control chart the risk is a property of the limit, not of a disease. The chart plots a quality characteristic against time, with a centre line and an upper and a lower limit. The limits are chosen so that, while only chance causes act, almost all points fall inside. If the limits are $0.001$ probability limits, the probability of a point above the upper limit is one in a thousand, and the probability of a point below the lower limit is one in a thousand, when the process is in control. Crossing a limit starts a search for an assignable cause. The probability of that search, when the cause is absent, is the false-alarm risk the limit encodes.{{cite:nist_pmc31_2002}} (§6.3.1)

Public-health risk and industrial risk share the structure and not the event. Both are probabilities of a bad outcome under a stated model. Both are changed by moving a definition: the population at risk, the time interval, the placement of a limit. Neither is a statement that the bad outcome will happen to a named person or a named item on the next draw.

## Statistical use
An epidemiological assessment reports the risks, the absolute difference, and the ratio, and it says which population and which interval. The two-region mortality table is the cautionary case for the population. Region A has $500$ deaths in $10\,000$ people, a crude rate of $5\%$. Region B has $1\,000$ deaths in $10\,000$, a crude rate of $10\%$. Age-specific rates are $5\%$ under $60$ and $15\%$ over $60$ in both regions. Region B is older. The crude relative risk of B against A is $2$, and the age-specific relative risks are $1$. An assessment that quotes only the crude ratio has assessed the risk of a randomly drawn resident, mixing age into the comparison. An assessment that quotes the age-specific ratios has assessed something else. Both can be correct. They are not interchangeable.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 182, 461)

Cornfield's 1951 statement of risk came from a follow-up comparison of a group of sick people and a group of healthy people. Since then the term has been used as a measure of association between a disease and its risk factors. Using it as a measure of association is appropriate when that is the question. Using it as a measure of the effect of changing the factor requires the temporal order and the rest of the causal conditions. OpenIntro's observational-study warning is the same limit in different language. Sunscreen use can be positively associated with skin cancer because sun exposure drives both, even if sunscreen reduces cancer risk. The relative risk of cancer by sunscreen use would then be greater than $1$ and would be the wrong sign for the effect of sunscreen. A risk assessment that stops at the ratio has not finished the scientific question the ratio is being asked to serve.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 62, 461){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.4)

A chart assessment reports the false-alarm probability the limits imply and the kind of shift the limits are likely to miss. Tightening the limits lowers the size of a shift that raises an alarm and raises the probability of a search when the process is stable. The NIST account treats that placement as the determinant of the risk of a needless search. A quality system that copies the limits from a specification, rather than from the stable process, assesses a different event: the event that a point falls outside the customer's tolerance, which is not the event that the process has left its stable state.{{cite:nist_pmc31_2002}} (§6.3.1)

Sample size is part of the assessment. A significant difference between epidemiological populations usually requires large samples, because the rates are often small and the comparisons are noisy. A relative risk of $2$ estimated from a handful of cases is a different assessment from a relative risk of $2$ estimated from thousands. The point estimate can match. The uncertainty does not. Reporting the ratio without the counts invites a reader to treat those assessments as the same.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 62)

## Assumptions and limits
The probability is conditional on the model that defines the event and the population. If the subgroup is not homogeneous in the exposure, the single risk is an average across different risks, and the average can match none of the people in the subgroup. If the disease was counted differently among the exposed and the unexposed, the ratio mixes the counting rule with the exposure. If the chart's data were already full of assignable causes when the limits were estimated, the false-alarm probability is not the probability computed from a stable model.

Temporality is a hard limit on causal use. A risk that is higher after the disease has begun does not establish that the correlate caused the disease. Dodge puts this criterion first among the conditions that an association still needs. OpenIntro's confounding example is the case where a third variable, sun exposure, produces the association. Neither source treats a large ratio as a substitute for those conditions.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 62){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.4)

A false-alarm probability is not a statement about the next point in isolation from the rule. If many charts are watched, or many rules are applied to one chart, the probability of some alarm is larger than the probability of one named limit being crossed. The $0.001$ figure is the probability for one limit on one side under the model. An assessment that quotes it as the probability that "the process will signal today," across a wall of charts, has changed the event.{{cite:nist_pmc31_2002}} (§6.3.1)

## History
The epidemiological notion of risk, in Dodge's account, was stated clearly by Cornfield in 1951, from a follow-up study that compared risk factors in a sick group and a healthy group. The broader study of how illness is distributed is older: Graunt on causes of death in 1662, Farr on death by age, sex, season, place, and profession, Snow on cholera and contaminated water. Those studies were already risk assessments in the sense that they compared frequencies across groups. The word risk, tied to a probability over an interval, is the later sharpening.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 181, 461)

The industrial version is the control chart. Shewhart's 16 May 1924 memorandum sketched the chart, and the handbook's account of why the chart works is the probability calculation: limits placed so that a stable process rarely produces a point outside them, and a point outside them starts a search. The history of quality control as craft is much older. The history of assessing the risk of a false search is the history of choosing those limits from a probability model of the stable process.{{cite:nist_pmc11_2002}} (§6.1.1){{cite:nist_pmc31_2002}} (§6.3.1)
