## Motivating example — Information from two yes-or-no measurements
Suppose an instrument records whether a reading exceeded a threshold, while a second indicator records whether a warning lamp was on. If only those two indicators are available, outcomes with the same indicator pair cannot be distinguished. Events that can be answered from the recorded information are unions of the corresponding groups of outcomes. Generated sigma-algebras formalise this information content, with complements and countable unions ensuring that ordinary logical combinations remain events.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.1, probability spaces and generated sigma-fields)

## Definition — Intersect every admissible event family
For a family $\mathcal C$ of subsets of $\Omega$, define $\sigma(\mathcal C)$ as the intersection of all sigma-algebras on $\Omega$ containing $\mathcal C$. At least one such sigma-algebra exists: the power set $2^\Omega$. The intersection contains $\Omega$, is closed under complements, and is closed under countable unions because each family in the intersection has those properties. It is therefore a sigma-algebra, contains $\mathcal C$, and is contained in every sigma-algebra that contains $\mathcal C$. These three statements establish the required smallest-family property without an iterative stopping rule.{{cite:tao2011_measure}} (§1.4, def. 1.4.15)

On a topological space, the Borel sigma-algebra is generated by its open sets. On $\mathbb R$ the same family is generated by open intervals, by open intervals with rational endpoints, or by the half-lines $(-\infty,a]$. Different generator lists can therefore describe exactly the same measurable information. Equality of generated sigma-algebras follows by showing that each set of generators is measurable for the other family.{{cite:tao2011_measure}} (§1.4, def. 1.4.16 and ex. 1.4.14)

## Properties — Atoms give a finite calculation
For finitely many generators $A_1,\ldots,A_k$, take all nonempty intersections formed by choosing either $A_j$ or $A_j^c$ for each index. These intersections partition $\Omega$. Every generator is a union of these atoms, and unions of atoms form a sigma-algebra. Conversely, complements and intersections put every atom in $\sigma(A_1,\ldots,A_k)$, and finite unions then put every union of atoms there. The generated family is exactly the collection of unions of atoms. If there are $m$ nonempty atoms, it has $2^m$ events, with $m\le2^k$.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.1, generated sigma-fields and finite discrete spaces)

This description also explains why adding a redundant generator has no effect. If $B\in\sigma(\mathcal C)$, then $\sigma(\mathcal C\cup\{B\})=\sigma(\mathcal C)$: each inclusion follows from the smallest-family property. Adding a set that splits an atom genuinely increases the information, because it separates outcomes that could not be distinguished before. The size of a generator list alone is therefore a poor measure of the size of its generated sigma-algebra.

## Interpretation — Why a countable interval basis is enough
Every open subset $U$ of $\mathbb R$ is the union of all rational-endpoint intervals contained in $U$. For each $x\in U$, openness gives a small interval around $x$, and density of the rationals gives rational endpoints lying within that neighbourhood on either side of $x$. There are only countably many rational pairs, so this is a countable union. Thus rational open intervals generate all open sets and hence all Borel sets. Conversely, each rational interval is open, giving the other inclusion.{{cite:tao2011_measure}} (§1.4, ex. 1.4.14)

Closed intervals are Borel by complementation and intersection, singletons are countable intersections of shrinking open intervals, and countable sets are countable unions of singletons. Closure under countable unions is essential; it does not license an arbitrary uncountable union. A Borel sigma-algebra is generally smaller than the power set. Completing a probability measure can add subsets of null sets, so a completed event family can be larger than the Borel family while describing the same probabilities for Borel events.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.1, Borel sets and completions)

## Worked example — A sensor pair with one impossible combination
Let $\Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$, $A=\{1,2,3\}$ and $B=\{3,4\}$. The four atoms are $\{3\}$, $\{1,2\}$, $\{4\}$ and $\{5,6\}$. All are nonempty, so the generated family has $2^4=16$ events. The event $\{1\}$ is absent: outcomes one and two have exactly the same pair of indicators. If only $A$ were recorded, the atoms would be $A$ and $A^c$, giving four events. Observing $B$ increases the event family without revealing every elementary outcome.

**Your turn.** Replace $B$ by $B'=\{1,2\}$. How many events are generated by $A$ and $B'$?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The nonempty atoms are $\{1,2\}$, $\{3\}$ and $\{4,5,6\}$. There are $2^3=8$ unions. The atom $A^c\cap B'$ is empty and contributes no independent choice.
:::

## Statistical use — Measurability checks through generators
To show that a real-valued map $X$ is measurable, it suffices to check $\{X\le a\}$ for all real $a$, or just for rational $a$. Indeed, the subsets $B\subseteq\mathbb R$ whose inverse images lie in $\mathcal F$ form a sigma-algebra, since inverse images preserve complements and countable unions. Once that family contains generators for the Borel sets, it contains every Borel set. The same argument underlies $\sigma(X)$, the information obtainable from a random variable: $\sigma(X)=\{X^{-1}(B):B\in\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\}$. The construction concerns observable events, rather than assigning a probability to every conceivable subset.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.3, definition of a random variable and generated sigma-fields)
