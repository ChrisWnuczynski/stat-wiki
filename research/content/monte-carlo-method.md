## Motivating example
A posterior probability may be an integral that has no convenient closed form. If a sampler can draw parameter values from the posterior, one can record whether each draw satisfies the event and average those zero-or-one results. The proportion of successes approximates the desired probability. The same idea estimates an expected cost, a predictive quantity or an area under a curve: simulation turns an integral into an average. The price is simulation error, which can be measured separately from uncertainty in the data.{{cite:dasgupta2011_graduate_probability}} (§1.29.10, pp. 180–182, Monte Carlo estimation)

## Intuition
Suppose the target is $I=\mathbb E_p[h(X)]$. An independent draw $X$ from $p$ gives a noisy value $h(X)$ whose mean is $I$. Averaging many such values cancels positive and negative fluctuations. For probability calculations $h$ can be an indicator, so the average is simply the fraction of simulated outcomes in the event. For integration over $[0,1]$, taking $X\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1)$ makes $I=\int_0^1h(x)\,dx$. The integrand can be complicated even when sampling $X$ is easy. This is *ordinary* Monte Carlo; a Markov chain is one way, but not the only way, to produce usable draws from a difficult target.{{cite:dasgupta2011_graduate_probability}} (§1.29.10, pp. 180–182, Monte Carlo estimation){{cite:hastings1970}} (§1, p. 97, simulation for integration)

## Definition
For independent $X_1,\ldots,X_N\sim p$, the Monte Carlo estimator is
$$
\widehat I_N=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N h(X_i),\qquad I=\int h(x)p(x)\,dx.
$$
Here $N$ is the *simulation* sample size, not the size of the observed dataset. If the draws come from a posterior, $p$ is that posterior; if they are uniform points for numerical integration, $p$ is the uniform density. The target distribution and the function $h$ must be specified before interpreting the average.{{cite:dasgupta2011_graduate_probability}} (§1.29.10, pp. 180–182, Monte Carlo estimation)

## Interpretation
With independent draws and finite variance $\sigma_h^2=\operatorname{Var}_p\{h(X)\}$, the standard deviation of the estimator is $\sigma_h/\sqrt N$. The sample standard deviation of the simulated $h(X_i)$ values estimates $\sigma_h$, giving a Monte Carlo standard error. A confidence interval around a Monte Carlo estimate describes numerical uncertainty about the integral, conditional on the stated target distribution. It is not a credible or confidence interval for an unknown model parameter. Doubling $N$ reduces this standard error by only a factor of $\sqrt2$; a tenfold reduction takes about a hundred times as many draws at fixed variance.{{cite:dasgupta2011_graduate_probability}} (§1.29.10, pp. 180–182, Monte Carlo estimation)

## Properties
**Unbiasedness and variance for independent draws.** If $h(X)$ is integrable, $\mathbb E(\widehat I_N)=I$. If its variance is finite, $\operatorname{Var}(\widehat I_N)=\sigma_h^2/N$. The law of large numbers then sends the average toward $I$; the central limit theorem gives an approximately normal numerical error at large $N$. The $N^{-1/2}$ rate is a statement about random error for this estimator, not a promise that every realised estimate improves monotonically as $N$ grows.{{cite:dasgupta2011_graduate_probability}} (§1.29.10, pp. 180–182, Monte Carlo estimation)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
Linearity gives $\mathbb E(\widehat I_N)=N^{-1}\sum_i\mathbb E\{h(X_i)\}=I$. Independence makes the cross-covariances zero, so $\operatorname{Var}(\widehat I_N)=N^{-2}\sum_i\operatorname{Var}\{h(X_i)\}=\sigma_h^2/N$. Thus its root mean squared error is $\sigma_h/\sqrt N$. The usual law of large numbers and iid central limit theorem apply to the summands when their respective moment assumptions hold.{{cite:dasgupta2011_graduate_probability}} (§1.29.10, pp. 180–182, Monte Carlo estimation)
:::

The variance formula also suggests how to improve a computation. More draws help, but a better sampling distribution or a variance-reduction construction can lower $\sigma_h^2$ itself. In importance sampling, draws come from a proposal $q$ and the average uses $h(X)p(X)/q(X)$, provided $q>0$ wherever the integrand contributes. The weights correct for the changed sampling distribution; omitting them generally changes the target. Variance reduction must be checked against its assumptions, because extreme weights can produce an unstable estimate.{{cite:hastings1970}} (§1, p. 97, importance sampling)

## Statistical use — Estimating posterior quantities
A posterior expectation is $\mathbb E\{h(\Theta)\mid y\}$. Draws from the posterior turn it into the average of $h(\Theta^{(i)})$; an indicator estimates a posterior event probability. The same estimator can approximate a predictive probability by drawing future data conditional on sampled parameters. Independent draws are especially simple because their usual sample variance estimates numerical precision. Markov-chain draws are dependent, and their standard error must account for autocorrelation rather than treating a long trace as the same number of independent observations.{{cite:hastings1970}} (§§1–2.1, pp. 97–99, dependent simulation)

For an ordinary integral, a uniform sampler is a convenient starting point. If $X\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1)$, then $\mathbb E(X^2)=\int_0^1x^2\,dx=1/3$. This known answer is useful for checking the arithmetic of a small simulation, although a real Monte Carlo calculation is most useful when the answer is not already available by elementary calculus. In high dimensions, a regular grid may require exponentially many points as the number of coordinates grows; Monte Carlo's characteristic $N^{-1/2}$ random-error order does not itself change with dimension, though the variance can.{{cite:dasgupta2011_graduate_probability}} (§1.29.10, pp. 180–182, Monte Carlo rate){{cite:hastings1970}} (§1, p. 97, high-dimensional integration)

## Worked example
Take five independent uniform draws, $0.2,0.7,0.9,0.4,0.6$, and estimate $I=\int_0^1x^2\,dx$. Their squared values are $0.04,0.49,0.81,0.16,0.36$, which sum to $1.86$; hence $\widehat I_5=1.86/5=0.372$. The exact integral is $1/3\approx0.3333$, so the observed simulation error is about $0.0387$. To assess numerical error, compute the sample variance of those five squared values. Their squared deviations from $0.372$ sum to $0.36108$, giving $s_h^2=0.36108/4=0.09027$ and an estimated Monte Carlo standard error $s_h/\sqrt5\approx0.13437$. With only five draws this estimated standard error is itself unstable; the point of the example is to expose the calculation.{{cite:dasgupta2011_graduate_probability}} (§1.29.10, pp. 180–182, Monte Carlo estimation)

**Your turn.** Use four independent uniform draws $0.1,0.3,0.7,0.9$ to estimate $\int_0^1x^2\,dx$. Compute the estimate and its realised error relative to $1/3$. Explain why the four-draw result does not establish that the method is biased.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The squared values are $0.01,0.09,0.49,0.81$, summing to $1.40$. Thus $\widehat I_4=1.40/4=0.35$ and its realised error is $0.35-1/3\approx0.0167$. Unbiasedness concerns the expectation across repeated independent sets of draws; one particular set can lie above or below the target.{{cite:dasgupta2011_graduate_probability}} (§1.29.10, pp. 180–182, Monte Carlo estimation)
:::

## Common misconceptions
Monte Carlo error is not posterior uncertainty: the former would shrink if the same calculation used more simulated draws, whereas the latter reflects the posterior distribution set by data and model. Monte Carlo does not require a Markov chain; independent simulation is the elementary case. Nor does a low estimated standard error certify a correct answer if the sampler targets the wrong distribution or the model is misspecified. Finally, $N^{-1/2}$ describes a standard-error scale, not a deterministic upper bound on each realised error.{{cite:hastings1970}} (§§1–2.1, pp. 97–99, dependent simulation)

## Assumptions and limits — Infinite variance and poor mixing
For $h(X)=X^{-3/4}$ with $X\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1)$, the expectation is finite but the second moment diverges. The usual finite-variance standard error and normal approximation do not apply, even though the law of large numbers still gives convergence. If a posterior sampler remains trapped in one of two separated modes, increasing the number of recorded iterations in that one mode does not repair the missing mass. If $q$ is zero where $h(x)p(x)$ is nonzero, importance weights cannot recover the omitted region. These failures arise from different sources: a heavy-tailed integrand, poor chain exploration, and invalid proposal support.{{cite:hastings1970}} (§§1–2.1, pp. 97–99, dependent simulation)

## Connections
The [law of large numbers](law-of-large-numbers.qmd) explains convergence of independent averages, while the [central limit theorem](central-limit-theorem.qmd) gives the usual error approximation. Importance sampling changes where draws are taken and compensates with weights. [Markov-chain Monte Carlo](markov-chain.qmd) supplies dependent draws when direct simulation is difficult, and [effective sample size](effective-sample-size.qmd) summarizes part of the resulting loss of precision. A [random seed](random-seed.qmd) can reproduce a calculation but cannot certify its target or convergence.{{cite:hastings1970}} (§§1–2.1, pp. 97–99, dependent simulation)

## History
Metropolis and Ulam's 1949 paper placed the named Monte Carlo method in a statistical journal in the context of computation by random sampling. Subsequent Markov-chain algorithms provided a way to sample complicated distributions; by 1990, Gelfand and Smith were presenting sampling-based methods for Bayesian marginal distributions. These developments broadened the range of integrals that could be approximated, but the basic estimator remains an average of simulated quantities.{{cite:metropolis_ulam1949}} (pp. 335–341, original Monte Carlo method){{cite:gelfand_smith1990}} (§1, pp. 398–399)
