## Lead context
A sigma-algebra is the family of events that stays closed when one takes complements and countable unions, so limits of events are still events.

## Motivating example
A single coin has four events: nothing, heads, tails, and the whole toss. An infinite sequence of tosses needs events such as “the proportion of heads settles at one half”, and that event is a countable combination of statements about finitely many coordinates. The family of events has to be large enough to contain those combinations.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.1, pp. 1–2)

## Intuition — Close the family under the operations you will use
Start with the outcomes you can name, such as one set $A$. A probability argument will also mention the complement, and it will mention unions of sequences of events. If each of those operations can leave the family, the probability is no longer defined. Durrett therefore takes a sigma-field to be a nonempty collection of subsets that contains the complement of each of its members and the union of every countable subcollection. The whole sample space is then included, because it is the union of a set and its complement, and countable intersections are included by taking complements of unions.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.1, pp. 1–2)

## Definition — Generated families and a finite example
If $\mathcal C$ is a collection of subsets of $\Omega$, the generated sigma-algebra $\sigma(\mathcal C)$ is the smallest sigma-algebra containing $\mathcal C$. On a finite $\Omega$ it is the set of all unions of the atoms formed by intersecting every generator or its complement.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.1, pp. 1–2)

Take $\Omega=\{1,2,3\}$ and $\mathcal C=\{\{1\}\}$. The four sets
$$
\emptyset,\quad \{1\},\quad \{2,3\},\quad \Omega
$$
form a sigma-algebra, and no smaller family contains $\{1\}$. The singleton $\{2\}$ is not one of those four sets, so it is not an event in $\sigma(\mathcal C)$.

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
The displayed family contains $\{1\}$, and it is closed under complements and under unions. Any sigma-algebra that contains $\{1\}$ contains $\{1\}^c=\{2,3\}$, contains $\emptyset=\{1\}\cap\{1\}^c$, and contains $\Omega$. So the family is exactly $\sigma(\{\{1\}\})$.

Durrett’s closure under countable intersections is the complement form of closure under unions: $\bigcap_i A_i=\bigl(\bigcup_i A_i^c\bigr)^c$.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.1, p. 1)
:::
:::

## Worked example
Assign $\mathbb{P}(\{1\})=1/3$ and $\mathbb{P}(\{2,3\})=2/3$, with $\mathbb{P}(\emptyset)=0$ and $\mathbb{P}(\Omega)=1$. This is a probability on $\sigma(\{\{1\}\})$. It does not assign a probability to $\{2\}$ alone, because $\{2\}$ is not in the sigma-algebra. Splitting $\{2,3\}$ into $\{2\}$ and $\{3\}$ would be a different, finer family.

**Your turn.** On $\Omega=\{1,2,3,4\}$, let $A=\{1,2\}$ and $B=\{1\}$. List the nonempty atoms of $\sigma(A,B)$ and say how many sets the sigma-algebra contains.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The atoms are $A\cap B=\{1\}$, $A\cap B^c=\{2\}$, and $A^c\cap B^c=\{3,4\}$. The intersection $A^c\cap B$ is empty. Three nonempty atoms give $2^3=8$ sets.
:::

## Connections — Random variables and countable additivity
A [random variable](random-variable.qmd) is a function whose inverse images of Borel sets lie in $\mathcal F$. That requirement is empty unless $\mathcal F$ is a sigma-algebra. Countable additivity, continuity along monotone sequences, and the [Borel–Cantelli lemmas](borel-cantelli-lemmas.qmd) are statements about events in $\mathcal F$.{{cite:durrett2019}} (Thm. 1.1.1, pp. 1–2) The [probability space](probability-space.qmd) is the triple $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mathbb{P})$.

## Check yourself

### The algebra generated by two sets {#self-check-ex-sp-generated-algebra}

Let $\Omega=\{1,2,3,4\}$, $A=\{1,2\}$, and $B=\{2,3\}$. List $\sigma(A,B)$.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The atoms are $\{2\}$, $\{1\}$, $\{3\}$, and $\{4\}$. Every singleton is an atom, so $\sigma(A,B)$ is the power set, with $2^4=16$ sets.
:::

### Continuity from above {#self-check-ex-sp-continuity-above}

If $A_n\downarrow A$, why does $\mathbb{P}(A_n)\downarrow\mathbb{P}(A)$?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The complements satisfy $A_n^c\uparrow A^c$. Continuity from below gives $\mathbb{P}(A_n^c)\uparrow\mathbb{P}(A^c)$. Subtract from $1$ to obtain $\mathbb{P}(A_n)\downarrow\mathbb{P}(A)$. The complements are events because $\mathcal F$ is a sigma-algebra., pp. 1–2)
:::
