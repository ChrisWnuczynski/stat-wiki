## Lead context
They compress a posterior distribution into a number for reporting or action, and different choices can answer different questions.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §2.3, pp. 32–34){{cite:hoff2013_bayes_estimators}} (pp. 8–10)

## Motivating example
Suppose two of ten independent trials succeed and the success probability $\theta$ began with a uniform prior. The posterior is $\operatorname{Beta}(3,9)$, with a long enough right tail that its most dense point, middle probability value, and balance point do not coincide. Reporting only one value forces a choice: the mode is $0.20$, the median is about $0.2358$, and the mean is $0.25$. A person choosing a point estimate under squared-error consequences has a reason to use the mean; someone minimizing absolute error has a reason to use the median. The posterior distribution remains the object that describes uncertainty around either number.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §§2.1, 2.3, pp. 29–34){{cite:hoff2013_bayes_estimators}} (pp. 8–11)

## Intuition
The posterior mean balances all possible parameter values using their probability as weight, so a long tail pulls it toward that tail. The median divides posterior probability into halves and is less affected by how far tail values extend. The mode marks a peak of posterior *density* on the stated parameter scale; it pays attention to the height of the curve rather than the mass in a neighborhood. For a symmetric unimodal posterior the three can coincide. For skewed or multimodal posteriors, their differences convey shape that a single estimate would hide.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §2.3, pp. 32–34)

## Definition
For a scalar parameter with proper posterior $p(\theta\mid x)$, the posterior mean is $m=\int\theta p(\theta\mid x)\,d\theta$ when $\int|\theta|p(\theta\mid x)\,d\theta<\infty$. A posterior median is any $d$ with at least half the posterior probability on each side. A maximum-a-posteriori estimate, or MAP, is a maximizer of $p(\theta\mid x)$ relative to the chosen density and parameterisation. Other posterior quantiles also supply single-number summaries, but a quantile needs its probability level stated. For a continuous posterior, a point has probability zero even at the mode, so “highest-density value” is more accurate than “most probable value.”{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §2.3, pp. 32–34){{cite:hoff2013_bayes_estimators}} (pp. 8–10)

These are descriptions until a loss function connects them to a decision. With a finite posterior second moment, the mean minimizes posterior expected squared error $\mathbb E[(\theta-d)^2\mid x]$. A median minimizes posterior expected absolute error $\mathbb E[|\theta-d|\mid x]$. A density mode is a different rule and need not minimize either loss. The loss should represent how the reported value will be used, not be chosen after looking for a preferred estimate.{{cite:hoff2013_bayes_estimators}} (p. 9, squared and absolute loss)

## Interpretation
For $\operatorname{Beta}(3,9)$, the ordering $0.20<0.2358<0.25$ reflects the posterior's rightward tail: its peak is left of its probability midpoint, and its average lies farther right. These are not competing estimates of three parameters; they are different summaries of one posterior for the same $\theta$. The squared-error question below demonstrates why the mean has its decision-theoretic role, while the MAP question warns against equating the mode with the squared-error choice. A [credible interval](bayesian-credible-interval.qmd) or a density plot should accompany a point summary when uncertainty or skewness affects interpretation.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §2.3, pp. 32–34){{cite:hoff2013_bayes_estimators}} (pp. 8–10)

## Properties
The posterior mean is equivariant under affine changes of scale: for $Z=a\theta+b$, $\mathbb E(Z\mid x)=a\mathbb E(\theta\mid x)+b$. A strictly increasing transform also maps a posterior median to a median of the transformed parameter. A nonlinear transform generally does not map the mean in the same way, and a density mode can change even after transforming it back. This is why a MAP on a probability scale need not equal the inverse-logit of a MAP on the log-odds scale.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §2.3, transformations and posterior summaries)

For independent exact posterior draws, their average estimates the mean, their middle order statistics estimate a median, and a fraction below a threshold estimates a posterior probability. The posterior-draw property states these calculations and an explicit $L=1000$ order-statistic convention. A histogram peak from finitely many draws is not automatically a stable MAP estimate: its location depends on binning or smoothing as well as the sample. For Markov-chain draws, dependence affects Monte Carlo precision even if the chain targets the correct posterior.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 1, §1.9, pp. 22–24){{cite:hoff2013_bayes_estimators}} (pp. 8–10)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Put $m=\mathbb E(\theta\mid x)$ and assume a finite posterior second moment. Expanding $\theta-d=(\theta-m)+(m-d)$ and averaging gives $\mathbb E[(\theta-d)^2\mid x]=\operatorname{Var}(\theta\mid x)+(m-d)^2$, uniquely minimized at $d=m$. For continuous CDF $F$, the derivative with respect to $d$ of $\mathbb E|\theta-d|$ is $F(d)-[1-F(d)]=2F(d)-1$ wherever the derivative exists. It changes sign at a median; with atoms, the corresponding one-sided derivatives show that any point with at least half the mass on each side minimizes absolute loss.{{cite:hoff2013_bayes_estimators}} (p. 9)

Linearity of expectation gives $\mathbb E(a\theta+b\mid x)=am+b$. If $h$ is strictly increasing and $d$ is a median, $\Pr\{h(\theta)\leq h(d)\mid x\}=\Pr(\theta\leq d\mid x)$ and likewise on the other side, proving the median rule. For a smooth one-to-one transform $z=h(\theta)$, the transformed density is $p_Z(z\mid x)=p_\theta(h^{-1}(z)\mid x)|d h^{-1}(z)/dz|$; the Jacobian can move its mode. In particular, for $\theta\sim\operatorname{Beta}(3,9)$, the mode on the probability scale is $(3-1)/(3+9-2)=0.20$. Under $z=\log\{\theta/(1-\theta)\}$, its density is proportional to $\theta^3(1-\theta)^9$, whose peak corresponds to $\theta=3/(3+9)=0.25$. Back-transforming that log-odds MAP gives $0.25$, not $0.20$.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §2.3, posterior modes and transformations)

Finally, multiplying a $\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)$ prior by the likelihood from two successes in ten trials gives the kernel $\theta^2(1-\theta)^8$, hence $\operatorname{Beta}(3,9)$. Its variance is $3\cdot9/[12^2(12+1)]=27/1872\approx0.01442$. A squared-error action at the mode $d=0.20$ has posterior risk $0.01442+(0.25-0.20)^2\approx0.01692$, greater than the risk $0.01442$ at the mean. For a Pareto tail with index $\alpha$, the $k$th-moment tail integral is proportional to $\int_1^\infty t^{k-\alpha-1}\,dt$, finite exactly when $\alpha>k$; this gives the heavy-tail counterexamples below. These calculations illustrate the loss result rather than declaring the mean universally preferable.{{cite:hoff2013_bayes_estimators}} (pp. 9–11)

## Statistical use — Choosing a posterior summary
First identify the parameter and the decision or reporting purpose. A mean is natural for squared-error decisions and many predictive averages; a median is appropriate for absolute deviations and can remain meaningful when a long tail makes a mean unstable. A mode may identify a local peak but should be labelled with its parameter scale. Report a posterior interval or other spread summary alongside any point estimate, especially when the posterior is skewed or has several peaks. In a multimodal mixture, the mean can fall in a low-density gap and misrepresent what values are individually plausible.{{cite:hoff2013_bayes_estimators}} (pp. 8–10){{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §2.3, pp. 32–34)

With simulated posterior draws, distinguish the posterior spread of $\theta$ from numerical uncertainty in the calculated mean or quantile. More draws improve Monte Carlo precision under a working sampler; they do not make the posterior distribution narrower for the same data. When the posterior is available analytically, use its formulas or numerical integration to check a simulation-based summary.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 1, §1.9, pp. 22–24)

## Worked example
With a uniform $\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)$ prior and two successes in ten conditionally independent trials, the posterior is $\operatorname{Beta}(3,9)$. Its mean is $3/12=0.25$, its interior mode is $(3-1)/(3+9-2)=2/10=0.20$, and numerical inversion of the posterior CDF gives median $0.2358$. If a chosen rate $d$ is penalized by squared error, reporting $0.25$ minimizes posterior expected loss. If it is penalized by absolute error, $0.2358$ does. If the aim is to mark the most dense point on this probability scale, report the MAP $0.20$ while making that scale explicit. The posterior standard deviation is about $0.1201$, so these nearby point estimates should not be reported as if they remove uncertainty about the rate.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §§2.1, 2.3, pp. 29–34){{cite:hoff2013_bayes_estimators}} (pp. 9–11)

**Your turn.** Keep the uniform prior but observe three successes in ten trials. Identify the Beta posterior and give its mean, median and mode to four decimal places. Which value minimizes squared-error loss, and which minimizes absolute-error loss?{{cite:hoff2013_bayes_estimators}} (pp. 9–11)

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The posterior is $\operatorname{Beta}(4,8)$. Its mean is $4/12=0.3333$, its median is approximately $0.3238$, and its mode is $(4-1)/(4+8-2)=3/10=0.3000$. The mean is the squared-error decision; the median is the absolute-error decision. The mode answers a density-peak question on the probability scale. The three differ because the posterior is skewed.
:::

## Common misconceptions
The mode is not a point with positive posterior probability in a continuous model; it maximizes density, which depends on scale. The posterior mean is not the best point under every loss function: absolute error selects a median instead. A point estimate does not describe posterior uncertainty, even when several summaries happen to agree. Finally, the spread of simulated draws is not the Monte Carlo error of a computed summary; one concerns the posterior, while the other concerns numerical approximation.{{cite:hoff2013_bayes_estimators}} (pp. 8–10){{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 1, §1.9; Ch. 2, §2.3)

## Assumptions and limits — Undefined means and unstable modes
A posterior must be proper before any of these probability summaries is defined. Even a proper heavy-tailed posterior may lack the needed moments. For a Pareto posterior with tail index $0.8$, the mean diverges; with index $1.5$, the mean exists but the second moment does not, so every finite squared-error action has infinite posterior risk. A symmetric two-component mixture concentrated near $-5$ and $5$ has mean zero even though almost no mass need lie near zero; a density plot exposes that failure of the mean as a typical-value summary. MAP has a different fragility: it changes under nonlinear reparameterisation, as the Beta-to-log-odds calculation proves. Choosing one number does not repair a misspecified prior or likelihood.{{cite:hoff2013_bayes_estimators}} (pp. 2, 8–10, existence of Bayes estimators){{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §2.3, skewed and multimodal posteriors)

## History
Gelman and colleagues trace inference about an unknown chance from observations to Bayes's 1763 essay and Laplace's later work. Their modern account lists mean, median and mode as distinct posterior summaries and recommends reporting posterior uncertainty as well. Hoff's decision-theoretic treatment, drawing on Lehmann and Casella's point-estimation framework, explains why a loss function rather than a historical naming convention determines which point estimate is appropriate for a decision.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 2, §§2.3, 2.10, pp. 32–34, 56){{cite:hoff2013_bayes_estimators}} (p. 1; pp. 8–10)
