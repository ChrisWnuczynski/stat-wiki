## Lead context

A laboratory repeats an assay. A survey organisation redraws a sample. A clinic randomises another cohort. Frequentist inference reads a statistical procedure by what would happen across such repetitions, with the unknown quantity held fixed and the data allowed to vary. The interval or test in the published report is one draw from that procedure. The reliability attached to it is a property of the procedure. {{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 1, §1.1, p. 3) {{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, §5.1)

## Intuition

Two ordinary readings of a probability sit side by side. One counts favourable cases among equally likely cases, as with a coin that is judged fair by symmetry. The other reads probability as a relative frequency in a long sequence of trials performed in the same way and physically independent of one another. Applied to inference, the trial is the whole study. Coverage of an interval, the type I error of a test, and power are frequencies of that kind. They are defined before the data arrive, as properties of a rule. The frequency reading has a built-in awkwardness: taken strictly, it does not attach a probability to a single toss that is not, at least in imagination, one of a long sequence. That awkwardness is exactly why a frequentist probability is not a probability for the parameter after these particular data have been seen. {{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 1, §1.5, pp. 11–12)

A confidence interval makes the contrast concrete. Once the sample is in, the computed interval is a fixed set of numbers. The 95 percent attached to it is the proportion of intervals, from the same method applied to repeated samples from the same population, that would cover the target. A Bayesian interval, by contrast, is offered as having a high probability of containing the unknown quantity. Users of ordinary confidence intervals often hear the second sentence when the first was the one the method earned. The two sentences are not interchangeable. {{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 1, §1.1, p. 3)

## Definition

Frequentist inference evaluates statistical methods by their operating characteristics in a sequence of repeated samples. Inferential statistics, in the sampling tradition, formulates hypotheses about population parameters and assesses them on a sample that stands in for the population. The calculations that carry out that programme — point estimates, intervals, and tests — are the procedures whose long-run success rates frequentist inference reports. Bayesian statements put probability on the parameter and on unobserved quantities such as a future observation. Frequentist statements put probability on the data-generating step. {{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 263–264) {{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 1, §1.1, p. 3)

A point estimate is frequentist when one studies its sampling distribution: where that distribution is centred, how spread out it is, and the probability that the estimate falls near the target. A [confidence interval](confidence-interval.qmd) is frequentist by the coverage calculation. A test is frequentist when its rejection region is chosen so that, if the null hypothesis is true, the probability of landing in that region equals a pre-set level, and when power is the probability of landing there if a stated alternative is true. The [p-value](p-value.qmd) is a tail probability computed inside the null sampling distribution. It is a summary of how unusual the sample is under that hypothesis. It is not a probability that the null hypothesis is true. {{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, §5.3) {{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 250–251)

The sampling distribution that justifies these summaries is a hypothetical object. In one simulation, the population proportion is 0.88 and each sample has size 1,000. One draw gave 0.894, an error of +0.014. Others gave 0.885, 0.878, and 0.859. Across 10,000 draws the centre was 0.880 and the standard deviation of the sample proportions was 0.010, which in this role is called a standard error. A smaller sample, of size 50, would be expected to have a larger standard error. The real poll contributes one number. The frequentist claim is about the cloud that number came from. {{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, §5.1.2)

## Statistical use

Report the procedure, not only the single interval or the single tail probability. For a proportion, that means the sample size, the estimate, the standard error, and the rule that turns them into an interval or a test. For a test, it means the null value, the level, the test statistic, and whether the rejection region is one-sided or two-sided. A reader who is given only “significant” has not been given the operating characteristic. {{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, §5.3) {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 251)

Large-sample frequentist calculations often coincide numerically with a Bayesian summary that uses a flat prior. When a normal approximation to the posterior holds, centred at the posterior mode with variance from the observed information, the same normal limit describes the repeated-sampling distribution of that mode if the model contains the distribution that generated the data. In that limit a 95 percent central posterior interval covers the true value in 95 percent of repeated samples. The numerical agreement does not make the two interpretations the same sentence. One is a probability for the parameter given these data. The other is a success rate of a method. Frequency calculations are also a way to check a Bayesian procedure once it is regarded as a rule embedded in repeated samples. {{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 4, §4.4, pp. 91–92)

Consistency and asymptotic unbiasedness are frequentist properties of a point estimate, whether or not the estimate was derived from a posterior. An estimate is consistent, in the sampling-theory sense, if its sampling distribution concentrates at the value it is said to estimate as the sample grows, when that value is the one that generated the data. It is asymptotically unbiased when its bias becomes negligible beside its standard deviation. The posterior mode, mean, and median have these properties, under mild conditions, when the truth is inside the family being fitted. In small samples a single point is a poor summary of a posterior that is still wide or asymmetric. The large-sample frequency properties can still be asked of whatever number is reported. {{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 4, §4.4, p. 92)

[Inference](inference.qmd) is the broader task of passing from a sample to an unobserved quantity. [Estimation theory](estimation-theory.qmd) builds the point and interval rules whose repeated-sampling behaviour is at issue. [Asymptotic analysis](asymptotic-analysis.qmd) supplies the normal approximations behind many large-sample standard errors. A [decision problem](decision-problem.qmd) can use a frequentist error rate as one ingredient and still needs a loss if the goal is to choose an action.

## Assumptions and limits

The frequency statement is conditional on the sampling story. The solar-energy simulation draws a simple random sample from a population whose proportion is fixed at 0.88. A sample that is chosen by whoever happens to be nearby, or a poll whose question pushes respondents toward one answer, does not have that sampling distribution. The standard error then describes a procedure that was not the one that was run. Bias of that kind is not removed by quoting a coverage probability. {{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, §5.1.1; Ch. 1, §1.3.3)

The asymptotic match between posterior intervals and repeated-sampling coverage assumes that the normal approximation is the right description and that the true distribution sits inside the model. Near the edge of a parameter space the normal approximation can fail, and a posterior that piles up at a boundary need not look like the sampling distribution of an interior mode. Tail probabilities can be wrong even when the centre of the approximation is acceptable, because a normal tail and the tail of the actual posterior need not agree at a finite sample size. Coverage in the limit is not coverage in the sample one has. {{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 4, §4.3–4.4, pp. 90–92)

A frequentist error rate is also silent about a single case that cannot be embedded in a sequence of comparable trials. The probability that a specified rocket launch fails can be given a frequency reading only by building a reference set of comparable launches. Without that set, the long-run sentence has no sequence to be long in. Building the set is a modelling step, not a fact delivered by the word “frequentist.” {{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 1, §1.5, p. 12)

## History

The frequency reading of probability was already the language of eighteenth-century work on games and on errors of measurement, the setting in which de Moivre, Bayes, Gauss, and Laplace connected observations with unknown quantities. The twentieth-century shape of frequentist inference is later and more specific. Pearson’s 1900 criterion asked whether a system of deviations could reasonably have arisen by random sampling. Gosset’s 1908 paper gave the mean of a small normal sample a reference distribution of its own. Neyman and Pearson’s 1928 papers organised the use and interpretation of test criteria, and their 1933 paper distinguished the error of rejecting a true null hypothesis from the error of failing to reject a false one. Fisher’s programme of estimation, likelihood, and experimental design supplied many of the procedures whose repeated-sampling behaviour those tests and intervals summarise. {{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 250–251, 263–264)

Comparisons between this programme and probability statements about parameters have been continuous since. Frequency calculations remain the usual way to say what a published interval or test does under repetition, including as a check on Bayesian procedures treated as rules. {{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 4, §4.4, p. 91)
