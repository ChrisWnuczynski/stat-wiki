## Motivating example
A measurement may differ from its unknown true conditional mean because of random error. It also differs from an estimated line, but that second difference uses coefficients calculated from the data. Calling both differences noise hides a dependence: changing one response can move the fitted line and hence alter several residuals. The observed residual vector is useful for diagnosis precisely because it records what the chosen fitted mean failed to explain.{{cite:nist_model_fit2002}} (§4.4.4, residuals)

## Definition — Separate the model from the calculation
In the fixed-design linear model $Y=X\beta+\varepsilon$, the error vector is $\varepsilon=Y-X\beta$. The coefficient vector $\beta$ is the unknown model parameter. After fitting [ordinary least squares](ordinary-least-squares.qmd), the residual vector is $e=y-X\widehat\beta$. It can be computed from observations and the fitted coefficients. The true errors generally cannot be recovered because their calculation would require knowing the true conditional means. This distinction applies even if the fitted line is an excellent approximation.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 457, Residual)

For a full-column-rank design, the hat matrix is $H=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$. It is symmetric and idempotent, and $e=(I-H)y=(I-H)\varepsilon$. Thus the residuals are projections of the errors, rather than independent copies of them. The normal equations give $X^\top e=0$. In particular, when an intercept is fitted, the residuals sum to zero. This is a constraint imposed by fitting; zero-sum errors are not an assumption of the population model.{{cite:nist_ls_straight2002}} (§4.4.3.1, least-squares criterion and normal equations)

## Properties — Fitting changes variance and dependence
Assume $\E(\varepsilon\mid X)=0$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon\mid X)=\sigma^2I$. Then
$$\E(e\mid X)=0,\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(e\mid X)=\sigma^2(I-H).$$
Multiplying out $(I-H)\sigma^2I(I-H)^\top$ proves the covariance identity. Consequently $\operatorname{Var}(e_i\mid X)=\sigma^2(1-h_{ii})$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(e_i,e_j\mid X)=-\sigma^2h_{ij}$ for distinct indices. Higher leverage reduces the marginal residual variance. Standardising only by a common residual standard deviation does not correct that leverage effect. The interpretation of residual plots should take the fitted model's constraints into account.{{cite:nist_model_fit2002}} (§4.4.4, constraints caused by parameter estimation)

Normality is unnecessary for these moment identities. If the errors are jointly normal, the residual vector is jointly normal too, but its covariance is singular whenever coefficients have been fitted. Residual normality checks concern approximate error-model adequacy, rather than a literal assertion that the fitted residuals are independent draws.{{cite:nist_model_fit2002}} (§4.4.4, model validation specifics)

## Worked example — An intercept-only model
Take responses $(1,2,6)$ and fit their common mean. The fitted value is $3$ for each observation and the residuals are $(-2,-1,3)$; their sum is zero and SSE is $14$. Here every entry of $H$ is $1/3$. If independent errors have variance $\sigma^2=9$, each residual has variance $6$ and every pair has covariance $-3$. Their correlation is $-1/2$, despite the independent errors in the model. The observed SSE estimates the error variance through $14/(3-1)=7$.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 457, residual definition; p. 399, within-group sum of squares)

**Your turn.** Add five to every response in this intercept-only example. What happens to the fitted values and residuals?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The fitted mean rises from 3 to 8. The residuals stay $(-2,-1,3)$, so SSE stays 14.
:::

## Statistical use — Diagnose what the model leaves unexplained
Plotting residuals against fitted values, predictors and observation order targets different possible failures: curvature, nonconstant spread or temporal dependence. A small residual alone does not guarantee an unimportant observation, since leverage and influence involve the design as well. Conversely, a large residual can be an informative sign of a misspecified mean, an unusual outcome or a data error. Diagnosis should return to the measurement and scientific context before changing a model.{{cite:nist_model_fit2002}} (§4.4.4)

## Intuition
The error $\varepsilon_i=Y_i-x_i^\top\beta$ compares the response with the unknown conditional mean declared by the model, whereas the residual $e_i=y_i-x_i^\top\widehat\beta$ compares the response with the fitted mean estimated from the entire sample, including $y_i$ itself. When $Y=X\beta+\varepsilon$, projection annihilates $X\beta$ and leaves $e=(I-H)\varepsilon$ subject to $X^\top e=0$. Fitting an intercept forces the residuals to sum to zero even when the realised errors have a nonzero sample sum, inducing negative pairwise correlations among residuals drawn from independent errors—for three equal-leverage observations, two negative residuals force the third to be positive.{{cite:nist_model_fit2002}} (§4.4.4, constraints caused by parameter estimation)

### Properties — Leverage rescales residual variance
The identity $\operatorname{Var}(e_i\mid X)=\sigma^2(1-h_{ii})$ shows that high leverage pulls a residual toward zero: because $y_i$ attracts the fitted surface, $y_i-\widehat y_i$ understates the spread of $Y_i-x_i^\top\beta$. For example, an error variance of $16$ and leverage $h_{ii}=1/8$ give $\operatorname{Var}(e_i\mid X)=16(1-1/8)=14$; studentised residuals divide by a scale incorporating $\sqrt{1-h_{ii}}$, whereas raw or constant-scale residual plots do not.{{cite:sas2012_glm}} (Ch. 4, p. 90, “Predicted and Residual Values”)

When $X$ contains an intercept column $\mathbf{1}$, $H\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}$ and $(I-H)\mathbf{1}=0$: adding any constant shift $c\mathbf{1}$ to $Y$ translates the fitted vector $HY$ by $c\mathbf{1}$ and leaves the residual vector $(I-H)Y$ invariant, whereas $\varepsilon=Y-X\beta$ shifts unless the true intercept also changes by $c$. More generally, an observed response of $15$ against a fitted mean of $11$ has residual $4$, while the true error differs from $4$ by $x_i^\top(\widehat\beta-\beta)$. Dividing $\operatorname{SSE}$ by $\operatorname{rank}(I-H)=n-p$ rather than $n$ compensates for the $p$ lost dimensions; neither $\E(e\mid X)=0$ nor $\operatorname{Cov}(e\mid X)=\sigma^2(I-H)$ requires Gaussian errors, and under Gaussian errors $e$ has a singular normal distribution rather than $n$ independent normal coordinates.{{cite:nist_model_fit2002}} (§4.4.4, constraints caused by parameter estimation)

## Common misconceptions
A residual of zero means only that the fitted surface passes through the observation; it does not mean the true conditional mean equals $y_i$ or that the case had little effect on $\widehat\beta$. High-leverage points can combine a tiny residual with large influence on the fitted coefficients, which is why Cook's distance combines standardized residual size with leverage.{{cite:sas2012_glm}} (Ch. 4, pp. 74, 91, “Introductory Example: Linear Regression” and “Testing Linear Hypotheses”)

A featureless residual plot supports the plotted mean and variance specification without proving independence of the errors, which depends on the sampling mechanism. Conversely, systematic curvature against a predictor signals mean misspecification that should be resolved before interpreting coefficient $p$-values from the misspecified fit.{{cite:nist_model_fit2002}} (§4.4.4, graphical residual analysis)

## Connections
In one-way analysis of variance, the within-group sum of squares is the residual sum of squares around group sample means, where group size $n_g$ gives hat diagonal $1/n_g$ and residual variance $\sigma^2(1-1/n_g)$. In a [generalised linear model](generalised-linear-model.qmd), non-constant mean–variance relations motivate two standardizations of the raw discrepancy $y_i-\widehat\mu_i$: the [Pearson residual](pearson-residual.qmd) divides $y_i-\widehat\mu_i$ by the model variance-function scale, while the [deviance residual](deviance-residual.qmd) takes the signed square root of observation $i$'s log-likelihood discrepancy from a saturated fit. Under the Gaussian linear model with identity link and constant variance both reduce to scaled versions of $y_i-\widehat y_i$, and in every family both remain functions of the fitted mean $\widehat\mu_i$ rather than the unobservable population disturbance.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 457, Residual){{cite:sas2012_glm}} (Ch. 40, p. 2816, “Residuals”)
