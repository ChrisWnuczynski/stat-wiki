## Motivating example
Suppose a laboratory records a response at several predictor levels and wants a line that summarizes their relationship. Many lines can pass near the points; ordinary least squares picks the one whose squared vertical residuals add to the smallest total. Squaring makes the objective smooth and gives larger misses more influence. The fitted line is useful for describing a trend and predicting at observed predictor levels, but a small training error alone does not certify that the relation is linear or that predictions will transfer elsewhere.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 4, §§4.1–4.2, pp. 50–53; Ch. 12, §12.3, pp. 203–204)

## Statement
The matrix formula in the statement applies when the $n\times p$ design matrix $X$ has full column rank. The parameter count $p$ includes the intercept column when one is fitted. The minimisation itself does not require normally distributed errors: for the observed $y$ and chosen $X$, it is an algebraic projection problem. Normality enters later, when using the stated exact normal, chi-squared and Student sampling distributions.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 11, §11.1, pp. 187–190; Ch. 12, §§12.2–12.4, pp. 202–208)

## Intuition
The normal equations $X^\top(y-X\widehat\beta)=0$ say that no column of the design matrix points in a direction that can further reduce the squared residuals. Thus the fitted vector $X\widehat\beta$ is the orthogonal projection of $y$ onto the space of possible fitted vectors. The coefficient vector depends on how that space is parameterised; the fitted vector depends on the space itself. This distinction becomes important when predictors duplicate one another: a unique fitted vector can exist even when individual coefficients are not identifiable.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 11, §§11.1–11.2, pp. 187–193; Ch. 12, §12.3, pp. 203–204)

The geometric view also explains the least-squares decomposition already displayed in Generalizations. Moving away from $\widehat\beta$ changes fitted values by $X(\beta-\widehat\beta)$, which lies in the design space and is perpendicular to the fitted residual vector. The candidate squared error is therefore the minimum SSE plus the squared length of that change. This proves a global minimum rather than merely locating a zero gradient.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 11, §§11.1–11.2, pp. 187–193)

## Key results
Under the fixed-design model $Y=X\beta+\varepsilon$ with $\mathbb E\varepsilon=0$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2I$, OLS is unbiased and has covariance $\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$. The residual sum of squares has expectation $(n-p)\sigma^2$, which explains the divisor $n-p$ in the usual unbiased variance estimator. These moment results require neither a Gaussian error law nor a Bayesian prior. Under independent Gaussian errors, the exact normal and Student laws in Generalizations follow; the same algebra alone cannot give them for arbitrary error distributions.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 11, §11.3, pp. 194–195; Ch. 12, §12.4, pp. 208–209; Ch. 4, §4.7, pp. 62–63)

## Proof
Let $L(b)=\|y-Xb\|_2^2$. Differentiating gives $\nabla_bL=-2X^\top(y-Xb)$, so full column rank yields the unique solution $\widehat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$. Put $H=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$. Since $H^\top=H$ and $H^2=H$, it projects onto the columns of $X$; $\widehat e=(I-H)y$ and $X^\top\widehat e=0$. Consequently $y-Xb=\widehat e+X(\widehat\beta-b)$ is a sum of perpendicular vectors. Squaring proves $L(b)=\operatorname{SSE}+(b-\widehat\beta)^\top X^\top X(b-\widehat\beta)$ and shows that the minimum is global and unique.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 11, §§11.1–11.2, pp. 187–193; Ch. 12, §12.3, pp. 203–204)

For sampling claims, write $Y=X\beta+\varepsilon$ with fixed full-rank $X$, $\mathbb E\varepsilon=0$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2I$. Then $\widehat\beta=\beta+(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\varepsilon$, giving the stated mean and covariance. Also $\operatorname{SSE}=\varepsilon^\top(I-H)\varepsilon$, so $\mathbb E\operatorname{SSE}=\sigma^2\operatorname{tr}(I-H)=(n-p)\sigma^2$. If $\varepsilon\sim N_n(0,\sigma^2I)$, orthogonal Gaussian projections $H\varepsilon$ and $(I-H)\varepsilon$ are independent. The former makes $\widehat\beta$ multivariate normal; the latter has squared length divided by $\sigma^2$ distributed as $\chi^2_{n-p}$. Their independence yields the $t_{n-p}$ coefficient pivot. Finally, maximizing the Gaussian likelihood in $\sigma^2$ at the fitted coefficients gives the divisor $n$, whereas unbiasedness uses $n-p$.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 11, §11.3, pp. 194–195; Ch. 12, §12.4, pp. 208–209; Ch. 7, §§7.1–7.2, pp. 115–124)

## Generalizations — What the assumptions buy
The calculation of a least-squares fit needs observed predictor values and an objective, not a stochastic model. Interpreting a slope as a conditional mean change requires a suitable mean model; calling its usual standard error exact requires further error assumptions. Gaussian errors with constant variance give the exact $t$ pivot in the coefficient property. With heteroskedastic errors, OLS can remain unbiased under a correct mean model, but $\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$ and the usual Student reference law need not describe its sampling uncertainty. Weighted or generalized least squares changes the objective to reflect a known covariance structure.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 4, §4.7, pp. 62–63; Ch. 21, weighted least squares)

### When it fails
If one design column is exactly twice another, $X^\top X$ is singular: infinitely many coefficient pairs make the same predictions, so the inverse formula and individual coefficient estimates fail even though a fitted projection can still be obtained. If the conditional mean is curved, such as $\mathbb E(Y\mid x)=x^2$ over a wide range, a straight-line fit leaves systematic residual structure and extrapolation can be misleading. If one observation has error variance nine while the others have variance one, a common-$\sigma^2$ standard error is miscalibrated. With Cauchy errors, the finite-variance argument for $s^2$ and the exact normal-error $t$ law both fail. Each is a specific failure of rank, mean form or error law, rather than a failure of the minimisation algorithm.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 4, §4.7, pp. 62–63; Ch. 12, §12.5, pp. 209–210; Ch. 13, §13.2, pp. 216–223)

## Statistical use — From fitted line to inference
First specify the response, predictors, transformations and whether an intercept belongs in $X$. Fit the coefficients, then examine residuals against fitted values and predictors: a pattern can expose missing curvature, changing spread or unusual cases. Compare predictive performance on data not used for fitting when prediction is the goal. Use the normal-error interval and test formulas only when their sampling assumptions are credible; a small SSE on the same observations used to fit the coefficients is not a measure of future accuracy.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 4, §§4.2 and 4.6–4.7, pp. 52–63; Ch. 6, §6.1, pp. 77–102)

## Common misconceptions
OLS is not inherently Bayesian: the coefficient minimizer is defined without a prior, even though it appears in a Bayesian regression analysis. Its residuals are not the unobserved model errors; fitting forces residuals to satisfy orthogonality constraints. A significant fitted slope does not establish causation, and a linear formula in coefficients does not prove the conditional mean is linear in the raw predictor. Finally, the formula for $\widehat\beta$ and the exact Student pivot do not have the same assumptions: the former is algebraic, while the latter uses a stochastic normal-error model.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 4, §§4.6–4.7, pp. 61–63; Ch. 11, §11.1, pp. 187–190)

## History
Legendre published the least-squares method in 1805, initially in the setting of astronomical calculation. Dodge's entry records this priority and presents the method as minimising squared residuals. Later regression theory separated that optimisation from the probability model used for standard errors and tests; the same distinction is important when the fitted line is used for prediction today.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 304–305, “Least-Squares Method”){{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 4, §4.7, pp. 62–63)

## Worked example
For predictor values $x=(0,1,2)$ and responses $y=(1,2,2)$, fit an intercept and a slope. Here $\bar x=1$, $\bar y=5/3$, $\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2=2$, and $\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)=1$. The slope is $1/2$ and the intercept is $5/3-(1/2)(1)=7/6$. The fitted values are $(7/6,5/3,13/6)$ and the residuals are $(-1/6,1/3,-1/6)$. Their sum is zero, as required by the intercept normal equation, and the SSE is $1/36+1/9+1/36=1/6$. With $n=3$ and $p=2$, the residual degrees of freedom are one, so $s^2=(1/6)/(3-2)=1/6$. This is a property of the fitted sample; no claim of normal errors follows from the calculation.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 11, §11.1, pp. 187–190)

**Your turn.** Fit an intercept and slope to $x=(0,1,2,3)$ and $y=(1,2,2,4)$. Find the coefficients, fitted values, SSE and unbiased residual-variance estimate.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
$\bar x=1.5$, $\bar y=2.25$, $S_{xx}=5$ and $S_{xy}=4.5$. Thus $\widehat\beta_1=0.9$ and $\widehat\beta_0=2.25-0.9(1.5)=0.9$. The fitted values are $(0.9,1.8,2.7,3.6)$ and residuals are $(0.1,0.2,-0.7,0.4)$, whose squares sum to $0.70$. With $n-p=4-2=2$, $s^2=0.70/2=0.35$. The estimate describes error spread under the assumed model; it does not show that the mean relationship is truly linear.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 11, §11.1, pp. 187–190; Ch. 12, §12.4, pp. 208–209)
