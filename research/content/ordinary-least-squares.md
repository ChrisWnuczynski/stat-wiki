## Motivating example
Suppose a laboratory records a response at several predictor levels and wants a linear summary of their relationship. Many candidate fits pass near the points; under the [least-squares](least-squares.qmd) criterion, ordinary least squares selects the coefficient vector whose squared vertical residuals sum to the smallest total. Squaring makes the objective differentiable and weights large discrepancies heavily. A small in-sample residual sum of squares describes the observed data, however, and does not by itself show that the conditional mean is linear or that predictions transfer outside the design.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 4, §§4.1–4.2, pp. 50–53; Ch. 12, §12.3, pp. 203–204)

## Statement
The closed-form estimator $\widehat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$ requires the $n\times p$ design matrix $X$ to have full column rank $p$, where $p$ counts the intercept column when one is included. For observed $y$ and chosen $X$, computing $\widehat\beta$ is an algebraic projection problem that needs no distributional assumption on the errors. Gaussian assumptions enter only when claiming exact finite-sample normal, chi-squared, and Student-$t$ sampling laws.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 11, §11.1, pp. 187–190; Ch. 12, §§12.2–12.4, pp. 202–208)

## Intuition
The normal equations $X^\top(y-X\widehat\beta)=0$ state that no column of $X$ has a nonzero inner product with the residual vector, so no linear adjustment of the coefficients can shrink the squared error further. Equivalently, $X\widehat\beta$ is the orthogonal projection of $y$ onto the column space of $X$. The coordinate vector $\widehat\beta$ depends on how that subspace is parameterised, whereas the fitted vector $X\widehat\beta$ depends only on the subspace itself; when predictors are collinear, the fitted projection remains unique even though individual coefficients are not identifiable.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 11, §§11.1–11.2, pp. 187–193; Ch. 12, §12.3, pp. 203–204)

Orthogonality also yields the [least-squares decomposition](least-squares-decomposition.qmd): replacing $\widehat\beta$ by any candidate $\beta$ shifts the fitted vector by $X(\beta-\widehat\beta)$, which lies in the column space of $X$ and is perpendicular to $y-X\widehat\beta$. By the Pythagorean identity, $\|y-X\beta\|_2^2$ equals the minimum residual sum of squares plus $\|X(\beta-\widehat\beta)\|_2^2$, establishing a global minimum rather than merely a stationary point.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 11, §§11.1–11.2, pp. 187–193)

## Key results
Under the fixed-design model $Y=X\beta+\varepsilon$ with $\E(\varepsilon)=0$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2I$, the fact that [least-squares estimators are unbiased](least-squares-estimators-are-unbiased.qmd) gives $\E(\widehat\beta)=\beta$ and linearity gives $\operatorname{Cov}(\widehat\beta)=\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$. The residual sum of squares satisfies $\E(\operatorname{SSE})=(n-p)\sigma^2$, motivating the unbiased variance estimator $s^2=\operatorname{SSE}/(n-p)$. None of these moment identities requires Gaussian errors or a prior distribution; independent Gaussian errors are what supply the exact normal, $\chi^2_{n-p}$, and $t_{n-p}$ laws of the [normal linear model](normal-linear-model.qmd).{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 11, §11.3, pp. 194–195; Ch. 12, §12.4, pp. 208–209; Ch. 4, §4.7, pp. 62–63)

## Proof
Let $L(b)=\|y-Xb\|_2^2$. Differentiating gives $\nabla_bL=-2X^\top(y-Xb)$, and full column rank makes $X^\top X$ positive definite, yielding the unique solution $\widehat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$. Put $H=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$. Because $H^\top=H$ and $H^2=H$, $H$ is the orthogonal projector onto $\operatorname{col}(X)$, $\widehat e=(I-H)y$, and $X^\top\widehat e=0$. Writing $y-Xb=\widehat e+X(\widehat\beta-b)$ decomposes the discrepancy into perpendicular vectors, so $L(b)=\operatorname{SSE}+(b-\widehat\beta)^\top X^\top X(b-\widehat\beta)$, which is uniquely minimised at $b=\widehat\beta$.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 11, §§11.1–11.2, pp. 187–193; Ch. 12, §12.3, pp. 203–204)

Under $Y=X\beta+\varepsilon$ with fixed full-rank $X$, $\E(\varepsilon)=0$, and $\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2I$, unbiasedness $\E(\widehat\beta)=\beta$ and covariance $\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$ follow from the linear representation $\widehat\beta-\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\varepsilon$ proved in [least-squares estimators are unbiased](least-squares-estimators-are-unbiased.qmd). Because $(I-H)X=0$, $\operatorname{SSE}=\varepsilon^\top(I-H)\varepsilon$ and $\E(\operatorname{SSE})=\sigma^2\operatorname{tr}(I-H)=(n-p)\sigma^2$. When $\varepsilon\sim N_n(0,\sigma^2I)$, the orthogonal projections $H\varepsilon$ and $(I-H)\varepsilon$ are independent Gaussian vectors: $\widehat\beta$ is multivariate normal, $\operatorname{SSE}/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-p}$ independently, and each standardized coordinate ratio follows $t_{n-p}$. Maximizing the Gaussian log-likelihood in $\sigma^2$ at $\widehat\beta$ instead divides $\operatorname{SSE}$ by $n$ rather than $n-p$.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 11, §11.3, pp. 194–195; Ch. 12, §12.4, pp. 208–209; Ch. 7, §§7.1–7.2, pp. 115–124)

## Generalizations — What the assumptions buy
Each layer of assumptions buys a distinct conclusion. Observed predictors and squared-error loss alone define the projection $X\widehat\beta$. Adding $\E(\varepsilon\mid X)=0$ makes $\widehat\beta$ unbiased for $\beta$ even under heteroskedastic errors, as shown in [least-squares estimators are unbiased](least-squares-estimators-are-unbiased.qmd), though $\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$ no longer describes its covariance when error variances differ across observations. Adding uncorrelated homoscedastic errors gives $\operatorname{Cov}(\widehat\beta\mid X)=\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$, and adding Gaussian errors yields exact finite-sample $t$ and $F$ pivots. When the error covariance $\Sigma$ is known and non-spherical, weighted or generalized least squares replaces the Euclidean norm to match $\Sigma^{-1}$.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 4, §4.7, pp. 62–63; Ch. 21, §§21.1–21.6, pp. 352–376)

### When it fails
If one design column is twice another, $X^\top X$ is singular: infinitely many coefficient vectors yield the same fitted projection, so $(X^\top X)^{-1}$ fails even though $X\widehat\beta$ remains uniquely defined. If the conditional mean is curved, such as $\E(Y\mid x)=x^2$ across a wide interval, a straight-line specification leaves systematic residual curvature and biases extrapolation. If one observation has error variance $9$ while the others have variance $1$, homoscedastic standard errors are miscalibrated. Under heavy-tailed Cauchy errors, finite-variance justifications for $s^2$ and the normal-error $t$ reference law both fail. Each breakdown reflects rank deficiency, mean misspecification, or the error law rather than the minimisation step itself.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 4, §4.7, pp. 62–63; Ch. 12, §12.5, pp. 209–210; Ch. 13, §13.2, pp. 216–223)

## Related concepts
The [least-squares decomposition](least-squares-decomposition.qmd) isolates the Pythagorean optimality identity, while [least-squares estimators are unbiased](least-squares-estimators-are-unbiased.qmd), the [sampling distribution of the regression estimators](sampling-distribution-of-the-regression-estimators.qmd), and the [sampling distribution of the OLS estimator](sampling-distribution-of-the-ols-estimator.qmd) separate first-moment, second-moment, and Gaussian-error conclusions. [Ridge regression](ridge-regression.qmd) penalises coefficient norm to stabilise nearly collinear designs. The [normal-equations question](../questions/ex-inf-normal-equations.qmd) derives the two-parameter minimiser, the [through-origin question](../questions/ex-inf-line-through-origin.qmd) examines omitting the intercept, and the [advertising regression question](../questions/ex-inf-advertising-r2.qmd) computes $s$ and a slope confidence interval.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 11, §11.1, pp. 187–190; Ch. 15, §15.4, pp. 265–270)

## Statistical use — From fitted line to inference
Specify the response, predictors, transformations, and whether an intercept column belongs in $X$ before fitting. After computing $\widehat\beta$, inspect residuals against fitted values and predictors to check for curvature, heteroskedasticity, or high-influence points, and evaluate predictive accuracy out of sample when prediction is the objective. Apply normal-theory $t$ intervals and tests only when independent, homoscedastic, roughly Gaussian errors are credible; a small in-sample $\operatorname{SSE}$ does not certify out-of-sample precision.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 4, §§4.2 and 4.6–4.7, pp. 52–63; Ch. 6, §6.1, pp. 77–102)

## Common misconceptions
Ordinary least squares requires no prior distribution on $\beta$, even though $\widehat\beta$ reappears as a posterior centre under flat or conjugate priors. Fitted [residuals](error-and-residual.qmd) are not independent copies of the unobservable errors $\varepsilon$, because projection enforces $X^\top\widehat e=0$. A statistically significant slope does not establish causation, and linearity in $\beta$ does not imply that $\E(Y\mid x)$ is a straight line in a raw predictor. Finally, $\widehat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$ is algebraic, whereas the exact $t_{n-p}$ pivot requires the Gaussian error model.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 4, §§4.6–4.7, pp. 61–63; Ch. 11, §11.1, pp. 187–190)

## History
Legendre published the method of least squares in 1805 to reconcile astronomical measurements by minimising the sum of squared residuals. Subsequent regression theory separated that algebraic minimisation from the error-distribution assumptions required for standard errors, confidence intervals, and hypothesis tests.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 304–305, “Least-Squares Method”){{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 4, §4.7, pp. 62–63)

## Worked example
For predictor values $x=(0,1,2)$ and responses $y=(1,2,2)$, fit an intercept and a slope. Here $\bar x=1$, $\bar y=5/3$, $\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2=2$, and $\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)=1$. The slope is $1/2$ and the intercept is $5/3-(1/2)(1)=7/6$. The fitted values are $(7/6,5/3,13/6)$ and the residuals are $(-1/6,1/3,-1/6)$. Their sum is zero, as required by the intercept normal equation, and the SSE is $1/36+1/9+1/36=1/6$. With $n=3$ and $p=2$, the residual degrees of freedom are one, so $s^2=(1/6)/(3-2)=1/6$. This is a property of the fitted sample; no claim of normal errors follows from the calculation.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 11, §11.1, pp. 187–190)

**Your turn.** Fit an intercept and slope to $x=(0,1,2,3)$ and $y=(1,2,2,4)$. Find the coefficients, fitted values, SSE and unbiased residual-variance estimate.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
$\bar x=1.5$, $\bar y=2.25$, $S_{xx}=5$ and $S_{xy}=4.5$. Thus $\widehat\beta_1=0.9$ and $\widehat\beta_0=2.25-0.9(1.5)=0.9$. The fitted values are $(0.9,1.8,2.7,3.6)$ and residuals are $(0.1,0.2,-0.7,0.4)$, whose squares sum to $0.70$. With $n-p=4-2=2$, $s^2=0.70/2=0.35$. The estimate describes error spread under the assumed model; it does not show that the mean relationship is truly linear.
:::
