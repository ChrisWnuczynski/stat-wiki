## Lead context
It lets a regression describe how much responses vary around their fitted means without changing the mean relationship itself.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (p. 544, variance function and dispersion)

## Motivating example
The clotting times in the source example are positive, and their spread grows where their fitted mean is larger. A Gamma GLM can describe both features: a log link keeps the mean positive, while its dispersion determines how widely times scatter around that mean. If two conditions have means of $6$ and $12$ seconds but the same Gamma dispersion, their variances need not be equal. The larger mean has four times the variance under the Gamma variance function. This is a statement about conditional variation at each predictor setting, not about variation in the predictor or uncertainty in a fitted coefficient.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (p. 544, mean, variance and link)

## Intuition
A GLM first chooses a mean $\mu_i$ for each observation from its predictors. Its variance function $V(\mu_i)$ then says how variability changes as that mean changes; a common multiplier $\phi$ sets the overall scale. Changing $\phi$ from $1$ to $2$ doubles the conditional variance at each fixed mean but does not double the mean. On a standard-deviation scale, the multiplier is $\sqrt2$. This separation explains why dispersion matters for uncertainty even if fitted mean predictions are unchanged.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (p. 544){{cite:rstats_summary_glm2026}} (“Details” and “Value”)

## Definition
For independent GLM responses conditional on their specified predictor values, write $\mu_i=\mathbb E(Y_i\mid\mathbf x_i)$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y_i\mid\mathbf x_i)=\phi V(\mu_i)$. Here $V$ is the family's variance function and $\phi>0$ is the dispersion parameter, conventionally common across observations. The normal model has $V(\mu)=1$ and $\phi=\sigma^2$; a Poisson model has $V(\mu)=\mu$ and fixed $\phi=1$. A Bernoulli response has $V(\mu)=\mu(1-\mu)$ and also fixes $\phi=1$. For a Gamma response parameterized by shape $\alpha$ and mean $\mu$, $V(\mu)=\mu^2$ and $\phi=1/\alpha$.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (p. 544){{cite:rstats_family2026}} (“Details”, fixed and free dispersion)

In the exponential-dispersion form, the density or mass function is $\exp\{[y\theta-b(\theta)]/\phi+c(y,\phi)\}$. Differentiating its normalizing identity gives $\mu=b'(\theta)$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=\phi b''(\theta)$, so $V(\mu)=b''(\theta)$. This is the convention used on the [exponential family](exponential-family.qmd) page. A mean and variance function alone can also define a quasi-likelihood estimating equation without specifying a complete distribution.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (pp. 543–544, model and moments){{cite:rstats_family2026}} (“Details”, quasi families)

## Interpretation
The same symbol describes different units in different families. Normal $\phi=\sigma^2$ has squared response units, whereas Gamma $\phi=1/\alpha$ is dimensionless. In the Gamma GLM, shape $1/\phi$ and rate $1/(\phi\mu_i)$ give mean $\mu_i$ and variance $\phi\mu_i^2$. A fitted mean of $6$ with shape $3$ has dispersion $1/3$, rate $3/6=0.5$, variance $(1/3)6^2=12$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{12}\approx3.464$. At mean $12$ with the same shape, variance becomes $48$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{48}\approx6.928$. The coefficient of variation stays $\sqrt{1/3}\approx0.577$. Thus constant dispersion does not mean constant variance.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (p. 544, Gamma and variance function)

## Properties
For a fixed mean model and variance function, multiplying $\phi$ changes the scale of conditional variance while leaving the mean equation alone. In the usual exponential-dispersion GLM score, a common $1/\phi$ factor cancels from the equation for the fitted coefficients. Their estimates can therefore remain the same while their estimated covariance and standard errors change. In particular, a quasi-Poisson model retains the Poisson mean score and permits $\phi>1$ or $\phi<1$ to describe greater or lesser variance than the Poisson assumption. R's `quasipoisson` and `quasibinomial` families free this parameter; the corresponding ordinary families fix it at one.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (p. 544, common dispersion and fitting){{cite:rstats_family2026}} (“Details”){{cite:rstats_summary_glm2026}} (“Details” and “Value”)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
For a Gamma variable with shape $\alpha$ and rate $r$, $\mathbb E Y=\alpha/r$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=\alpha/r^2$. Put $\alpha=1/\phi$ and $r=1/(\phi\mu)$. Then $\alpha/r=\mu$ and $\alpha/r^2=\phi\mu^2$. Its standard deviation divided by its mean is $\sqrt\phi$. Holding $\phi$ fixed while replacing $\mu$ by $2\mu$ multiplies variance by $4$.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (p. 544, Gamma family and variance relation)

For a quasi-Poisson log link, $\mu_i=e^{\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta}$, $\partial\mu_i/\partial\boldsymbol\beta=\mu_i\mathbf x_i$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y_i\mid\mathbf x_i)=\phi\mu_i$. Its quasi-score is therefore $U(\boldsymbol\beta)=\phi^{-1}\sum_i\mathbf x_i(Y_i-\mu_i)$. Multiplying an equation by the nonzero constant $\phi$ does not change its roots, which are the Poisson score roots for the same data and mean model. The inverse information or quasi-score covariance carries the factor $\phi$, so its standard errors carry $\sqrt\phi$ under the usual model assumptions.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (pp. 543–544, likelihood and common-dispersion fitting){{cite:rstats_summary_glm2026}} (“Value”, scaled covariance)
:::
:::

## Statistical use
Start by specifying the response and variance function, then examine whether the observed spread around fitted means is plausible. For a Poisson count model, a common descriptive estimate of extra dispersion is $\hat\phi_P=X_P^2/(n-p)$, where $X_P^2=\sum_i(y_i-\hat\mu_i)^2/V(\hat\mu_i)$ is the Pearson residual sum and $p$ is the fitted model rank. R's `summary.glm` uses residual Pearson chi-squared divided by residual degrees of freedom when it estimates dispersion. The ratio is a diagnostic and estimator under the chosen mean, variance and independence conditions, not a cure for a wrong mean pattern.{{cite:rstats_summary_glm2026}} (“Details”)

An estimated $\phi>1$ enlarges coefficient standard errors relative to a fixed-one Poisson analysis. A quasi-Poisson fit provides mean and variance equations but no full count likelihood, so an ordinary likelihood-ratio comparison or likelihood-based AIC cannot simply be carried over. If distinct subgroups have different variance relationships, one shared $\phi$ may be too crude; if observations are dependent, treating them as independent will also distort a Pearson calculation. Inspect residuals and the data-generating setting before interpreting the scale estimate.{{cite:rstats_family2026}} (“Details” and “Value”, quasi-family AIC){{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (p. 544, common dispersion)

## Worked example
Suppose four independent counts are $1,3,5,7$. An intercept-only Poisson model fits their mean $\hat\mu=(1+3+5+7)/4=4$. With one fitted coefficient, its Pearson sum is $X_P^2=[(1-4)^2+(3-4)^2+(5-4)^2+(7-4)^2]/4=20/4=5$ on $n-p=4-1=3$ residual degrees of freedom. The quasi-Poisson estimate is $\hat\phi_P=5/3\approx1.667$. The count sample variance is $20/3\approx6.667$, exceeding its mean $4$; indeed their ratio is $5/3$. The Poisson standard error for the fitted log mean is $1/\sqrt{16}=0.25$, and scaling its variance by $5/3$ gives a quasi standard error $\sqrt{5/3}/4\approx0.323$. This four-count estimate is noisy, so it illustrates the calculation rather than establishing a population dispersion.{{cite:rstats_summary_glm2026}} (“Details” and “Value”, Pearson estimate and covariance scaling)

**Your turn.** For independent counts $2,2,8,8$, fit an intercept-only Poisson mean, calculate the Pearson dispersion estimate with its residual degrees of freedom, and state the factor by which a quasi-Poisson standard error changes.{{cite:rstats_summary_glm2026}} (“Details”)

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The mean is $\hat\mu=20/4=5$. The squared deviations sum to $4(3^2)=36$, so $X_P^2=36/5=7.2$. There are $4-1=3$ residual degrees of freedom, giving $\hat\phi_P=(36/5)/3=12/5=2.4$. The standard-error factor is $\sqrt{12/5}\approx1.549$. The estimated variance at mean $5$ is $\hat\phi_P\hat\mu=12$, although a quasi model specifies no complete count distribution.{{cite:rstats_summary_glm2026}} (“Details” and “Value”)
:::

## Common misconceptions
Dispersion is not the fitted mean or the variance function; it multiplies that function at a given mean. A constant $\phi$ does not imply equal variance across predictor settings: the Gamma example's variance quadruples as its mean doubles. A Poisson model does not acquire an independently adjustable variance parameter merely by naming one; free $\phi$ in quasi-Poisson specifies a mean–variance relation without the original Poisson likelihood. Finally, $\hat\phi_P>1$ is evidence of extra residual spread under the fitted model, not proof that one specific alternative distribution generated the counts.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (p. 544){{cite:rstats_family2026}} (“Details”)

## Assumptions and limits
The common-dispersion formula assumes the selected variance function applies at each mean. A data set of counts with a structural excess of zeros is a concrete failure of an ordinary Poisson shape: merely inflating all Poisson variances with one multiplier does not supply probabilities for those zeros. Repeated counts from the same patient offer another failure when within-patient dependence is ignored; a large Pearson ratio then blends dependence and marginal spread. For Bernoulli observations with one trial each, a free scale cannot turn the Bernoulli probability law into another Bernoulli law with the same mean, so quasi-binomial dispersion should be read as a variance or working-covariance convention. Model checking, study design and the unit of observation determine whether a reported $\phi$ is meaningful.{{cite:rstats_family2026}} (“Details”, quasi-binomial restriction){{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (p. 544, independence and common dispersion)

## History
Nelder and Wedderburn's 1972 paper unified normal, Poisson, binomial and Gamma regression using a common fitting framework. McCullagh's historical introduction explains that their original notation called the multiplier a “scale factor”; the later McCullagh–Nelder notation calls $\phi$ the “dispersion parameter.” The change of name makes the distinction from the variance function explicit, while the underlying mean–variance relation remains $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=\phi V(\mu)$.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (Description, publisher record, title and date){{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (pp. 543–544, terminology table and variance relation)
