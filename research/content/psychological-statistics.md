## Lead context
Psychological statistics is the design and analysis of studies of behaviour, perception, and mental measurement. The measurements are often scores on a task, ratings on a scale, or choices in an experiment, and the scientific claim is about a difference or an association in those measurements. The statistical work is ordinary: experiments and observational comparisons, effect sizes, and tests. What is distinctive is the subject matter and the standard the field has recently been held to, because a large replication project found that many published psychological effects shrink or disappear when the study is repeated with new data.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 519){{cite:osc2015}} (Results)

## Definition
Dodge places psychology and psychometry among the domains of statistics: problems related to the evaluation of study capacity, intelligence, personal characteristics, and normal and abnormal behaviour, and the establishment of scales of measurement and instruments of measure. The statistical content of that description is the construction of a scale and the comparison of people, or of conditions, on that scale. A scale assigns numbers to responses. A comparison asks whether the numbers differ across groups, across treatments, or across repeated occasions.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 519)

The designs that produce the numbers fall into the same families used elsewhere. An experiment assigns conditions. An observational study records behaviour without that assignment. A test score is a measurement, with its own error. A correlation between two scores is an association. None of these is special to psychology in the arithmetic. They are special in what the number is supposed to represent: a construct such as memory, attention, or attitude, which is not observed directly and is tied to the score by the design of the instrument. The instrument is part of the model. A change in the wording of an item can change the score without any change in the person.

Effect size is the scale on which psychological results are now commonly compared across studies. In the Reproducibility Project the original effects, summarised as a correlation, had mean \(0.403\) and standard deviation \(0.188\). The replication effects had mean \(0.197\) and standard deviation \(0.257\). The replication mean is about half the original mean. That comparison is a statistical summary of a literature, not a summary of one experiment.{{cite:osc2015}} (Results)

## Interpretation
A significant test in a psychological experiment is a statement about a sampling model and a preselected comparison. It is not, by itself, a statement that the effect will reappear at the same size in a new sample. The Reproducibility Project makes the gap numerical. Of \(100\) studies, \(97\) original effects were positive results. The replications were designed with high power, on average \(0.92\), relative to the original effect sizes. If those original effects had been true and accurately estimated, about \(89\) replications would have been expected to be positive. The number observed was \(35\), which is \(36.1\%\), with a \(95\%\) interval from \(26.6\%\) to \(46.2\%\). The reduction is large. It is a fact about this set of replications, not a constant of nature, and it is computed from a vote that treats \(p<0.05\) in the same direction as success.{{cite:osc2015}} (Results)

That vote has a weakness the project itself records. A replication with \(p=0.06\) in the same direction is counted as a failure, even if it is close to the original effect. The project therefore also compared effect sizes directly. Original effects were larger than replication effects on a paired test, Wilcoxon's \(W=7137\). Of \(99\) studies with an effect size in both the original and the replication, \(82\) showed the stronger effect in the original. Original and replication effect sizes were positively correlated, Spearman's \(r=0.51\). The correlation says that larger original effects tended to have larger replication effects. It does not say that the original magnitude was recovered.{{cite:osc2015}} (Results)

The field split is part of the interpretation. Among social-psychology effects, \(25\%\) replicated by the \(p<0.05\) criterion. Among cognitive-psychology effects, \(50\%\) did. The project reports that the difference is tied in part to weaker original effects in social psychology, and to the greater frequency of within-subject and repeated-measure designs in cognitive psychology. Interaction effects replicated less often than main effects: \(8\) of \(37\) interactions, against \(23\) of \(49\) main or simple effects. A statistical interaction is a difference of differences. It is a harder target, and the replication rate says so.{{cite:osc2015}} (Results)

Ioannidis's earlier argument is the theoretical companion. A research finding, defined as a relationship that reaches formal statistical significance, has a probability of being true that depends on the prior odds in the field, the power, and the Type I error rate. The positive predictive value in his calculation is \((1-\beta)R/(R-\beta R+\alpha)\), where \(R\) is the ratio of true relationships to false ones among those tested. Small studies, small effects, a large number of tested relationships, and flexibility in design and analysis all push that value down. Psychology, as practised in the journals the replication project sampled, is one of the fields in which those conditions are common. The replication numbers are what that argument looks like when the studies are actually repeated.{{cite:ioannidis2005}} (p. 0696){{cite:osc2015}} (Results)

## Statistical use
A psychological experiment is reported with the design, the measurement, the effect size, and the uncertainty of the effect size. The design says whether the comparison was assigned or only observed. The measurement says what the score is. The effect size says how large the difference was, on a scale that a later study can try to recover. A \(p\)-value says how extreme the statistic was under a null model. The replication project is evidence that the \(p\)-value, alone, is a poor guide to whether the effect size will reappear. Original studies with \(p<0.001\) were more likely to replicate at \(p<0.05\): \(20\) of \(32\), about \(63\%\). Original studies with \(p>0.04\) were less likely: \(2\) of \(11\). The strength of the original evidence predicts replication better than a binary "significant."{{cite:osc2015}} (Results)

Power is part of the design, not a correction applied after a failure. The replications aimed for at least \(80\%\) power based on the original effect size, and the average power of the \(97\) significant originals was \(0.92\). High power did not produce a high replication rate. If the original effect sizes were inflated, the power calculation, which uses those sizes, is aimed at a target larger than the effect the replication can find. Publishing the materials and the analysis is what makes that diagnosis possible. The project required replication materials and data to be archived. A result that cannot be repeated because the score cannot be reconstructed is a failure of the record, distinct from a failure of the effect.{{cite:osc2015}} (Method, Replication Protocol)

Scales and instruments, in Dodge's description of psychometry, are themselves statistical objects. A score that is a sum of items has a reliability, a relation between the variation of interest and the variation of the measurement. Comparing groups on an unreliable score shrinks differences. Comparing groups on a score that does not measure the construct named in the conclusion measures something else. The statistical use of a psychological result includes saying which instrument was used and which comparison was pre-specified. Flexibility in the choice of outcome and analysis is one of the conditions Ioannidis lists as lowering the probability that a significant finding is true.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 519){{cite:ioannidis2005}} (p. 0696)

## History
The statistical treatment of mental measurement is older than the replication project. Spearman, a psychologist, introduced the rank correlation in 1904, as a way to measure association without relying on the raw spacing of the scores. Psychometry, as Dodge lists it among the applications of statistics, is the construction of scales for capacity, intelligence, and personality. The experimental tradition compared conditions on those scales and reported significance tests.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 502, 519)

The replication crisis in the field is a twenty-first-century reckoning with that reporting habit. Ioannidis argued in 2005 that most claimed research findings are false, in a precise sense: the probability that a significant claim is true can fall below one half when effects are small, studies are small, and many relationships are tested. The Open Science Collaboration's 2015 project took \(100\) experimental and correlational studies from three psychology journals and repeated them. The numerical result is the one quoted above: replication effects about half as large, and about \(36\%\) significant in the same direction. That project is now part of the definition of what a published psychological effect has to survive.{{cite:ioannidis2005}} (p. 0696){{cite:osc2015}} (Results)
