## Motivating example
A clotting-time model must predict a positive waiting time, yet its linear predictor can move in either direction as concentration changes. The reciprocal, or *inverse*, link puts the reciprocal mean on the linear scale: if a higher dose raises that scale, the expected time falls. This is a natural way to describe a rate of a process, but it also makes predictions delicate when the linear predictor approaches zero. A gamma response family can use this link because its mean is positive; choosing the link still requires a scientific judgment about how mean clotting time changes with dose.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§3.4, pp. 380–381, gamma inverse transformation){{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Usage and Arguments, Gamma links)

## Intuition
The two directions of a link are easy to confuse. Every GLM link $g$ maps a mean $\mu$ to a predictor $\eta$; its inverse $g^{-1}$ maps the predictor back to the mean. On this page, the named *inverse link* means the particular function $g(\mu)=1/\mu$. It happens to be its own inverse: $g^{-1}(\eta)=1/\eta$. R calls this option `link = "inverse"` and calls the mean-producing function of any link `linkinv`. The same software has `linkinv` for a logit or log link, even though neither of those links is reciprocal.{{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Arguments, link names; Value, `linkfun` and `linkinv`)

## Definition — Domain
Let $\mu_i=\mathbb E(Y_i\mid\mathbf x_i)>0$ and $\eta_i=\mathbf x_i^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\beta$. The reciprocal link specifies $\eta_i=1/\mu_i$, so $\mu_i=1/\eta_i$. Both directions have domain $(0,\infty)$ when the mean must be positive. The condition $\eta_i>0$ must hold for every covariate combination at which the model is interpreted or predicted. A fitted coefficient vector does not enforce that inequality automatically. The gamma variance model, $\operatorname{Var}(Y_i\mid\mathbf x_i)=\phi\mu_i^2$, is a separate assumption; the same reciprocal mean map is available with an inverse-Gaussian variance model, $\phi\mu_i^3$.{{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Usage and Arguments, Gamma and inverse-Gaussian links; Value, variance and valideta){{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§§1.1–1.3, pp. 371–372; §3.4, p. 380)

## Interpretation — A linear rate, a curved mean
If $\eta=\beta_0+\beta_1x$, then each unit increase in $x$ adds $\beta_1$ to the reciprocal mean. It does not add a constant amount to the mean. At a predictor of $0.25$ the mean is $4$; at $0.05$ it is $20$. Thus equal shifts of the predictor can have very unequal effects on the response scale. A slope is most plainly interpreted as a change in the reciprocal mean, with its physical units reversed from those of the response. For a waiting time measured in seconds, $1/\mu$ is measured in inverse seconds. That rate-like reading is useful only when a reciprocal waiting time answers the application’s question.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§3.4, pp. 380–381, inverse transformation for gamma means)

The derivative makes the curvature visible. With $\eta(x)=\beta_0+\beta_1x>0$, $d\mu/dx=-\beta_1/\eta(x)^2$. A positive slope makes the mean decrease, and its absolute change per unit $x$ grows as $\eta$ gets close to zero. A change of $\Delta$ has the exact mean ratio $\mu(x+\Delta)/\mu(x)=\eta(x)/\{\eta(x)+\beta_1\Delta\}$, provided both predictors are positive. There is no constant mean ratio as there would be under a log link. The limit $\eta\downarrow0$ sends the fitted mean to infinity.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§1.3, p. 372, linear predictor and link; §3.4, p. 380, inverse gamma link)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
From $\mu=\eta^{-1}$ and $\eta=\beta_0+\beta_1x$, differentiation gives $d\mu/dx=-\eta^{-2}\beta_1$, and division of the two reciprocal means gives $\mu(x+\Delta)/\mu(x)=\eta(x)/\{\eta(x)+\beta_1\Delta\}$. For a gamma variable with fixed shape $k>0$ and mean $\mu$, the density is proportional, as a function of $\mu$, to $\exp\{-ky/\mu-k\log\mu\}$. In a standard exponential-dispersion representation with dispersion $\phi=1/k$, the natural parameter is $\theta=-1/\mu$ and its cumulant is $b(\theta)=-\log(-\theta)$, since $b'(\theta)=-1/\theta=\mu$. Thus the exact canonical link under this sign convention is $-1/\mu$. The positive reciprocal link used by R differs only by the sign of the linear predictor; its admissible predictor values are positive.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§§1.1–1.3, pp. 371–372, exponential family and link; §3.4, p. 380, gamma natural parameter){{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Usage, default Gamma link)
:::
:::

## Statistical use — Choosing the family and link
In a gamma GLM the inverse link is R’s default. A gamma model with a log link instead sets $\mu=e^\eta$, which remains positive for every finite real predictor and gives multiplicative mean changes. An inverse-Gaussian model can use `link = "inverse"` as well, while its default is `"1/mu^2"`, corresponding to $\mu=1/\sqrt\eta$. The distributions change the variance function and likelihood even if the fitted mean formula uses the same link. Compare candidate links through the shape of the mean relation, the allowed prediction range, residual behaviour and an appropriate model-comparison or predictive criterion.{{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Usage and Arguments, accepted links; Value, variance function){{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§3.4, pp. 380–381, gamma example)

The canonical-link label needs care. In the usual gamma exponential-family parameterisation the natural parameter is negative reciprocal mean. The positive reciprocal transformation has the same geometry after reversing coefficient signs, and R calls it `inverse`. It has no special guarantee that a linear predictor will remain in its legal range. In the clotting-time example, an intercept $0.5$ and slope $-0.1$ give $\eta(4)=0.1$ and $\mu(4)=10$; at $x=6$ the predictor is $-0.1$ and the formal reciprocal is $-10$, which is not a possible positive mean. The model stops being usable before that extrapolation: at $x=5$ its predictor is zero.{{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Usage and Arguments, Gamma inverse link; Value, `valideta`){{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§3.4, p. 380, gamma natural parameter)

## Worked example
Suppose a gamma model for waiting time in seconds specifies $1/\mu(d)=0.02+0.003d$, where $d$ is a dose in the range of the observed study. At dose $10$, the predictor is $0.05$ inverse seconds and the mean time is $20$ seconds. At dose $20$, the predictor is $0.08$ and the mean time is $12.5$ seconds. Doubling this dose has shortened the fitted mean by $7.5$ seconds, but the slope $0.003$ itself is a change in inverse seconds per dose unit. The local mean slope at dose $10$ is $-0.003/(0.05)^2=-1.2$ seconds per dose unit; at other doses it differs. The model gives $50$ seconds at dose zero, making its low-dose behaviour explicit.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§3.4, p. 380, inverse gamma mean form)

**Your turn.** A second model has $1/\mu(d)=0.04+0.002d$. Find the fitted means at doses $5$ and $15$, and their ratio. What is the mean’s derivative at dose $5$?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
At dose $5$, $\eta=0.05$ and $\mu=20$. At dose $15$, $\eta=0.07$ and $\mu=100/7\approx14.286$. The second mean divided by the first is $5/7\approx0.7143$. At dose $5$, $d\mu/dd=-0.002/(0.05)^2=-0.8$ seconds per dose unit. The ratio is specific to these doses because a reciprocal link has no constant multiplicative effect.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§3.4, p. 380, inverse mean form)
:::

## Common misconceptions
“Inverse link” does not mean applying a reciprocal to every link: the inverse of the logit maps a predictor to a probability by a logistic curve. A reciprocal coefficient is not an absolute change in mean time; $d\mu/dx$ varies with $x$. A gamma distribution does not force the reciprocal link, since a log link can be chosen with the same gamma variance family. Finally, a canonical choice does not validate predictions beyond the region where $\eta>0$. These are different errors about names, coefficient interpretation, distribution choice and domain.{{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Arguments, link names; Value, linkinv and valideta)

## Assumptions and limits
The domain restriction can fail inside a study as well as during extrapolation. With $\eta(x)=0.5-0.1x$, $x=4$ gives a legitimate mean of $10$, $x=5$ makes the mean undefined and $x=6$ gives the impossible formal value $-10$. Even values just below $x=5$ create implausibly huge times. A link whose legal predictor region is narrow can therefore be sensitive to uncertainty in fitted coefficients. If observed variance rises far faster or slower than $\mu^2$, changing the link alone does not repair a gamma variance assumption. A log link is a practical alternative when the mean must be positive throughout a broad covariate domain, but its constant multiplicative effects are a different substantive assumption.{{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Usage and Arguments, Gamma links and variance; Value, valideta)

## History
Nelder and Wedderburn’s 1972 formulation separated an exponential-family random component, a linear predictor and a linking function. Their gamma section identified the inverse transformation of the mean with that family’s natural-parameter construction. The distinction let later software offer the same response family with different links. R’s family interface now exposes both `linkfun` and `linkinv`, and lists inverse, identity and log as gamma link choices.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§1.3, p. 372; §3.4, pp. 380–381){{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Usage and Arguments; Value, linkfun and linkinv)
