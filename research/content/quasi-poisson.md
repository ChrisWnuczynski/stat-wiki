## Motivating example — Extra scatter around a usable log-linear mean
Two groups of machines may have failure counts whose averages follow a clear log-linear pattern but whose scatter exceeds the Poisson prediction. A quasi-Poisson model keeps the regression mean and allows variance to be a constant multiple of it. This can preserve useful coefficient estimates while widening uncertainty to reflect extra variation. The mean stays $\mu$ and the variance becomes $\phi\mu$, with the log link unchanged.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.1, eqs. 6.1–6.2, pp. 193–194){{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§5.4){{cite:ver_hoef_boveng2007}} (p. 2767, eq. 1)

## Intuition — A variance factor is not a new mean
A Poisson regression has conditional variance equal to its conditional mean. Quasi-Poisson replaces that equality by $\operatorname{Var}(Y_i\mid x_i)=\phi\mu_i$. The factor $\phi$ changes the spread, not the expected count. When $\phi>1$, overdispersion makes the same fitted effect less precise than a Poisson standard error would suggest; when $0<\phi<1$, the assumed spread is smaller. The second of those cases is the less common one, written $\sigma^2<1$ in the log-linear development.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.1, p. 194) The formula describes two moments, so it does not by itself assign probabilities to individual count values.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§9.1, pp. 323–324){{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§5.4) Ver Hoef and Boveng's statement of the quasi-Poisson variance takes the multiplier greater than one, so it covers the overdispersed side of the same moment model.{{cite:ver_hoef_boveng2007}} (p. 2767, eq. 1)

## Definition — Mean $\mu$ and variance $\phi\mu$, with no unique mass function
For count observations with $\mu_i=\exp(x_i^\top\beta)>0$, the quasi-Poisson specification is $E(Y_i\mid x_i)=\mu_i$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y_i\mid x_i)=\phi\mu_i$, with one common $\phi>0$. A log exposure offset can be included when observation times or populations differ: the log of an exposure enters with coefficient fixed at $1$.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.3.2, eq. 6.5, p. 206) The variance function is $V(\mu)=\mu$; specifying it and the mean supports a quasi-score, not a unique count probability mass function. The quasi-likelihood attached to $V(\mu)=\mu$ is $y\log\mu-\mu$, the Poisson kernel, without a claim that the mass function is Poisson.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§9.2.2, p. 326) At $\phi=1$ the first two moments match Poisson regression, while the quasi specification alone still does not assert the full Poisson law.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§5.4){{cite:ver_hoef_boveng2007}} (p. 2767)

## Interpretation — The same coefficients, standard errors times $\sqrt{\phi}$
With the same data, design, link and unpenalised fitting rule, a common $\phi$ divides the Poisson score by a positive scalar. The quasi-score is $U(\boldsymbol{\beta})=D^\top V^{-1}(Y-\mu)/\sigma^2$, so a dispersion that does not depend on $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ cannot move the root.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§9.2.3, eq. 9.5, p. 327) Thus the fitted coefficient vector is the same whenever the score has a well-defined solution. Its usual model-based covariance is $\sigma^2(D^\top V^{-1}D)^{-1}$, and each standard error is multiplied by $\sqrt{\phi}$, not by $\phi$.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§9.2.3, eq. 9.6, p. 327; §6.2.3–6.2.4, pp. 199–200) A ratio of expected counts remains $e^{\beta_j}$ for a one-unit change in $x_j$ at fixed other covariates and exposure; overdispersion changes confidence in that ratio, not its point value.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§5.4)

## Properties — Pearson dispersion and why AIC does not apply
The Pearson dispersion estimate is $\widehat\sigma^2=X^2/(n-p)$, where $X^2=\sum_i(y_i-\mu_i)^2/\mu_i$ is evaluated at the fitted mean and $p$ is the number of fitted coefficients.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.2.4, eq. 6.4, p. 200) It measures excess or reduced residual variation against the fitted mean and variance shape. Its reliability depends on the mean model, independence and enough residual information. Because quasi-Poisson has no fully specified likelihood, ordinary likelihood-ratio tests and AIC cannot simply be reused after multiplying standard errors. Information criteria that need a full distributional likelihood do not choose between a quasi-Poisson fit and a negative-binomial fit: the quasi model is characterised only by its mean and variance.{{cite:ver_hoef_boveng2007}} (p. 2767){{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§5.3.1, Pearson dispersion; §5.4)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
For $\mu_i=e^{x_i^\top\beta}$, differentiation gives $\partial\mu_i/\partial\beta=\mu_ix_i$. In the quasi-score, $U(\beta)=\sum_i (Y_i-\mu_i)(\phi\mu_i)^{-1}\partial\mu_i/\partial\beta=\phi^{-1}\sum_i x_i(Y_i-\mu_i)$.
The final sum is the Poisson log-link score, so a positive common multiplier cannot alter its zeros. Under the independent working covariance $\operatorname{diag}(\phi\mu_i)$, the model-based inverse information is $\phi(X^\top W X)^{-1}$ with $W=\operatorname{diag}(\mu_i)$, giving the square-root standard-error factor. This covariance statement requires the working moments and adequate rank.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§9.2.3, eqs. 9.5–9.6, p. 327){{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§5.4)
:::
:::

## Statistical use — Extra-Poisson variation
Begin with the count, its exposure and the covariates that determine its mean. Fit the log-mean equation, examine residual variation against fitted means, and estimate $\phi$ only after checking for missing covariates, clustering or a wrong mean shape. Report coefficient estimates alongside dispersion-adjusted uncertainty. If the research question requires probabilities of zero counts, simulated new counts or a genuine likelihood comparison, choose and check a full count distribution, such as an appropriate negative-binomial model.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§5.4){{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§9.1, pp. 323–324)

If one equipment cluster fails together, counts on its machines are dependent; multiplying independent-case variances by one common $\phi$ does not model the covariance. If the mean has a sharp age threshold but the regression forces a straight log-mean line, correcting dispersion leaves systematic bias. If variance grows roughly as $\mu^2$ rather than $\mu$, a constant multiplier of $\mu$ fits the wrong variance shape. The negative binomial variance $\mu+\kappa\mu^2$ weights large counts differently from $\phi\mu$, because the iteratively weighted least-squares weights level off as the mean grows instead of staying proportional to it.{{cite:ver_hoef_boveng2007}} (p. 2767, eq. 2; p. 2768){{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.2.3, p. 199) And a question about the chance of zero failures needs a full distribution, which this moment model does not supply.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§5.4)

## Worked example — Two groups, a factor of nine, and an unchanged ratio
Suppose equal-exposure groups have observed counts $(0,0,6)$ and $(0,0,12)$. Their fitted group means in a two-level log-mean model are $2$ and $4$, so the coefficient for the second group is $\log(4/2)=\log2\approx0.69315$ and its expected-count ratio is $2$. The Pearson residual sum is $12$ for the first group and $24$ for the second: for example, the first contributes $2+2+8=12$. With six observations and two fitted coefficients, $\widehat\phi=(12+24)/(6-2)=9$. If the Poisson standard error for the group coefficient is $0.20$, the quasi-Poisson version is $\sqrt9(0.20)=0.60$. The point ratio stays $2$; its uncertainty grows. Four zero counts also invite a check of the mean and count mechanism, and six observations make the dispersion estimate fragile.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§5.3.1, Pearson dispersion; §5.4)

**Your turn.** In another fit, $\widehat\phi=4$ and a coefficient's Poisson standard error is $0.15$. Find the quasi-Poisson standard error and say whether the common dispersion changes the fitted coefficient.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The standard error is $\sqrt4(0.15)=0.30$. With the same mean model and one common $\phi$, the quasi-score is only a constant multiple of the Poisson score, so the coefficient estimate remains the same. A different variance shape or observation-specific dispersion would not give this cancellation.
:::

## Common misconceptions — Quasi-Poisson is not a distribution to simulate from
Quasi-Poisson is not a probability distribution from which one can simulate merely by choosing $\mu$ and $\phi$. The first two moments do not determine the mass function.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§9.1, pp. 323–324){{cite:ver_hoef_boveng2007}} (p. 2767) Dispersion greater than one does not change the fitted mean if the quasi-score is just the rescaled Poisson score. Standard errors scale by $\sqrt\phi$, not $\phi$. And extra variation does not prove a particular cause: omitted predictors, dependence and latent heterogeneity can all produce it.{{cite:wuthrich_merz2022_glm}} (§5.4)

## History — Wedderburn’s 1974 quasi-likelihood
Wedderburn introduced quasi-likelihood in 1974. The extension of the second-moment assumption to generalised linear models uses that idea: it is necessary to know how the variance of each observation changes with its mean, and it is not necessary to specify the distribution in its entirety.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§1.2.2, p. 9; §1.3, p. 18) McCullagh and Nelder record that the term seems first to have been used in this context then.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§9.7, p. 352) Ver Hoef and Boveng describe quasi-Poisson regression, which they attribute to that introduction, as characterised by its first two moments.{{cite:ver_hoef_boveng2007}} (pp. 2766–2767) A precursor is Fisher's analysis of tuberculin measurements: he took the variance to be proportional to the mean and estimated the effects as if the observations were Poisson, with the systematic part linear on the log scale.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§3.1, p. 377)
