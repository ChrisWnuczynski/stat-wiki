## Lead context

Counting a sample and naming a population still leaves a mathematical question: which functions of the sample are entitled to stand for which parameters, and what can be proved about those functions before any particular sample arrives. Mathematical statistics is that question, pursued with the language of probability. It is where an estimator is separated from the number it happens to return, where bias and efficiency are defined as properties of the procedure, and where a test is calibrated from a sampling distribution rather than from the look of one table.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 184–185, Estimation and Estimator)

The subject is the common foundation under the procedures that applied fields then specialise. A clinical trial, a price index, a forecast interval, and a classifier all use estimators, intervals, or tests. Mathematical statistics is the place where those objects are defined for a model, and where the theorems that justify a standard error or a critical value are stated with their assumptions visible.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, §5.1.1)

## Definition

Mathematical statistics is the mathematical development of methods that pass from a sample to statements about a population. Estimation is the procedure that determines the value of a parameter associated with a population: a sample is drawn, and the value of an estimator is calculated. The theory splits that procedure into point estimation, which returns a number, and interval estimation, which returns a set. An estimator is any statistical function of the sample used to estimate an unknown parameter. The number it produces in one sample is an estimate. The function and the number are different objects, and the theory is about the function.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 184–185, Estimation and Estimator)

Inference, in the same encyclopedic sketch, is reasoning by induction from information collected on a sample. Estimation and hypothesis testing are the two large constructions inside that reasoning. Descriptive calculation can stop at indicators computed on the sample that was seen. Mathematical statistics asks what those indicators do when the sample is regarded as random and the population parameter is fixed but unknown.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 263, Inference)

A first course meets the subject as a point estimate, a standard error, and a reference distribution. The sample mean of a poll is a point estimate of a population proportion. The standard error describes the variability of that estimate across samples of the same size, and the central limit theorem supplies a normal approximation for the sampling distribution when the sample is large enough and the observations are independent. A second course meets the same project as theorems: an estimator is unbiased when its expectation equals the parameter, efficient when its variance is smaller than that of any other unbiased estimator, and consistent when the probability that it differs from the parameter by more than a fixed positive amount goes to one as the sample size grows. Both courses are one subject at different depths.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 185, Estimator){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, §§5.1.1–5.1.3)

## Interpretation

The probabilities in this theory are properties of the procedure, computed before the sample is seen, or computed as if the experiment were repeated under the same model. A confidence interval with a stated coverage is a set-valued procedure whose long-run hit rate, under the model, is the nominal level. A test with a stated type I error is a rule whose probability of rejecting a true null equals that error, again under the model. Neither number is the probability that a particular realised interval contains the parameter, and neither is the probability that a particular null is true. Reading them that way changes the interpretation from a property of the method to a probability statement about the parameter.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, §5.1.2){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 183, Type I and Type II error)

Unbiasedness is an expectation identity, not a statement that the estimate in hand equals the parameter. An unbiased estimator of a mean can still be far from the mean in a small sample. Efficiency compares variances among estimators that share the same expectation. Consistency is a large-sample claim: it says the estimator concentrates, and it does not by itself give the rate, the shape of the error, or a usable interval. The central limit theorem is the usual extra ingredient that turns a consistent average into an approximate normal error with a standard error that can be estimated from the sample. The approximation is for the sampling distribution of the estimator. It is available when the technical conditions named with the theorem hold, including independence and a sample large enough for the skewness of the observations to be washed out.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, §5.1.3){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 185, consistent estimator)

## Statistical use

The working products are the procedures other pages define one by one. An [estimator](estimator.qmd) is a statistic aimed at a parameter. The [bias of an estimator](bias-of-an-estimator.qmd) is the gap between its expectation and the target. A [confidence interval](confidence-interval.qmd) is a set-valued procedure with a coverage probability. A test uses a [critical region](critical-region.qmd) whose probability under the null is the type I error. [Maximum likelihood](maximum-likelihood.qmd) and the [method of moments](method-of-moments.qmd) are two constructions whose properties the theory then studies. [Ordinary least squares](ordinary-least-squares.qmd) is the construction for a mean that is linear in its coefficients.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 184–185){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, §5.1.1)

When an exact sampling distribution is unavailable, the theory supplies limit theorems. The law of large numbers is the concentration claim. The central limit theorem supplies the normal shape for an average, and smooth functions of an average inherit a normal limit by the delta method once the average does. Those approximations are what make a standard error and a normal critical value usable for a proportion, for a mean, and for many regression coefficients. The same approximations are the reason a first course can teach one critical value and then reuse it. Reuse is legitimate only inside the conditions that were used to prove the limit.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, §5.1.3)

The theory also marks its own boundary. A procedure proved unbiased under independence and a correct mean is not thereby unbiased under a different sampling scheme. A large-sample normal limit does not repair a parameter that the likelihood cannot identify. A test whose type I error was computed for one null does not answer a different null. Using the theorems means keeping the assumptions that were used to prove them, and reporting the estimator, the interval, and the test as procedures with those assumptions attached.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 185, Estimator){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, §5.1.2)

## History

The name is attached, in the standard historical sketch of estimation, to the first mathematical treatments of the passage from observations to a parameter. Jacob Bernoulli’s *Ars Conjectandi* (1713) is the early probability of causes in that list. Laplace’s memoir on the probability of causes (1774) and Daniel Bernoulli’s comparison of discrepant observations (1778) continue it. Least squares, associated with Legendre (1805) and Gauss (1809), gave a computational rule for combining observations. Pearson’s method of moments in the 1890s estimated the constants of a frequency curve from sample moments. Fisher’s 1912 absolute criterion for fitting frequency curves introduced maximum likelihood. The 1922 paper on the mathematical foundations of theoretical statistics and the 1925 paper on the theory of statistical estimation then separated bias, efficiency, and sufficiency from the mere computing of a formula.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 184–185, Estimation)

Testing grew on a parallel track. Pearson’s chi-squared criterion of 1900, Gosset’s *t* distribution for a mean under the name Student (1908), and the Neyman–Pearson theory of tests gave hypothesis testing its sampling-theory form. By the middle of the twentieth century the subject had textbooks whose title was mathematical statistics and whose chapters were estimation, hypothesis testing, and the distributions required to carry them out. Introductions today still open the same chapters, with computing changed and the logical order intact: a point estimate, the variability of that estimate across samples, and a limit theorem that turns the variability into a reference distribution.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 263, Inference){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, §§5.1.1–5.1.3)
