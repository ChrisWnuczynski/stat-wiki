## Lead context

A finance ministry, a central bank, and a newspaper can each quote a growth rate and still be talking about different things: output of firms, income of households, or sales across the border. The system of national accounts is the agreement that keeps those stories in one ledger. It is a statistical framework for measuring economic activity, not a theory of what governments ought to maximise. Countries at very different levels of development are expected to be able to use the same concepts, and other economic statistics are supposed to line up with them.{{cite:sna2008}} (§1.1, p. 1){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 394)

## Definition — Scope

The System of National Accounts is the internationally agreed standard set of recommendations on how to compile measures of economic activity, under strict accounting conventions based on economic principles. The recommendations are a set of concepts, definitions, classifications, and accounting rules. Gross domestic product is defined inside that standard. It is the most frequently quoted indicator of economic performance, and it is not the only purpose of the accounts. The framework is meant to show the interactions among sectors, not only a single total for the whole economy. It can be implemented for an individual institutional unit, for a sector of such units, or for the total economy.{{cite:sna2008}} (§1.1, p. 1)

The accounts are required to be comprehensive, consistent, and integrated. Comprehensive means that the designated activities, and their consequences for the agents in the economy, are covered. Consistent means that one action is given the same value for every party, under the same rules. Integrated means that the consequences of one action, including the change in wealth recorded on a balance sheet, appear together. The accounts are compiled for a succession of periods, so they are a flow of information about performance over time and also a statement of assets and liabilities at a date. An external account records the links with the rest of the world.{{cite:sna2008}} (§1.1–§1.2, pp. 1–2)

Within official statistics, national accounts are one domain among others. National accounting and the balance of payments sit beside prices, production, public finance, employment, and population, not in place of them. The accounts describe the economy as a whole. They do not replace a household survey or a population census. They inherit the definitions of industry, sector, and residence that the standard has fixed. Satellite accounts and input–output tables extend the same framework when a question, such as a particular industry or a social purpose, needs a more detailed matrix.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 394){{cite:sna2008}} (§1.1, p. 1)

## Interpretation

At the centre of the system is the production of goods and services. Some of that production is consumed in the period. Some is accumulated for later use. In simple terms, the value added generated by production is gross domestic product, and the income corresponding to that product is distributed and redistributed so that one agent can consume what another produced, or can acquire goods for later consumption. The system captures this by naming the institutional units and by giving each stage of production, distribution, and use its own account.{{cite:sna2008}} (§1.6, p. 2)

Two kinds of institutional unit are distinguished: households, and legal entities. Legal entities are corporations and non-profit institutions, created for production, or government units, created by political processes. An institutional unit can own goods and assets, incur liabilities, and engage in transactions in its own right. Resident units are grouped into five mutually exclusive sectors: non-financial corporations, financial corporations, government units including social security funds, non-profit institutions serving households, and households. Together the five sectors are the total economy. Units resident abroad are the rest of the world. The system does not require a full set of accounts for activity that takes place abroad, but every transaction between a resident and a non-resident is recorded, so that the behaviour of residents is complete.{{cite:sna2008}} (§1.9–§1.11, p. 2)

The goods and services account rests on an identity. Goods and services produced in the economy must be consumed, used for capital formation, or exported, and goods and services used in the economy must have been produced there or imported, once taxes and subsidies on products are allowed for. From that identity the account, and thence gross domestic product, is derived. The production account records producing activity. Its balancing item, gross value added, is output minus intermediate consumption. It is the contribution to gross domestic product of a producer, an industry, or a sector, and it is the source of the primary incomes that the next account distributes. Value added and gross domestic product can also be measured net, by deducting consumption of fixed capital.{{cite:sna2008}} (§1.13 and §1.17, p. 3)

A rise in expenditure is not, by itself, a rise in quantity. Price and volume have to be separated before a growth rate is read as news about output. A labour-force survey can disagree with the employment total in the accounts because the units and the reference periods differ. The discrepancy is a reason to publish both methods. Averaging the two numbers in silence would hide the difference of definition.{{cite:sna2008}} (§1.17, p. 3){{cite:un_res68261}} (Principle 3)

## Statistical use

Compiling the accounts is an exercise in adding inconsistent sources until they balance. Business surveys, tax records, trade statistics, and price indices are reconciled so that the production, income, and expenditure descriptions of the same economy do not contradict one another without explanation. Transactions are the basic source. A market transaction supplies a price, and a transaction between two units is recorded for both, which is what makes the linkages checkable. [Time series](time-series.qmd) of quarterly accounts are benchmarked to annual totals. [Seasonality](seasonality.qmd) is often removed before a short-term movement is treated as a change in the underlying path.{{cite:sna2008}} (§1.8, p. 2)

Some countries, at least at first, can calculate only a small number of accounts for the total economy, with little sector detail. A reduced set is not an alternative system. The concepts are meant to be the same in a less developed economy and in a more developed one, in a closed economy and in an open one, and in a high-inflation economy and in a low-inflation one. A definition may be idle in a particular country, for example if there is no inflation, and a general system still carries the definition. Priorities for which table to compile first are set country by country, by people who know that country's needs. They are not a second, smaller standard.{{cite:sna2008}} (§1.4–§1.5, p. 1)

International concepts are what make one country's accounts comparable with another's. The Fundamental Principles ask statistical agencies to use international concepts, classifications, and methods, and to present sources, methods, and procedures so that the figures can be interpreted. They also ask agencies to decide on methods according to professional considerations, including scientific principles and professional ethics. A growth rate that moves because a minister dislikes it has left that standard.{{cite:un_res68261}} (Principles 2, 3, and 9){{cite:sna2008}} (§1.1, p. 1)

## History

Counting people and goods for a state is ancient. Statisticians in the modern sense appear in the late seventeenth century, with the modern state and with the administrative needs of France and England. In the eighteenth century the same activity was described as political arithmetic, the application of numerical calculation to political subjects. Regular decennial censuses in the United States began in 1790, when the new state needed to know the political weight of its parts. After the Second World War, new national states built statistical systems as a condition of elections, assemblies, and modern corporations. The United Nations, and in particular its Statistical Commission, promoted official statistics, and organised world population censuses in 1950 and 1960. National accounts, including the balance of payments, are one of the domains that this official apparatus now treats as standard, beside prices, production, and public finance.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 393–394)

The 2008 system is the agreed standard for that domain. It states the concepts, the five sectors, the production identity, and the rule that the same definitions apply across very different economies. Official statistics, in the encyclopedia's account, have always had a political boundary: power decides how far the study of unemployment, poverty, or well-being extends. The principles adopted by the General Assembly in 2014 answer that pressure with professional independence, published methods, and international classifications. The accounts are usable as a common ledger only while those conditions hold.{{cite:sna2008}} (§1.1 and §1.9, pp. 1–2){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 394){{cite:un_res68261}} (Principles 2 and 9)

## Connections

The accounts are a product of [official statistics](official-statistics.qmd). They neighbour public finance, price statistics, and the balance of payments, which Dodge lists in the same map of official domains. A household survey and a census remain separate collections. The accounts use them, and they do not replace them. A [time series](time-series.qmd) of quarterly totals, and the [seasonality](seasonality.qmd) taken out of it before a growth rate is announced, are the forms in which most users meet the system. The identity that goods produced are consumed, invested, or exported is the accounting reason those announcements can be checked against one another.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 394){{cite:sna2008}} (§1.13, p. 3)
