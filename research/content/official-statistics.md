## Lead context

A government that taxes, builds, and holds elections needs a shared description of its population and economy. Counting soldiers and stores is ancient. The modern practice is a public system: agencies collect data under law, publish the figures, and are expected to do so without favouring the government of the day. Those figures are official statistics. They are used to allocate seats, track unemployment, and compare countries, so the rules that govern them are part of the measurement. A number that leaves an official office still has a definition, a source, and a coverage, and those three facts are what a user needs before the number can be compared with another year or another country.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 393–394, Official statistics){{cite:un_res68261}} (Principle 1)

## Definition

Official statistics are statistics compiled and released by a public statistical agency, or by a body working for the state, about the economic, demographic, social, and environmental situation. The adjective *official* means coming from the government or authorised by a proper authority. The term covers a general concept that can be obtained in different ways, including a full count and a sample. Principle 1 of the Fundamental Principles states the public purpose: official statistics provide an indispensable element in the information system of a democratic society, serving the government, the economy, and the public with data about that situation, compiled and made available on an impartial basis.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 393, Official statistics){{cite:un_res68261}} (Principle 1)

The same idea excludes a private poll or a firm's internal dashboard, unless a statistical law has given that source a role in the official system. Principle 5 allows agencies to draw on surveys or on administrative records, choosing the source for quality, timeliness, cost, and the burden on respondents. Principle 6 then closes the other door: individual data collected for statistical compilation, whether they refer to natural or legal persons, are strictly confidential and are to be used exclusively for statistical purposes. A file that was collected for a tax or a benefit does not become a public microdata set merely because a statistician touched it.{{cite:un_res68261}} (Principles 5 and 6)

What sits inside the boundary is itself a political choice. History shows a mutual dependence between statistics and the evolution of the state, and political power determines the limits of the study, expanding or limiting the contents. Definitions of unemployment, poverty, wellness, and professional categories can be widened or narrowed, and the published level moves with the definition. That dependence is why the principles separate the choice of methods, which Principle 2 assigns to professional and scientific considerations, from the political demand for a particular headline.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 394–395, Domains and limitations){{cite:un_res68261}} (Principle 2)

## Properties

The basic operation is still a [census](census.qmd), a count of every unit in a stated population. The United States has taken a regular decennial census since 1790, begun so that the new republic could weigh its parts. Between censuses, agencies estimate totals from samples. [Stratified and cluster sampling](stratified-and-cluster-sampling.qmd) and post-collection weighting are the usual tools when a full enumeration is too slow or too costly. Repeated monthly and quarterly series are [time series](time-series.qmd); [seasonality](seasonality.qmd) is removed when the published headline is meant to show the underlying movement rather than the calendar. Price indices and national accounts turn many micro-records into a few macroeconomic totals.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 393–394)

The published domains are wide. Population and migration, the environment, employment, national accounts, prices, production and trade, agriculture, energy, construction, tourism, transport, financial markets, insurance, health, education, and public finance are the lists a standard sketch gives. An indicator programme, such as the one the OECD has pursued since the 1960s, tries to turn that output into a compact briefing for policy. Principle 9 asks agencies to use international concepts, classifications, and methods so that those totals can be compared across countries. The comparison still fails when the underlying choices differ: needs are not the same in every state, and international statistics are not always comparable even when the words match.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 394–395, Examples and comparability){{cite:un_res68261}} (Principle 9)

Two properties follow from the legal frame rather than from a sampling formula. Impartiality is the requirement that the figures be compiled without favouring a user, including the government that funds the agency. Confidentiality is the requirement that identifiable records stay inside the statistical purpose. A system can be impartial and still be wrong if the frame is incomplete. A system can be accurate at the aggregate and still be a misuse of the law if the microdata are released for a non-statistical purpose. The principles treat those as separate duties.{{cite:un_res68261}} (Principles 1 and 6){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 395, Objectivity)

## History

Kings in Babylon, Egypt, China, Greece, Persia, and Rome counted people and goods when they needed armies or taxes. Those counts were sporadic. Statisticians in the later sense appear in the late seventeenth century, with the modern state and colonial administration in France and England. Diderot and d'Alembert's encyclopedia described arithmetical politics as the application of arithmetical calculations to political subjects. The French Revolution and the birth of the United States justified a further step: regular decennial censuses in the United States began in 1790 so that the new republic could know the political weight of its parts.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 393, History)

After the Second World War, empires and colonies were replaced by national states that needed elections, assemblies, and a description of their economies. The United Nations, and in particular its Statistical Commission, promoted national statistical systems. Under UN auspices the first world population census programme was organised in 1950, with another in 1960. In 1994 the Statistical Commission adopted ten Fundamental Principles. The General Assembly endorsed them on 29 January 2014, in resolution 68/261, after the Economic and Social Council had reaffirmed them in 2013. The resolution's preamble ties public trust to the professional independence of the system. Principle 2 assigns methods to scientific considerations. Principle 10 treats bilateral and multilateral cooperation as part of improving the systems themselves.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 393–394){{cite:un_res68261}} (Principles 2 and 10)

## Connections

Official statistics sit next to demography, social statistics, and the [system of national accounts](system-of-national-accounts.qmd). [Public health](public-health.qmd) uses the same agencies for denominators: population, births, and deaths. A sample survey and a census answer different questions; publishing one as if it were the other is a failure of method. The same machinery can mislead when a definition is changed without a clear note, which is why Principle 4 gives agencies a right to comment on erroneous interpretation and [misuse of statistics](misuse-of-statistics.qmd). Confidentiality constraints are also why a public microdata file is not an automatic success of [open science](open-science.qmd): individual records collected for statistical compilation are to be used only for statistical purposes. The figure a user quotes is the aggregate the agency published, together with the method the agency was obliged to show.{{cite:un_res68261}} (Principles 3, 4, and 6){{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 394–395)
