## Motivating example
A temporary disturbance to a stationary autoregression has an effect that decays over time. In a random walk, that disturbance becomes part of every later level. This persistence changes both long-run variation and forecasting uncertainty. Differencing a random walk isolates its increments, which can be stationary even though the level is not.{{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§9.1, “Stationarity and differencing”, random walk model)

## Definition — The ordinary root at one
For an autoregression written $\phi(B)X_t=\varepsilon_t$, a root of $\phi(z)$ on the unit circle is a unit root. The common nonseasonal case is a root at $z=1$, so $\phi(B)$ contains a factor $(1-B)$. If that factor occurs once and the remaining factors describe a stable stationary process, one ordinary difference removes that root.{{cite:brockwell_davis2016}} (§6.3, pp. 169–171) Seasonal or repeated unit roots require the corresponding factors to be addressed as well. For the AR(1) polynomial $1-\varphi z$, a root at one means $\varphi=1$.{{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§§9.1–9.3, differencing, backshift notation and autoregressive models)

Autocorrelation compares values of a time series separated by a lag. It therefore summarises a different object from the level variance at a particular time. The covariance calculations below show explicitly why a random-walk level cannot be summarised by a stationary lag-only covariance.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 24, Autocorrelation)

## Properties — Accumulated innovations determine variance
Let $X_t=X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t$, with fixed $X_0$, zero-mean uncorrelated innovations and positive common innovation variance $\sigma^2$. Iteration gives $X_t=X_0+\sum_{j=1}^t\varepsilon_j$. Variances therefore add to $t\sigma^2$. For $s\le t$, the covariance is $s\sigma^2$, which depends on both times rather than just their lag. This directly rules out weak stationarity of the levels. The first difference equals $\varepsilon_t$, so it has the stable innovation variance. The random-walk representation makes the distinction between levels and changes explicit.{{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§9.1, random walk model){{cite:shumway_stoffer2017}} (§1.4, p. 22)

For the linear $h$-step forecast using the most recent observation, the error is a sum of $h$ future innovations, with unconditional variance $h\sigma^2$ under the stated assumptions, so the forecast-error standard deviation is $\sigma\sqrt h$: the variance grows in proportion to the horizon, while the standard deviation grows only with its square root, and the two must not be interchanged. If innovations are independent of the observed past, the same expression is the conditional forecast-error variance. A drift term adds $h$ times the drift to the point forecast but does not remove the accumulation of innovation variance. Forecast uncertainty widens with horizon even when the increment model is simple.{{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§9.8, “Forecasting”){{cite:shumway_stoffer2017}} (§3.6, p. 135)

## Worked example — A series with stable changes and unstable levels
Take innovation variance four and $X_0=0$. The level variances at times one, five and twenty are $4,20,80$. The variance of each one-step increment is four. A five-step forecast error has standard deviation $\sqrt{20}\approx4.472136$, while a twenty-step error has standard deviation $\sqrt{80}\approx8.944272$. Four times the forecast horizon doubles the standard deviation.

A stationary AR(1) with coefficient $0.8$ and the same innovation variance has stationary variance $4/(1-0.8^2)=100/9\approx11.111111$. Its finite variance illustrates the discontinuity at coefficient one: substituting one into that stationary formula does not yield a finite stationary variance.

**Your turn.** If the random walk has drift $0.5$ and its latest value is ten, what is the three-step point forecast and forecast-error variance?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The forecast is $10+3(0.5)=11.5$. With innovation variance four, the forecast-error variance is twelve.
:::

## Statistical use — Test the assumption that matters
A slow-decaying sample autocorrelation can motivate a stationarity investigation, but it is not a proof of a unit root. Unit-root and stationarity tests have different null hypotheses and nonstandard or model-specific calibrations. For example, the KPSS test uses stationarity as its null, while a Dickey–Fuller-type test takes a unit root as its null. Always state the deterministic specification — no constant, a constant only, or a constant plus a linear trend — together with the differencing convention and the null being tested, before interpreting its p-value, because the reference distribution differs across those specifications.{{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§9.1, “Unit root tests”){{cite:dickey_fuller1979}} (pp. 427–428, equations (1.1) and (2.1)–(2.2))

## Assumptions and limits — Level regressions can be misleading
Independent persistent series can yield apparently strong fitted relationships and ordinary t statistics whose usual stationary regression interpretation is invalid. A fitted line through their levels does not establish a shared generating mechanism. Inspecting residual dependence and considering a suitable model for differences are part of the analysis; genuine long-run relations require their own conditions. The spurious-regression issue is documented in the econometric literature.{{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§10.1, “Estimation”, spurious regression)

## Intuition
A stable AR(1) forgets a shock geometrically. An autoregression with coefficient $0.8$ multiplies that shock by $0.8$ at the next time, by $0.8^2$ at the time after, and so on. The weights are summable, and the marginal variance settles at $\sigma^2/(1-0.8^2)$. With innovation variance $4$ that stationary variance is $100/9\approx 11.111111$. The root at $z=1$ is the boundary where that decay stops: the weight on a past shock is $+1$ at every future time. The shock is added into the level and never multiplied by a factor smaller than one, so each impulse pushes all later levels the same way and never leaves. A root at $z=-1$ lies on the same unit circle but is a different boundary: the AR(1) coefficient there is $\varphi=-1$, and a shock enters the level $h$ steps later with weight $(-1)^h$, flipping sign at every step instead of accumulating in one direction. The two roots are both nonstationary boundaries, but only the $+1$ root is the random walk whose impulses accumulate permanently and in the same direction. The variance of the level is then the sum of the innovation variances still in memory, which is every innovation since the process started. With a fixed start at zero, that sum is $t\sigma^2$ and grows without a finite ceiling.

Differencing is the arithmetic that isolates the innovation. If $X_t=X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t$, then $X_t-X_{t-1}=\varepsilon_t$. The differences can be stationary, uncorrelated, and of constant variance while the levels are none of those things. Forecasting the level puts the future innovations back in. An $h$-step forecast error under this random walk is a sum of $h$ innovations and has variance $h\sigma^2$. The forecast fans out as the horizon grows. A drift $0.5$ shifts the point forecast by $0.5h$ and leaves that error variance unchanged.

### Properties — Covariance depends on the earlier time
For $s\le t$ the random walk shares the first $s$ innovations, so $\operatorname{Cov}(X_s,X_t)=s\sigma^2$ when $X_0$ is fixed and the innovations are uncorrelated with variance $\sigma^2$. The correlation is $\sqrt{s/t}$. At times $5$ and $20$ with $\sigma^2=4$, the covariance is $5\cdot 4=20$ and the correlation is $\sqrt{5/20}=1/2$. A stationary series would have a covariance that depends on the lag $t-s$ alone. Here the lag is $15$ in this pair, and a pair with the same lag earlier in the series, such as times $1$ and $16$, has covariance $1\cdot 4=4$, not $20$. The lag does not determine the covariance. Weak stationarity fails for that reason, before any test is computed.

A unit root of the autoregressive polynomial is the same boundary written as an equation. The AR(1) polynomial is $1-\varphi z$. A root at $z=1$ means $\varphi=1$. Seasonal unit roots are roots at other points on the unit circle. A seasonal difference removes only the roots contained in its own differencing factor, each with the multiplicity that factor carries: $(1-B^s)$ contains each of the $s$ seasonal roots once, so a seasonal root of higher multiplicity needs the factor applied again, and roots outside the factor are untouched. An ordinary first difference alone does not remove a seasonal factor: applying it to a series that still has a seasonal unit root leaves that seasonal factor in the polynomial.

## Common misconceptions
A slowly decaying sample autocorrelation is a prompt to consider a unit root. It is also what a stationary autoregression with coefficient $0.8$ produces at moderate lags: the population autocorrelation at lag $k$ is $0.8^k$. At lag $10$ that value is about $0.107$. Distinguishing a slow geometric decay from a unit root is a question about the model and the sample length. The sample correlogram does not settle it by eye.

Tests differ in which statement they treat as the null. A Dickey–Fuller-type statistic is built so that the null is a unit root, with a nonstandard reference distribution in every specification of the usual calibrations under their standard null restrictions: estimating no constant, a constant alone, or a constant plus a trend changes which nonstandard distribution applies, not whether the distribution is nonstandard. The scoping matters, because Dickey and Fuller (§3.2, p. 429) themselves distinguish cases with certain true nonzero deterministic terms, where the limiting distribution is normal instead. The KPSS statistic is built so that the null is stationarity. Rejecting KPSS is a decision against that stationarity null. It is not numerically the same event as rejecting a unit-root null, and a failure to reject a unit-root null is not an estimate of the autoregressive coefficient. The deterministic terms included in the regression, the differencing already applied, and the null that was actually tested all belong in the report.{{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§9.1, “Unit root tests”){{cite:dickey_fuller1979}} (p. 429, §3.2){{cite:shumway_stoffer2017}} (§5.2, p. 252)

## Connections
The partial-sum process of a random walk is the setting for a functional limit that looks like Brownian motion. For centred iid innovations with variance $\sigma^2>0$, rescale time by $n$ so that step $\lfloor nu\rfloor$ sits at rescaled time $u$, divide the levels by $\sigma\sqrt n$, and interpolate linearly between the integer steps: the resulting continuous-path process converges in distribution to Brownian motion.{{cite:morters_peres2010}} (§5.4, p. 134) Ordinary $t$ critical values are the wrong reference for a regression coefficient fitted to such levels, which is the spurious-regression phenomenon. Differences, or a model that explicitly allows a shared stochastic trend, change the object being estimated. Forecasting then has to say whether the target is a future level, a future difference, or an interval whose width is allowed to grow with the horizon. The random-walk calculation gives the forecast-error standard deviation as $\sigma\sqrt h$ when the innovations are the only source of error and their variance is constant. That standard deviation is not itself an interval width. A width is a fixed multiple of $\sigma\sqrt h$ only under an interval construction that uses such a multiple, as a normal-theory interval with a fixed quantile does; in general the width need not scale with $\sigma\sqrt h$ at all. With innovations independent and equally likely to be $+\sigma$ or $-\sigma$, the central 95% equal-tailed interval has width $2\sigma$ at horizon one and $4\sigma$ at horizon two, a ratio of two rather than $\sqrt 2$. Parameter uncertainty and a change in the process can change the width further; neither effect is included in the innovation formula.{{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§9.8, “Forecasting”){{cite:shumway_stoffer2017}} (§3.6, p. 135)
