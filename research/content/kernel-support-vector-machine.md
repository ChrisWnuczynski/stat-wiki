## Motivating example
Opposite corners of a square have the same label in XOR. A straight line in the input plane cannot separate the two diagonals, but their coordinate product distinguishes them immediately. A kernel support vector machine lets a linear separator use nonlinear features through inner products, so the algorithm can find a curved boundary without constructing every feature coordinate. The choice of kernel determines which geometry the margin uses.{{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§§5.7.2, 6.1)

## Intuition — A linear separator in another geometry
A valid kernel is a function $K(x,z)=\langle\phi(x),\phi(z)\rangle$ for some feature map into an inner-product space. Its finite Gram matrices are symmetric positive semidefinite, since $a^\top K a=\|\sum_i a_i\phi(x_i)\|^2\ge0$. A nonlinear input boundary can therefore arise from a hyperplane $\langle w,\phi(x)\rangle+b=0$. The model is linear in the feature representation, while its predictions need not be linear in $x$. The same identity is what lets a support vector classifier replace each feature inner product by a kernel entry and never store $\phi(x)$.{{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§5.7.2, Support Vector Machines){{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (Ch. 12, §12.3, p. 424, Eq. 12.21)

For $x\in\mathbb R^2$, the quadratic kernel has the explicit representation
$$\phi(x)=(1,\sqrt2x_1,\sqrt2x_1x_2,\sqrt2x_2,x_1^2,x_2^2)^\top,$$
because multiplying two such feature vectors gives $(1+x^\top z)^2$. This ordering makes the cross-product coordinate in the XOR calculation precise. Higher-degree polynomial expansions can become large, and Gaussian kernels correspond to infinite-dimensional representations, yet each prediction can still be computed using kernel values. The fitted score is a linear combination of kernel values against the training points, $f(x)=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i K(x,x_i)+b$, so the vectors $\phi(x_i)$ are never required at prediction time.{{cite:zhang_d2l2023}} (§11.2, similarity kernels, for Gaussian distance weighting){{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§5.7.2, kernel trick){{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (§12.3.1, eq. 12.23–12.24, p. 424)

## Properties — The penalty and the support vectors
The soft-margin primal minimizes
$$\frac12\|w\|^2+C\sum_i\xi_i,\qquad y_i(\langle w,\phi(x_i)\rangle+b)\ge1-\xi_i,\quad\xi_i\ge0.$$
Its dual uses the Gram matrix and bounds each multiplier by $C$. At a solution the weight in feature space is $w=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i\phi(x_i)$ with $0\le\alpha_i\le C$. Large $C$ makes violations costly relative to feature-space norm; small $C$ allows more violations. The numerical meaning of $C$ depends on whether the loss is summed or averaged, and on kernel scaling. A kernel's bandwidth and $C$ should be tuned together with validation data. In the dual, $C$ is the upper bound on the multipliers and the kernel replaces each feature inner product.{{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§12.6, regularization and cross-validation){{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§5.7.2){{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (Ch. 12, §12.2.1, pp. 420–421, Eqs. 12.12 and 12.17){{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (Ch. 12, §12.2.1, p. 420, Eq. 12.13; §12.3, p. 424)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof of the finite kernel representation"}
::: {.proof}
Introduce multipliers $\alpha_i\ge0$ for the margin constraints and $\gamma_i\ge0$ for $\xi_i\ge0$. Stationarity in $w,b,\xi$ gives $w=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i\phi(x_i)$, $\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0$, and $C-\alpha_i-\gamma_i=0$. Substituting the first identity into the Lagrangian yields the dual quadratic form and the bounds $0\le\alpha_i\le C$. Complementary slackness gives $y_k f(x_k)=1$ when $0<\alpha_k<C$, which proves the intercept formula. If no multiplier is strictly between its bounds, that particular formula has no eligible index; the KKT inequalities must instead determine an admissible intercept. The argument requires a positive semidefinite kernel for the convex norm interpretation.
:::
:::

## Worked example — Changing the margin penalty for XOR
The square's Gram matrix is $8I+\mathbf1\mathbf1^\top$, with eigenvalues $12,8,8,8$. Because the labels sum to zero, the rank-one term vanishes on the labelled multiplier vector. The hard-margin optimum $\alpha_i=1/8$ is thus unique. With a soft-margin bound $C\ge1/8$, it stays feasible and is still optimal. For $0<C<1/8$, symmetry and strict concavity give $\alpha_i=C$ and one admissible intercept is $b=0$. The decision function becomes $f(x)=-8Cx_1x_2$, and every training margin is $8C<1$. This illustrates that a correct training label need not have margin at least one in a soft-margin fit.{{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§5.7.2, margin and kernel classifier)

**Your turn.** Use $C=1/16$ for the four XOR points. What are their functional margins, and what is $f(0.5,-0.5)$?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The decision function is $f(x)=-x_1x_2/2$. Each training margin is $1/2$, and $f(0.5,-0.5)=1/8>0$, predicting the positive class.
:::

## Statistical use — Scoring new observations
Prediction needs only training points with nonzero multipliers, the support vectors. Sparse multipliers can make scoring faster, although an SVM can have many support vectors. A point with $0<\alpha_k<C$ lies on the margin with zero slack, and its label and score give the intercept through $b=y_k-\sum_i\alpha_i y_i K(x_i,x_k)$. A point with $\alpha_k=C$ has met the upper bound and can have positive slack, so the same equality need not hold. If every multiplier sits at $0$ or at $C$, the intercept is fixed by the remaining inequalities rather than by that free-point formula. Input standardization must use training estimates before distances are computed. A decision score is a signed margin, not a posterior class probability; probability calibration requires an additional fitted relationship and an appropriate validation procedure.{{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§5.7.2, computational costs){{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§12.5, probabilities and objective functions){{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (Ch. 12, §12.2.1, pp. 420–421, Eqs. 12.12 and 12.17)

### The hinge and the feature-space margin
The soft-margin objective charges $\xi_i$ once the functional margin $y_i f(x_i)$ drops below $1$. That charge is the hinge $[1-y_i f(x_i)]_+$. Past margin $1$ the hinge is zero, so a point that is already correct and far from the boundary in feature space does not pull the fit. Inside the margin the hinge grows linearly. A point on the wrong side of the decision value has hinge greater than $1$. Squared error has no flat region of this kind: a correct point with a large score still contributes a residual. The population hinge, for a fixed input, is minimized by predicting the more probable label. The fitted machine minimizes the hinge together with a penalty on the feature-space weight, which is a different target from maximum likelihood for a logistic probability.{{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (§12.3.2, pp. 426–427, hinge and squared error){{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (§12.3.2, p. 427, population minimisers)

For the quadratic kernel on the XOR square, the hard-margin multipliers $\alpha_i=1/8$ remain optimal for every penalty bound $C\ge1/8$. They produce functional margin $1$ at each training point. For $0<C<1/8$ the bound forces $\alpha_i=C$, the decision function is $f(x)=-8C x_1 x_2$, and every training margin equals $8C<1$. At $C=1/16$ the margin is $1/2$ and $f(0.5,-0.5)=1/8>0$. The signs are still the XOR labels, and the margin constraints of the hard problem are not met. A correct training label and a margin of at least one are separate statements. The first can hold while the hinge is positive.{{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (Ch. 12, §12.2, pp. 419–420, Eqs. 12.7–12.8){{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§5.7.2, margin and kernel classifier)

## Common misconceptions
The kernel trick is an equality of inner products, $K(x,z)=\langle\phi(x),\phi(z)\rangle$. It does not by itself choose $C$, the polynomial degree, or the Gaussian width. Two kernels can both be positive semidefinite and still induce different margins on the same labels. The Gram matrix of a valid kernel is positive semidefinite for every finite set of inputs, because $c^\top K c=\|\sum_i c_i\phi(x_i)\|^2$. A symmetric matrix of similarities that fails this test on some subset is not an inner-product kernel, and the feature-space norm in the primal is then not the norm the dual quadratic is using.{{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (Ch. 12, §12.3, p. 424, Eq. 12.21){{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§5.7.2, Support Vector Machines)

Scaling the inputs changes $K$. A coordinate measured in centimetres instead of metres changes squared distances inside a Gaussian kernel and changes the polynomial features $x_j^2$ and $x_1 x_2$. The multipliers that were support vectors in one scaling need not be the support vectors after the units change. Standardizing with the training mean and standard deviation, and applying those same training constants to later points, keeps the geometry that was penalized. Recomputing the constants on a pool that includes the test points lets the test sample influence the kernel.{{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (§12.3.1, eq. 12.23, p. 424){{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§12.6, regularization and cross-validation)

The sign of $f(x)$ is a classification. The size of $f(x)$ is a margin in the units of the chosen kernel and of $C$. Calibrating it to a probability is a second fit, trained on scores that were not themselves probabilities. A large positive score can still belong to a region where the two classes overlap in the original measurements, because the overlap is measured in the feature geometry. Report the kernel, the penalty, and the preprocessing with the decision rule.{{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§12.5, probabilities and objective functions){{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (§12.3.2, pp. 426–427, hinge and squared error)

## History
The soft-margin dual is a quadratic programme in the multipliers. Its objective is $\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j y_i y_j K(x_i,x_j)-\sum_i\alpha_i$, with $0\le\alpha_i\le C$ and $\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0$. A multiplier at zero drops that training point from the expansion of the feature-space weight. A multiplier strictly between the bounds marks a point whose functional margin is one, and that point determines the intercept from its kernel row against the training set. A multiplier at the upper bound $C$ is allowed a smaller margin, with the slack absorbing the difference.{{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (Ch. 12, §12.2.1, pp. 420–421, Eqs. 12.12 and 12.17){{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§5.7.2, Support Vector Machines)

The XOR square makes the same partition numerical. With the quadratic kernel and opposite labels on the two diagonals, the hard-margin solution uses $\alpha_i=1/8$ at every corner. Any penalty bound $C\ge 1/8$ leaves that solution inside the box constraints, so each training margin stays $1$. The tighter bound $C=1/16$ forces every multiplier to the ceiling. The score becomes $f(x)=-8C x_1 x_2$, the training margin falls to $1/2$, and the sign at $(0.5,-0.5)$ is still the XOR label. The hinge at each training corner is then $1/2$. Correct signs on the four corners, and a functional margin of at least one, are separate statements about the same fit.{{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (Ch. 12, §12.2, pp. 419–420, Eqs. 12.7–12.8){{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§5.7.2, margin and kernel classifier)

Prediction uses only the points with a positive multiplier: $f(x)=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i K(x,x_i)+b$. A Gaussian kernel evaluates each term from a squared distance in the original coordinates, so the feature map is never stored. Changing the unit of a coordinate changes those distances and can change which multipliers the quadratic programme sets above zero. The training mean and standard deviation, applied unchanged to a later point, keep the kernel that the penalty was fitted to. The sign of $f(x)$ is the classification. The size of $f(x)$ is a margin in the units of $K$ and of $C$. A probability attached to that score is a second model, trained on scores that were not themselves probabilities.{{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (§12.3.1, eq. 12.23, p. 424){{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§12.5, probabilities and objective functions)

The population hinge, for a fixed input, is minimized by predicting the more probable label. The fitted machine minimizes the hinge together with a penalty on the feature-space weight. That target is different from maximum likelihood for a logistic probability, which has no flat region once a point is already on the correct side of the margin. Past margin $1$ the hinge is zero, so a correct point far from the boundary in feature space does not pull the fit. Inside the margin the hinge grows linearly. Reporting the kernel, the penalty, and the preprocessing with the decision rule records the geometry in which that margin was measured.{{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (§12.3.2, pp. 426–427, hinge and squared error){{cite:holmes_huber2019_msmb}} (§12.6, regularization and cross-validation)
