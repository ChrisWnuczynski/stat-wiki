## Lead context

A survey fails in more than one way. The list can be wrong, the draw can be wrong, the answers can be wrong, and the people who decline can be different from the people who accept. Survey methodology is the practice of designing the questions, the selection, and the follow-up so that those failures stay visible and, where possible, small.{{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "survey"){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "survey errors")

## Motivating example

A mail survey of graduates draws a random sample and then hears back from only a fraction of them. OpenIntro's illustration is a survey in which 30 percent of the people randomly sampled actually respond. The unreturned forms are not a random hole that the random start has already cured. If the graduates who answer differ from those who do not, the returned mean is a mean for respondents. The methodology problem is the non-response.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.3){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "nonresponse error")

## Definition

Survey methodology is the design and administration of surveys: development of the questionnaire, selection of the sample, ideally by scientific randomisation, and management of non-response.{{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "survey")

The errors that the methodology has to budget are classified, following Salant and Dillman, as coverage error, sampling error, measurement error, and non-response.{{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "survey errors") Measurement error in a survey is an answer that is inaccurate, imprecise, or hard to compare with other answers, and that class splits further into the mode of administration, the questionnaire, the interviewer, and the respondent.{{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "measurement error")

A probability design is what lets a sampling-error formula be stated. From the list of random methods, simple random sampling, stratified sampling, systematic sampling, and cluster sampling are the draws for which a selection probability can be given. A convenience selection, taking whoever is at hand, is the contrast.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 152 and 470){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "convenience sample")

## Properties

Coverage error arises when the frame does not match the population to which one wants to generalise.{{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "coverage error") Sampling error, in the glossary's survey sense, arises when the selection is biased relative to that population.{{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "sampling error") Non-response error arises when those who decline differ, in a way that matters, from those who answer.{{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "nonresponse error")

Mode is part of measurement. Face-to-face, mail, and telephone administrations do not produce identical answers, and the difference is a method error.{{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "method error") An interviewer can shift answers by manner, nonverbal cues, or wording. That shift is interviewer error. A respondent can answer incorrectly on purpose or by mistake. That is respondent error. The form, structure, and wording of the questions contribute questionnaire error.{{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "interviewer error"){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "respondent error"){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "questionnaire error")

The size of the generalisation error also depends on the estimator, not only on the draw. A sample can be representative and the estimator still a poor summary of it. Modes are chosen with response in mind. A mail survey's reminders, postcards, and a final certified letter are devices aimed at non-response. A proctored room keeps the written answer confidential and is used when a high response rate is the point of gathering people in one place.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 533){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "mail survey"){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "proctor survey")

Scientific randomisation, in the experimental sense, assigns treatments by a random mechanism. A systematic assignment, such as giving treatment A to units 1–12 and treatment B to units 13–24, is the contrast the glossary draws. A survey draw has the same distinction: an interval through a list is not a random sample unless the start was random.{{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "randomization, scientific"){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 533)

## Worked example

A frame lists 800 graduates. The population of interest has 1000 graduates, so 200 were never eligible to be drawn. The coverage rate of the frame is $800/1000 = 0.8$. From the frame, 200 are drawn and 140 reply. The response rate among the drawn is $140/200 = 0.7$, and the non-response count is $200 - 140 = 60$. The proportion of the target population that both could be drawn and did reply is $140/1000 = 0.14$.{{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "coverage error"){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "nonresponse error")

**Your turn.** Reminders bring in 20 of the 60 non-respondents. What are the new response count and the new response rate among the 200 drawn?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The response count is $140 + 20 = 160$. The response rate is $160/200 = 0.8$.
:::

## Assumptions and limits

The four-way split is a classification of sources, not a measurement of each source in a given survey. Non-response error requires that the non-respondents differ in a relevant way. A random subset of refusals would not create that bias, and one rarely knows that the subset was random. A response rate of 0.7, or a 30 percent response in a worked illustration, describes who answered. It does not by itself measure how different the others are.{{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "nonresponse error"){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.3)

Coverage and non-response are different errors. A high response rate does not repair a frame that omitted a class of people. In the worked frame, even if all 200 drawn graduates replied, the 200 people who were never on the list of 800 would still be missing. Writing a clearer question does not shrink sampling error. Questionnaire error and sampling error sit in different parts of the classification.{{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "coverage error"){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "questionnaire error"){{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "sampling error")

The ideal of a scientifically randomised selection is often not what the budget buys. When the selection probability is unknown, the usual sampling-error formula does not apply. The methodology decision is whether the field work implemented the design the formula assumes.{{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "survey"){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 470)

## History

The classification of survey error into coverage, sampling, measurement, and non-response is the working budget of modern survey practice, the classification the glossary takes from Salant and Dillman.{{cite:nist_glossary2002}} (Glossary, "survey errors") The sampling half of that budget is the probabilistic theory that replaced the idea that only a census was serious. Kiaer argued for representative samples at the International Statistical Institute in 1895, against a congress that still preferred complete enumeration. By the Rome congress of 1925 the question had moved from whether to sample to how to sample. Neyman favoured random selection over purposive selection and worked on stratified and cluster designs. Systematic sampling, simple random sampling, stratified sampling, and cluster sampling are the random methods that this development left as the designs for which a selection probability can be stated.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 468–470 and 533)
