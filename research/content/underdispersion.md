## Motivating example — Counts can sit tighter than a Poisson mean allows

Suppose a count model predicts an average of two bids for each comparable firm. A Poisson model then assigns conditional variance two as well, because the mean, the variance and every cumulant of a Poisson count equal $\mu$.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.2.1, p. 195){{cite:ver_hoef_boveng2007}} (p. 2766) If the observed counts stay tightly around two even after the relevant firm characteristics have been accounted for, the Poisson variance may be too large. This is underdispersion: a comparison between residual variation and a model's variance prediction, not simply a statement that a histogram looks narrow.

## Intuition — When counts vary less than Poisson

Poisson regression locks the conditional variance to the conditional mean at each covariate setting.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.2.1, p. 195) Real count processes need not obey that lock. A bounded number of opportunities produces a binomial count, whose variance is below its mean. A recording device that cannot register two events too close together, such as a counter with a dead time, also yields fewer distinct records than an ideal Poisson process at a high rate.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.1, p. 193) Underdispersion is the less common of the two departures, and the working description puts a dispersion $\sigma^2<1$ on the Poisson variance.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.1, p. 194)

Underdispersion is conditional on the proposed mean model. A group of counts that looks variable in aggregate can be tightly bunched once distinct groups have different fitted means. Conversely, a badly specified mean can make an otherwise suitable variance model appear overdispersed. Both parts of a log-linear specification need checking, and chiefly the mean equation, before the variance multiplier is interpreted as a property of the counts.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.1, p. 194; §6.2.3, p. 199)

## Definition — Variance below a stated reference $V(\mu)$

Relative to a reference count model with conditional mean $\mu_i$ and variance function $V(\mu_i)$, underdispersion means that the conditional variance is smaller than $V(\mu_i)$ in the relevant range. For a Poisson reference, $V(\mu_i)=\mu_i$. A quasi-Poisson description writes $\operatorname{Var}(Y_i\mid x_i)=\phi\mu_i$; $0<\phi<1$ indicates underdispersion, $\phi=1$ the Poisson variance and $\phi>1$ overdispersion. This $\phi$ is a variance multiplier, not a mean parameter. The case $\sigma^2<1$ is the same multiplier written in McCullagh and Nelder's notation.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.1, p. 194)

An estimated Pearson dispersion is $\widehat\phi=X_P^2/(n-p)$, where $X_P^2=\sum_i(y_i-\widehat\mu_i)^2/\widehat\mu_i$ and $p$ fitted mean parameters. McCullagh and Nelder write the same estimator as $X^2/(n-p)$ with $X^2=\sum_i(y_i-\mu_i)^2/\mu_i$ at the fitted mean.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.2.4, eq. 6.4, p. 200) Values below one suggest underdispersion, but sampling noise, leverage, dependence, and poor mean fit complicate interpretation. The ratio is a diagnostic, not by itself a proof that a particular alternative distribution is correct.

## Properties — A binomial count is already underdispersed

A binomial count supplies a simple structural counterexample to Poisson equality. If $Y\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(m,q)$, then $\mathbb E Y=mq$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=mq(1-q)$, so its variance-to-mean ratio is $1-q$ when $q>0$. It is below one for $0<q\leq 1$. In the exponential-family form the binomial variance function is $V=\mu(n-\mu)/n$, which is smaller than $\mu$ whenever $0<\mu<n$.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§3.3, p. 379) Greenwood and Yule record the same comparison for the positive binomial: the second moment $npq$ is necessarily less than the mean $np$.{{cite:greenwood_yule1920}} (p. 279) A bounded number of trials can therefore produce underdispersed counts naturally, although a binomial model requires a meaningful trial count $m$.

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Write $Y=\sum_{j=1}^{m}B_j$ for independent Bernoulli$(q)$ variables. Linearity gives $\mathbb E Y=mq$, and independence gives $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=\sum_jq(1-q)=mq(1-q)$. Division by the positive mean yields $1-q<1$ for $q>0$. The same ratio is $(n-\mu)/n$ when the mean is $\mu=nq$ and the variance function is $\mu(n-\mu)/n$.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§3.3, p. 379)

For a Poisson model with fitted intercept only, $\widehat\mu$ is the sample mean. Substituting it into the Pearson statistic gives the stated sum of squared residuals divided by that mean; dividing by its residual degrees of freedom yields the conventional dispersion estimate.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.2.4, eq. 6.4, p. 200) These calculations establish the example and diagnostic formula, not the validity of a chi-square approximation in a small sample.

## Interpretation — $\phi<1$ and $\nu>1$ can name the same direction

The sign of a dispersion parameter depends on its model convention. The mean-parameterised Conway–Maxwell–Poisson model has a shape parameter $\nu$, with $\nu=1$ at the Poisson case. In general $\nu<1$ implies overdispersion and $\nu>1$ implies underdispersion relative to a Poisson distribution with the same mean.{{cite:huang2017_cmp}} (§2, after Result 1) Sellers and Shmueli read an estimate $\hat\nu>1$ the same way: in one of their regressions $\hat\nu=5.78$ is reported as under-dispersion.{{cite:sellers_shmueli2010}} (§4.1) This runs in the opposite numerical direction from the quasi-Poisson variance multiplier $\phi<1$. Reporting “dispersion 1.75” without naming the model can therefore mislead. The two parameters also have different inferential meanings: quasi-Poisson specifies a mean–variance relationship without a full probability mass function, while the Conway–Maxwell–Poisson is a count distribution.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§9.1, pp. 323–324)

A low Pearson ratio affects uncertainty estimates. If the assumed Poisson variance is too large, standard errors based strictly on it can be unnecessarily conservative for a correctly specified mean model; however, automatically shrinking all standard errors from a single low ratio can be unsafe when dependence or a wrong mean model is responsible. The fitted mean, residual diagnostics, study design, and plausible count mechanism should be considered together. The assumed variance $\sigma^2\mu$ can and should be checked, and errors in its shape usually matter less than an error in the mean.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.1, p. 194; §6.2.3, p. 199)

## Statistical use — Check the mean before shrinking the standard errors

First fit a scientifically sensible mean model and plot residuals against fitted means and important covariates. Estimate the Pearson dispersion with appropriate residual degrees of freedom. If it is materially below one and the pattern persists across groups, consider whether the response is bounded by a number of opportunities, whether a quasi-likelihood variance adjustment is adequate, or whether a flexible count distribution is needed. A standard negative-binomial count model has variance $\mu+\mu^2/\nu$, which is greater than its mean for finite positive $\nu$, and the negative binomial regression is limited to overdispersed data.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.2.3, p. 199){{cite:greenwood_yule1920}} (p. 279){{cite:sellers_shmueli2010}} (§1) It cannot be the direct remedy for genuine Poisson underdispersion.

An intercept-only comparison of a handful of counts assumes comparable units and no hidden group shifts. If some units come from one group and the rest from another with a different mean, that comparison mixes mean structure with dispersion. A sample of identical counts yields a zero Pearson statistic, but that alone does not imply a population variance of zero. Correlated repeated counts can also invalidate the usual independent-observation dispersion estimate. If a trial count is unknown or varies by unit, the simple binomial explanation may not be a usable model even when the observed ratio is low. The restricted generalised Poisson regression can represent only a limited degree of under-dispersion, because its dispersion parameter is confined to $-2/\mu_i<\alpha<0$, and that restriction ties the allowable means to the amount of under-dispersion.{{cite:sellers_shmueli2010}} (§1){{cite:huang2017_cmp}} (§1)

## Worked example — Ten tightly concentrated counts

Ten comparable units produce counts $1,3,2,2,2,2,2,2,2,2$. The fitted Poisson intercept gives $\widehat\mu=2$ and expected variance $2$. The sum of squared residuals is $(1-2)^2+(3-2)^2=2$, so the sample variance is $2/9\approx0.2222$ and its ratio to the mean is $1/9\approx0.1111$. Equivalently, $X_P^2=2/2=1$ and with one fitted parameter $\widehat\phi=1/(10-1)=1/9$. These numbers show a tightly concentrated sample relative to Poisson, but ten observations alone do not establish the mechanism or validate an asymptotic test. The comparison also assumes that the ten units are exchangeable. A hidden split into groups with different means, or dependence among the counts, would mix that structure into $X_P^2$.

**Your turn.** A response counts successes in four independent trials, each with success probability $0.5$. Find its mean, variance and variance-to-mean ratio. Would a Poisson law with the same mean match its spread?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
For $Y\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(4,0.5)$, the mean is $4(0.5)=2$ and variance is $4(0.5)(0.5)=1$, so the ratio is $1/2=0.5$. A Poisson variable with mean $2$ has variance $2$, twice as large, and also lacks the binomial upper bound of four.
:::

## Common misconceptions — A small mean is not underdispersion

Underdispersion does not mean a small mean; a count can have a large mean and a variance smaller than that mean. It is not diagnosed by comparing raw variance and raw mean when covariates change the conditional mean across observations. A quasi-Poisson multiplier below one and a Conway–Maxwell–Poisson parameter above one indicate the same direction of spread under different conventions.{{cite:huang2017_cmp}} (§2, after Result 1){{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.1, p. 194) Finally, replacing Poisson with a negative binomial does not fix underdispersion. The second moment of the negative binomial series is necessarily greater than its mean.{{cite:greenwood_yule1920}} (p. 279) A gamma mixture of Poisson rates makes the same comparison in another parameterisation: the variance is $\mu+\mu^2/\nu$, which exceeds $\mu$ for finite positive $\nu$.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.2.3, p. 199)

## Connections — Poisson, quasi-Poisson, and a family that can be tight

The [Poisson distribution](poisson-distribution.qmd) sets the reference equality of mean and variance. [Overdispersion](overdispersion.qmd) is the opposite direction relative to that reference. A [quasi-Poisson model](quasi-poisson.qmd) changes the variance scale without supplying a full likelihood, while the [Conway–Maxwell–Poisson distribution](conway-maxwell-poisson-distribution.qmd) can represent narrower counts in a fully specified family.{{cite:sellers_shmueli2010}} (§1; §2) The mean and the variance remain separate summaries: locking them together is a Poisson assumption, not a property of every count.

## History — A variance multiplier, then a distribution that can be tight

Over- and under-dispersion were handled in the generalised linear model by a single multiplier on the Poisson variance. Under-dispersion, described as less common in practice, is the case $\sigma^2<1$.{{cite:mccullagh_nelder1989}} (§6.1, p. 194) A count distribution that could be tighter than the Poisson, and also wider, is the Conway–Maxwell–Poisson law. Huang, reporting the earlier development, places its origin in a queueing model with dependent service times, and Sellers and Shmueli turned the distribution into a regression whose link is $\log\lambda$. Huang then reparameterised it by the mean, so that a coefficient refers to $\mu$ rather than to a rate that is not the mean.{{cite:huang2017_cmp}} (§1; §2){{cite:sellers_shmueli2010}} (§3) The mean-parameterised form has been fitted to underdispersed takeover bids: the number of bids received by 126 firms that were successful targets of tender offers, after the covariates, is tighter than a Poisson mean allows.{{cite:huang2017_cmp}} (§5.3)
