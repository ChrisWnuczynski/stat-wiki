## Motivating example
Three instruments share a hidden calibration effect. Their measurements may be correlated marginally even if two become independent after conditioning on a third measurement. An undirected graph records permitted conditional associations and makes separators visible. It does not say that every drawn edge is causal, or that removing an edge leaves the marginal distribution unchanged.{{cite:wainwright_jordan2008}} (§§2.1.2–2.2, pp. 9–12)

## Intuition — What an absent edge says
In a pairwise Markov graph, an absent edge $i-j$ asserts $X_i\perp X_j\mid X_{V\setminus\{i,j\}}$. This conditions on all other variables. Global separation can give a smaller conditioning set: when $S$ intersects every path from $A$ to $B$, a distribution with the global Markov property satisfies $X_A\perp X_B\mid X_S$. A graph represents a family of distributions; a particular member may have extra independences, so an edge permits a dependence without forcing one.{{cite:wainwright_jordan2008}} (§2.2)

In a factorized model, clique potentials need not sum or integrate to one. Their product is normalized by $Z$. Multiplying a potential by a positive constant changes $Z$ by that same factor and leaves the distribution unchanged. This is why graph factors should not automatically be read as marginal distributions of their cliques.{{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§3.14, Structured Probabilistic Models)

## Worked example — A positive binary chain
Let $X_1,X_2,X_3\in\{0,1\}$ and set
$$p(x_1,x_2,x_3)=Z^{-1}2^{\mathbf1\{x_1=x_2\}}2^{\mathbf1\{x_2=x_3\}}.$$
The graph is the chain $1-2-3$. For either fixed middle value, summing each neighboring factor gives $3$, hence $Z=2(3)(3)=18$. Given $X_2=0$, each endpoint independently equals zero with probability $2/3$. Thus $\mathbb{P}(X_1=0,X_3=0\mid X_2=0)=4/9$, the product of its two conditional marginals. The same factorization holds at $X_2=1$. Marginally, $\mathbb{P}(X_1=X_3)=5/9$, exceeding $1/2$: conditional independence has not removed the association induced by the shared middle variable.{{cite:wainwright_jordan2008}} (§§2.1.2–2.2)

The six-vertex example has maximal cliques $\{1,2,3\}$, $\{2,3,4\}$ and $\{4,5,6\}$. Removing vertex 4 disconnects the first three vertices from 5 and 6, so their subvectors are independent given $X_4$ under the global property. Removing only vertex 2 does not separate 1 from 5: the path $1-3-4-5$ remains.

**Your turn.** For the binary chain, compute $\mathbb{P}(X_1=1,X_3=0\mid X_2=0)$ and explain its product form.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The first endpoint equals one with probability $1/3$ and the last equals zero with probability $2/3$. Conditional independence gives $2/9$. Directly the unnormalized weight is $1\cdot2=2$ and the fixed-middle normalizer is $9$.
:::

## Properties — Why a separator yields a factorization
For finite discrete variables, suppose a joint mass function factorizes over the cliques and $S$ separates $A$ from $B$. After fixing $X_S=s$, no clique can contain vertices from different connected components of $G\setminus S$. Collect the remaining potentials by those components. The conditional joint mass is a product of component functions. Its finite normalizing sum is the product of their sums, so the components are conditionally independent. Marginalizing components not of interest preserves the separation between $A$ and $B$. This proves factorization implies the global property in this setting.{{cite:wainwright_jordan2008}} (§§2.1.2–2.2)

The converse direction from Markov independences to factorization needs positivity conditions. On finite product spaces a strictly positive joint mass function is sufficient. With zeros, some conditioning configurations do not occur, and the independence assertions alone can lose the information required to construct all factors. This condition is about support, rather than about the visual sparsity of a graph.{{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§16.2, Using Graphs to Describe Model Structure)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Conditional-independence identities for finite variables"}
::: {.proof}
Suppose $p(x,y\mid z)=p(x\mid z)p(y\mid z)$ on conditioning events with positive probability. Symmetry is immediate. Summing over all $x$ with $h(x)=u$ gives $p(u,y\mid z)=p(u\mid z)p(y\mid z)$, proving the transformation rule. Restricting instead to such a fibre and dividing by $p(u\mid z)$ shows $p(x,y\mid z,u)=p(x\mid z,u)p(y\mid z)$, proving independence after also conditioning on the function of $X$.

For contraction, combine $X\perp Y\mid Z$ and $X\perp W\mid(Y,Z)$. By the product rule,
$$p(x,y,w\mid z)=p(x\mid y,w,z)p(w,y\mid z)=p(x\mid y,z)p(w,y\mid z)=p(x\mid z)p(w,y\mid z).$$
Thus $X\perp(W,Y)\mid Z$. The displayed density characterizations follow by multiplication, division on positive conditioning marginals, and summation. For general measurable spaces the statements use versions of conditional distributions and hold almost surely, as required by their definitions.

## Statistical use — Reading a fitted graph
An absent edge is a conditional-independence restriction in the model. In the binary chain the absent edge is $1-3$, and the conditional probability factors: given $X_2=0$, $\mathbb{P}(X_1=0,X_3=0)=4/9=(2/3)(2/3)$. The marginal probability $\mathbb{P}(X_1=X_3)=5/9$ is larger than $1/2$, so the endpoints remain associated until the middle variable is conditioned on. A data analysis that reports only the marginal correlation between instruments $1$ and $3$ answers the marginal question. A data analysis that reports the partial association given instrument $2$ answers the graphical one. Both numbers can be computed from the same joint distribution. They are not substitutes.{{cite:wainwright_jordan2008}} (§§2.1.2–2.2)

A separator is a conditioning set that blocks every path. In the six-vertex graph, vertex $4$ meets every path from $\{1,2,3\}$ to $\{5,6\}$, so the global Markov property gives independence of those blocks given $X_4$. Vertex $2$ does not meet the path $1-3-4-5$, so the same property does not give independence of vertex $1$ and vertex $5$ given only $X_2$. The extra vertices in a pairwise conditioning set, all variables other than the two endpoints, are a sufficient conditioning set for an absent edge. Separation can certify a smaller one. Using the smaller set is valid under the global property. Using a set that leaves a path is a different claim.{{cite:wainwright_jordan2008}} (§2.2)

## Common misconceptions
An edge permits a direct conditional dependence. It does not prove that the two endpoints are marginally dependent, and it does not prove that every distribution in the family has a nonzero partial association. A particular distribution can set a potential so that an extra independence holds. The graph is the family. A sample correlation is one distribution's estimate. Selecting edges from sample correlations and then reading the selected graph as if the missing edges had been known in advance mixes estimation with the Markov property. The property says what independences the model contains. It does not supply the sampling distribution of a graph estimate.{{cite:wainwright_jordan2008}} (§2.2){{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§16.2, Using Graphs to Describe Model Structure)

Clique potentials are not marginal distributions of the cliques. In the chain, each factor $2^{\mathbf1\{x_i=x_{i+1}\}}$ equals $2$ or $1$. Those values do not sum to one over the pair, and the joint normalizer is $Z=18$, not a product of separate pair normalizers taken from the marginals. Multiplying one potential by $4$ multiplies $Z$ by $4$ and leaves $p$ unchanged. Reading the factor as "the probability of the pair" double-counts the shared variables and ignores $Z$. After the variables are discrete and positive, the Hammersley–Clifford argument reconstructs some factorization from the Markov properties. The potentials in that factorization are still defined only up to the rescaling that $Z$ absorbs.{{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§3.14, Structured Probabilistic Models){{cite:wainwright_jordan2008}} (§§2.1.2–2.2)

## Connections — Gaussian zeros live in the precision matrix
For a nonsingular multivariate normal vector, $X_i\perp X_j\mid X_{V\setminus\{i,j\}}$ if and only if the precision matrix has a zero in position $(i,j)$. The covariance matrix can be nonzero in that position. The chain $1-2-3$ therefore has a precision zero for the pair $(1,3)$ and can still have $\operatorname{Cov}(X_1,X_3)\ne0$, which is the Gaussian version of the marginal association computed in the binary example. A zero in the covariance matrix is marginal independence, a different restriction, corresponding to a missing edge in a different graph. Estimating a sparse covariance and calling it a sparse precision answers the marginal question with the conditional vocabulary.{{cite:wainwright_jordan2008}} (§3.3, ex. 3.3, Gaussian models)

The finite-state proof that factorization implies separation uses the supports of the cliques. Once $X_S$ is fixed, no remaining clique crosses between the components of $G\setminus S$, so the conditional mass function is a product of component functions. Summing a component out of the conditional distribution preserves the product for the blocks that remain. The converse, from the conditional independences back to a factorization, uses positivity so that every conditioning value in the product space actually occurs. A distribution with structural zeros can satisfy the stated independences on the configurations that have positive probability and still fail to factor in the way a strictly positive distribution must. The binary chain above is strictly positive, $Z=18$ covers every configuration with a positive weight, and the factorization is visible directly.{{cite:wainwright_jordan2008}} (§§2.1.2–2.2){{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§16.2, Using Graphs to Describe Model Structure)

## Assumptions and limits
Conditioning and marginalizing have different graphical consequences. Removing an unobserved middle variable from a chain generally creates a marginal dependence between its endpoints. In a nonsingular Gaussian model, zeros in the precision matrix describe conditional independences, whereas zeros in the covariance matrix describe marginal independences. Confusing the two matrices changes the question being answered. A fitted graph also depends on estimation and selection uncertainty; its absent edges are model statements, not deductions from a single sample correlation. Continuous variables with densities need a version of the conditional-independence statements, holding almost everywhere rather than at every point. The finite binary calculation does not need that qualification because every atom was enumerated.{{cite:wainwright_jordan2008}} (§3.3, ex. 3.3, Gaussian models)
