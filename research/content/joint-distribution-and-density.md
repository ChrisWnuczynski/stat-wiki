## Motivating example
Two measurements may each look continuously distributed while their pair is constrained to a curve. If $X$ is uniform on $(0,1)$ and $Y=X$, both marginal distributions have ordinary densities, but the pair always lies on the diagonal of the unit square. That diagonal has area zero. A joint density with respect to area cannot assign it probability one. The joint law describes dependence as well as the separate coordinate distributions.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§3.10, multivariate distributions and degenerate normal laws)

## Definition — A CDF exists for every real random vector
For a pair $(X,Y)$, the joint distribution function is $F(x,y)=\Pr(X\le x,Y\le y)$. For a vector, the inequalities hold coordinate by coordinate. This definition applies to discrete, continuous and mixed laws. A joint density is an additional representation: there must be a nonnegative measurable $f$ such that $\Pr((X,Y)\in A)=\int_A f(x,y)\,dx\,dy$ for every Borel set $A$. In particular $\int_{\mathbb R^2}f=1$. Merely writing two continuous marginal densities does not supply such a representation.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 274–275, Joint Density Function and Joint Distribution Function)

A rectangular probability requires inclusion–exclusion:
$$\Pr(a<X\le b,c<Y\le d)=F(b,d)-F(a,d)-F(b,c)+F(a,c).$$
This follows by subtracting the two unwanted strips and adding their overlap back. Every such increment must be nonnegative. Coordinatewise monotonicity alone is weaker than this requirement, so a proposed bivariate CDF needs more than separately increasing curves. The same construction in dimension $p$ has $2^p$ corner terms.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§3.10, multivariate distribution functions)

## Properties — Marginalise before conditioning
If a joint density exists, $f_X(x)=\int f(x,y)\,dy$ and $f_Y(y)=\int f(x,y)\,dx$. Tonelli's theorem justifies these nonnegative integrals and shows they integrate to one. Whenever $f_X(x)>0$, a version of the conditional density is $f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)=f(x,y)/f_X(x)$. A density at a point is not the probability of that point: probabilities come from integrating over sets. Conditioning on $X=x$ is therefore handled through a conditional-density construction even though $\Pr(X=x)=0$ in a continuous model.{{cite:grinstead_snell1997}} (§4.2, continuous conditional probability)

Independence of the coordinates is equivalent to $F(x,y)=F_X(x)F_Y(y)$ for every pair of thresholds. When a joint density exists, it is equivalent to $f(x,y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y)$ almost everywhere. The reverse implication follows by integrating over rectangles, which generate the Borel sets; the forward implication follows from uniqueness of densities. A zero covariance is only one moment identity and cannot replace this factorisation criterion.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§2.1, independence and product distributions)

## Worked example — A density that changes with both coordinates
Let $F(x,y)=xy(x+y)/2$ on the unit square, extended to a CDF by setting the lower tails to zero and capping each coordinate at one. Its mixed derivative is $f(x,y)=x+y$ for $0<x,y<1$. This is nonnegative and integrates to one. Each marginal density is $x+1/2$ on the unit interval, and each marginal CDF is $(x^2+x)/2$. The rectangle $(1/4,3/4]\times(0,1/2]$ has probability $3/16$, obtained either by integrating $x+y$ or by the four CDF corners.

The joint mean product is $\mathbb E(XY)=1/3$, while $\mathbb EX=\mathbb EY=7/12$. Hence $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=1/3-49/144=-1/144$. The dependence is weak in covariance magnitude but it is exact: $x+y$ is not $(x+1/2)(y+1/2)$. Small covariance need not mean independence.

Here $f_X(1/2)=1$, so $f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid1/2)=y+1/2$ on $(0,1)$. Its mean is $\int_0^1 y(y+1/2)\,dy=7/12$.

## Assumptions and limits — Differentiation loses singular mass
For an absolutely continuous joint CDF, differentiating in every coordinate recovers a density almost everywhere. Conversely, integrating an arbitrary mixed derivative of a CDF need not recover the whole law, because atoms or lower-dimensional singular mass can be lost. The pair $(X,X)$ illustrates this directly: $F(x,y)=\min(x,y)$ within the unit square, and its smooth regions have zero mixed derivative, while the diagonal still carries all the probability. A representation that combines singular and absolutely continuous components must account for both.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§3.10, densities and degenerate distributions; Appendix A.4, Theorem A.4.7)

## Connections — Transformations must respect dimension
For an invertible matrix $A$ and $Y=AX+b$, a density transforms as $f_Y(y)=f_X(A^{-1}(y-b))/|\det A|$. Indeed, substitute $y=Ax+b$ in the integral over a Borel set to obtain its probability under $Y$. The determinant accounts for the volume change and its absolute value makes the density nonnegative even when orientation reverses. The measurable-map formulation more generally defines a transformed law by inverse images of events.{{cite:tao2011_measure}} (§1.3, ex. 1.3.16; §1.4, exs. 1.4.38–1.4.39)

A lower-dimensional affine variable mapped into a higher-dimensional vector can instead have a singular law. Its possible values lie in a translated column space whose dimension is smaller than the output dimension, a set of zero volume. The pair $(X,X)$ is an example. The question to ask first is whether the transformed vector has a full-dimensional density, because a determinant density formula for an invertible square map cannot apply to a dimension-increasing map.

Rectangle probabilities are four-corner differences of the joint distribution function, whether or not a density exists. A joint density is a mixed partial of that function on the absolutely continuous part. Marginal densities can still exist when the pair is singular: if one coordinate copies the other, the pair lies on a diagonal of area zero, and each margin can be uniform. [Joint probability density](joint-probability-density.qmd) is the case with a density. Independence asks for a product of marginal pieces, which is stronger than a covariance of zero. A small covariance is one mixed moment. It does not certify that product.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§2.1, independence and product distributions){{cite:durrett2019}} (§3.10, densities and degenerate distributions; Appendix A.4, Theorem A.4.7){{cite:grinstead_snell1997}} (§4.2, continuous conditional probability)

## Intuition
The joint distribution function $F(a,b)=\mathbb{P}(X\le a, Y\le b)$ is defined for every pair of real random variables, whether or not a density exists. A rectangle probability is the four-corner difference $F(a_2,b_2)-F(a_1,b_2)-F(a_2,b_1)+F(a_1,b_1)$. When a joint density $f$ exists, that probability is also the integral of $f$ over the rectangle, and $f$ is the mixed partial of $F$ at the continuity points of the density. The pair $(X,X)$ has distribution function $F(a,b)=\min(F_X(a),F_X(b))$ when the two coordinates are the same random variable. On the unit square with $X$ uniform, $F(a,b)=\min(a,b)$, the mixed partial is zero off the diagonal, and the diagonal still carries all of the probability. Differentiating the smooth part and forgetting the diagonal drops the whole law.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§3.10, densities and degenerate distributions; Appendix A.4, Theorem A.4.7)

Marginal densities can exist in that singular case. If $X$ is uniform on $(0,1)$ and $Y=X$, each margin is uniform and has density $1$ on $(0,1)$. The pair does not have a density on the plane, because every neighbourhood of the diagonal that is thin enough has area going to zero while the probability stays one. Asking for a joint density, and asking for two marginal densities, are different questions. Independence is a third question: it asks for $F(a,b)=F_X(a)F_Y(b)$, or for a product density when densities exist. A covariance of zero is one mixed moment. The product structure is a statement about every rectangle.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§2.1, independence and product distributions){{cite:grinstead_snell1997}} (§4.2, continuous conditional probability)

## Statistical use
A conditional density, when the marginal density of the conditioning variable is positive, is the joint density divided by that marginal. In the worked example the joint density is $f(x,y)=x+y$ on the unit square. The marginal of $X$ is $f_X(x)=\int_0^1(x+y)\,dy=x+1/2$. At $x=1/2$ this equals $1$, so the conditional density of $Y$ is $y+1/2$ on $(0,1)$, and the conditional mean is $\int_0^1 y(y+1/2)\,dy=7/12$. The division is legitimate at that $x$ because the marginal density is positive. On a set where the marginal density vanishes, the conditional density is not obtained by dividing.{{cite:grinstead_snell1997}} (§4.2, continuous conditional probability){{cite:durrett2019}} (§3.10, densities and degenerate distributions; Appendix A.4, Theorem A.4.7)

An invertible linear image $Y=AX+b$ multiplies the density by $1/|\det A|$. The absolute value keeps the density nonnegative when $A$ reverses orientation, and the determinant is the volume change. A map that sends a lower-dimensional set into a higher-dimensional vector, such as $x\mapsto(x,x)$, has no invertible square Jacobian on the output space. The image lies in a line of area zero. The density formula for an invertible matrix does not apply to it, and the correct description is the singular law concentrated on that line. Rectangle probabilities remain available from the distribution function in either case.{{cite:tao2011_measure}} (§1.3, ex. 1.3.16; §1.4, exs. 1.4.38–1.4.39)

## Common misconceptions
A formula that integrates a mixed partial of $F$ recovers the absolutely continuous part of the law. Atoms and mass on curves or surfaces have to be added back if they are present. The diagonal example is the warning in two dimensions: the mixed partial is zero on the open triangles above and below the diagonal, and integrating that partial over the square returns zero, while $\mathbb{P}((X,X)\in[0,1]^2)=1$. A computation that returns a density integrating to less than one has left some of the probability in a singular component, or it has integrated over the wrong region.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§3.10, densities and degenerate distributions; Appendix A.4, Theorem A.4.7)

Zero covariance is compatible with a singular pair and with a dependent pair that has a density. The coordinates of $(X,X)$ have covariance $\operatorname{Var}(X)$, which is positive when $X$ is nondegenerate, so that particular singular pair is dependent and correlated. A different construction can have covariance zero and still fail to factor into a product of margins. The check for independence is the product formula for the joint distribution, or the product formula for the density on the set where a density exists. The check for a density is whether the probability of every set is the integral of a nonnegative function. The two checks use different objects.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§2.1, independence and product distributions){{cite:grinstead_snell1997}} (§4.2, continuous conditional probability)
