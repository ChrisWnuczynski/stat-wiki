## Motivating example
Imagine a distribution on two states, $A$ and $B$, with unnormalised weights $1$ and $3$. We want to spend three times as many steps at $B$ as at $A$, but our proposal mechanism may be asymmetric: from $A$ it suggests $B$ often, while from $B$ it suggests $A$ less often. Accepting every proposal would inherit that imbalance. Metropolis–Hastings corrects each proposed move using both the target-weight ratio and the forward and reverse proposal probabilities. This simple example isolates the reason for the Hastings factor before a continuous posterior adds calculus and numerical integration.{{cite:hastings1970}} (§2.2, pp. 99–101, acceptance and reversibility)

## Intuition
At equilibrium, probability should flow from $x$ to $y$ at the same rate as from $y$ to $x$. The proposal $q(y\mid x)$ sets how often the first move is offered; the acceptance rule sets how often it is taken. For distinct states, the accepted flow in either direction is the smaller of $\pi(x)q(y\mid x)$ and $\pi(y)q(x\mid y)$. Rejections leave the state where it is, completing a valid transition probability. This symmetry proves the target is stationary, but it says nothing by itself about how fast a chain from a chosen starting point explores the target.{{cite:hastings1970}} (§2.2, pp. 99–101, acceptance and reversibility)

## Interpretation
The term $q(x\mid y)/q(y\mid x)$ repairs an uneven proposal. A symmetric random walk has equal forward and reverse proposal densities, so its correction cancels and only the target ratio remains. An independent proposal $q(y\mid x)=g(y)$ does *not* generally cancel: its ratio is $g(x)/g(y)$. The target's unknown normalising constant cancels in either case, allowing a posterior to be sampled from an unnormalised likelihood-times-prior kernel. This is the practical gain: evaluating relative weights can be much easier than integrating the whole posterior.{{cite:hastings1970}} (§2.2, pp. 99–101, acceptance and reversibility)

## Properties
The detailed-balance proof above covers the accepted moves and the rejection probability. Its core equality can be read in one line: $\pi(x)q(y\mid x)\alpha(x,y)=\min\{\pi(x)q(y\mid x),\pi(y)q(x\mid y)\}$ for $x\ne y$. Interchanging $x$ and $y$ leaves the right side unchanged, while a rejected proposal stays at $x$ and contributes only to the diagonal. Summing the balanced flows proves stationarity. A chain that cannot reach a region of positive target mass can nevertheless satisfy this balance equation on its accessible part, so irreducibility and adequate exploration remain separate requirements.{{cite:hastings1970}} (§2.2, pp. 99–101, acceptance and reversibility)

For numerical implementation, compute $\log r=\log h(y)-\log h(x)+\log q(x\mid y)-\log q(y\mid x)$ when the terms exist. Then compare $\log U$ with $\min(0,\log r)$ for $U\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1)$. This avoids forming a very small product of densities, but it does not rescue an incorrectly coded target or proposal. The required support is also real: if a proposal never offers transitions between two regions of positive target mass, no acceptance formula can invent those moves.{{cite:hastings1970}} (§2.2, pp. 99–101, acceptance and reversibility)

## Statistical use — Sampling from an unnormalised target
Metropolis–Hastings is useful when a target density can be evaluated up to a constant but direct independent sampling is difficult. In Bayesian work, the target can be a nonconjugate posterior. In statistical physics, it can be a configuration distribution with a partition function that is difficult to sum. The states of the chain are dependent; after suitable exploration, averages estimate expectations under the target, but precision depends on autocorrelation as well as the count of iterations. Several chains from dispersed starting points, trace inspection and effective sample size are ways to assess the computation, not substitutes for a correctly specified transition rule.{{cite:metropolis1953}} (p. 1087, molecular configuration Monte Carlo){{cite:hastings1970}} (§2.1, pp. 98–99, dependent sample error)

## Worked example
Let $h(A)=1$ and $h(B)=3$, so the target is $\pi(A)=1/4=0.25$ and $\pi(B)=3/4=0.75$. Suppose $q(B\mid A)=0.8$ and $q(A\mid B)=0.4$. The remaining probabilities propose staying put. For a proposed $A\to B$ move,
$$
r(A,B)=\frac{3(0.4)}{1(0.8)}=1.5,
\qquad \alpha(A,B)=1.
$$
In reverse, $r(B,A)=1/1.5=2/3\approx0.6667$. The actual off-diagonal transition probabilities are therefore $P(A,B)=0.8$ and $P(B,A)=0.4(2/3)=4/15\approx0.2667$. The stationary flows match: $0.25(0.8)=0.20$ and $0.75(4/15)=0.20$. With states ordered $(A,B)$, the transition matrix has rows $(0.2,0.8)$ and $(4/15,11/15)$, because rejection or a self-proposal keeps $B$ with probability $11/15\approx0.7333$. This explicit stay probability matters: redrawing until acceptance would produce a different chain.{{cite:hastings1970}} (§2.2, pp. 99–101, acceptance and reversibility)

**Your turn.** Let $h(A)=2$, $h(B)=1$, $q(B\mid A)=0.6$ and $q(A\mid B)=0.2$. Find both acceptance probabilities, the off-diagonal transition probabilities, and the equilibrium flows.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The forward ratio is $r(A,B)=1(0.2)/\{2(0.6)\}=1/6$, so $\alpha(A,B)=1/6$; the reverse ratio is $6$, so $\alpha(B,A)=1$. Thus $P(A,B)=0.6/6=0.1$ and $P(B,A)=0.2$. The target probabilities are $(2/3,1/3)$, and both flows equal $(2/3)(0.1)=(1/3)(0.2)=1/15\approx0.0667$. The chain can still reject at $A$, so a rejected draw records $A$ again.
:::

## Common misconceptions
A rejected proposal is not deleted from the chain: the current state is repeated, preserving the intended transition kernel. The Hastings factor is not optional for an independent proposal; it cancels only when forward and reverse proposal probabilities agree. Detailed balance establishes stationarity, not rapid convergence from an arbitrary start. A high acceptance rate is not automatically efficient: tiny proposals may nearly always be accepted while moving slowly across the target. Finally, a chain of dependent states cannot be assigned the independent-draw standard error simply by counting its iterations.{{cite:hastings1970}} (§2.1, pp. 98–99, dependent sample error)

## Assumptions and limits — Reducibility and slow mixing
Suppose $\pi$ has equal mass on two separated islands and $q$ only proposes moves within the current island. The acceptance rule satisfies local balance, but a chain started on one island never visits the other; its average can be badly biased for a function that distinguishes them. If the proposal step is extremely large relative to a narrow target, most proposed moves fall in low-density regions and are rejected. If it is extremely small, the trace moves slowly despite high acceptance. If the target weight is zero at the current state, the usual ratio is undefined, so implementation should start in positive target support. These are failures of reachability, efficiency and arithmetic, respectively.{{cite:hastings1970}} (§2.2, pp. 99–101, acceptance and reversibility)

## History
Metropolis and collaborators introduced a Monte Carlo algorithm in 1953 for interacting-molecule configurations. Hastings' 1970 paper generalized its sampling rule and discussed the assessment of Monte Carlo error. The proposal-density correction is the mathematical distinction that allows the method to use asymmetric proposals; later Bayesian applications used the resulting chains for posterior computation.{{cite:metropolis1953}} (p. 1087, molecular application){{cite:hastings1970}} (§§1–2.2, pp. 97–100){{cite:gelfand_smith1990}} (pp. 398–399, Bayesian sampling applications)
