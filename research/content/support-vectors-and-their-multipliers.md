## Motivating example
Suppose a classifier perfectly separates two clouds of points. Many lines may make zero training errors, but the support vector machine chooses the line that leaves the widest margin. A few observations nearest that margin then determine its position. Their Lagrange multipliers explain precisely which observations take part in the fitted decision rule and why moving a distant observation may leave that rule unchanged.{{cite:cortes_vapnik1995}} (§2.1, pp. 278–280, optimal hyperplanes and support vectors){{cite:burges1998}} (§§3.1–3.2, margin and multipliers)

## Intuition
Each training observation imposes a margin constraint. A multiplier $\alpha_i$ measures the role of that constraint in the optimisation: a strictly positive multiplier marks a point that contributes to the normal vector of the separating hyperplane. The point’s class label supplies a sign, so its contribution is $\alpha_i y_i\mathbf x_i$, not merely $\alpha_i\mathbf x_i$. The fitted score for a new point therefore depends on inner products with the observations carrying nonzero multipliers. In a soft-margin machine, a cap $C$ limits each multiplier and distinguishes points exactly on the margin from those inside it.{{cite:burges1998}} (§3.1, dual expansion; §3.5, bounded multipliers)

## Definition — Margin constraints and the dual
For labels $y_i\in\{-1,+1\}$, write $f(\mathbf x)=\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x+b$. In a linearly separable sample, the hard-margin machine minimises $\lVert\mathbf w\rVert^2/2$ subject to $y_i f(\mathbf x_i)\ge1$. Its full margin width is $2/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert$. The nonnegative multiplier $\alpha_i$ is attached to observation $i$’s constraint. At an optimum, $\mathbf w=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i\mathbf x_i$ and $\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0$. The complementary-slackness equation $\alpha_i\{y_i f(\mathbf x_i)-1\}=0$ means a point strictly outside the margin has zero multiplier. A point on the margin may have a positive or a zero multiplier when constraints are redundant.{{cite:cortes_vapnik1995}} (§2.1, pp. 278–279; Appendix A.1, pp. 291–292){{cite:burges1998}} (§§3.1–3.2, dual and KKT conditions)

## Interpretation — Which observations matter?
If $\alpha_i>0$ in the hard-margin fit, complementary slackness forces $y_if(\mathbf x_i)=1$: the point sits on a margin plane. This is the usual support-vector criterion. The converse need not hold, because two or more observations can impose the same effective constraint. A zero multiplier says that point contributes nothing to the displayed expansion of $\mathbf w$ at that optimum. It does not by itself say the point is geometrically far away. Duplicate points, for example, can split or exchange multipliers without changing the separating plane.{{cite:burges1998}} (§3.2, complementary slackness; §3.7, nonunique multipliers)

The hard-margin decision score is $f(\mathbf x)=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i\mathbf x_i^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x+b$. This is why kernels can replace inner products with $K(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x)$: the same nonzero multipliers select the influential training cases in feature space. Sparsity depends on the data and chosen kernel; it is a property to inspect after fitting, not a guarantee that every fitted classifier has few support vectors. Input scaling changes inner products and hence changes both the margin geometry and the multipliers.{{cite:cortes_vapnik1995}} (§2.1, pp. 279–280, dual expansion; §4, kernel construction){{cite:burges1998}} (§§3.1, 4, dual score and kernels)

## Properties — Soft margins and the value of C
When classes overlap, a hard-margin separator may not exist. A standard soft-margin machine adds slacks $\xi_i\ge0$ and minimises $\lVert\mathbf w\rVert^2/2+C\sum_i\xi_i$ subject to $y_i f(\mathbf x_i)\ge1-\xi_i$. Its dual has $0\le\alpha_i\le C$. A free support vector, with $0<\alpha_i<C$, lies exactly on a margin plane and has zero slack. A bound support vector, with $\alpha_i=C$, may be on the margin, inside it, or misclassified. At the optimum its slack is $\max\{0,1-y_if(\mathbf x_i)\}$. A point with $\alpha_i=0$ has zero slack and lies on or beyond its margin plane. These categories describe a fitted solution; the size of $\alpha_i$ is neither a classification probability nor a geometric distance.{{cite:burges1998}} (§3.5, soft-margin dual and KKT conditions)

Increasing $C$ makes slack more costly relative to the squared norm, but it does not guarantee fewer support vectors or better test accuracy. An overlapping sample can use many bound vectors at a high $C$, because fitting hard to contradictory observations pulls the boundary toward them. A smaller $C$ can allow a wider slab and more margin violations. Comparisons at $C=0.03$, $1$ and $100$ show this trade-off; those are choices of optimisation penalty, not posterior probabilities. Choose $C$ with validation that reproduces the whole preprocessing and fitting pipeline.{{cite:burges1998}} (§3.5, role of C and nonseparable data)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
For hard margins, form $L(\mathbf w,b,\boldsymbol\alpha)=\lVert\mathbf w\rVert^2/2-\sum_i\alpha_i\{y_i(\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x_i+b)-1\}$ with $\alpha_i\ge0$. Stationarity in $\mathbf w$ and $b$ yields $\mathbf w=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i\mathbf x_i$ and $\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0$; substituting them yields the dual objective $\sum_i\alpha_i-\tfrac12\sum_{ij}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j\mathbf x_i^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x_j$. Complementary slackness gives $\alpha_i\{y_if(\mathbf x_i)-1\}=0$. For linear-slack soft margins, subtract $\sum_i\alpha_i\{y_if(\mathbf x_i)-1+\xi_i\}+\sum_i\nu_i\xi_i$ from the primal objective. Stationarity in $\xi_i$ gives $C-\alpha_i-\nu_i=0$, with $\nu_i\ge0$, hence $0\le\alpha_i\le C$. If $0<\alpha_i<C$, then $\nu_i>0$ forces $\xi_i=0$ and $\alpha_i>0$ forces $y_if=1$. If $\alpha_i=C$, the first complementarity equation gives $y_if=1-\xi_i\le1$; minimal slack is $\max\{0,1-y_if\}$. If $\alpha_i=0$, then $\nu_i=C>0$ gives $\xi_i=0$ and feasibility gives $y_if\ge1$. Convexity of the primal and linear constraints make these KKT conditions sufficient at a feasible solution.{{cite:burges1998}} (§§3.1–3.2, hard-margin KKT; §3.5, soft-margin dual and KKT)
:::

## Statistical use — Reading a fitted classifier
After fitting, inspect the signed functional margins $y_if(\mathbf x_i)$ together with $\alpha_i$ and the value of $C$. A point with $0<\alpha_i<C$ supplies the equation $y_if(\mathbf x_i)=1$ and can be used to recover $b$. Using several such points and averaging their calculated intercepts can reduce numerical noise. A bound point cannot generally supply $b$ by the same equality because its slack may be positive. If no free support vector exists, $b$ must be determined from the remaining feasible interval or the solver’s KKT conditions. Tolerances matter: a value close to zero or $C$ may be a numerical boundary, not an exact symbolic equality.{{cite:burges1998}} (§§3.2, 3.5, recovering b from KKT conditions; §5, numerical optimisation)

## Worked example
Take three two-dimensional training observations: $\mathbf x_1=(0,0)$ with $y_1=-1$, $\mathbf x_2=(2,0)$ with $y_2=+1$, and $\mathbf x_3=(3,2)$ with $y_3=+1$. The maximum-margin boundary is the vertical line $x^{(1)}=1$, represented by $\mathbf w=(1,0)$ and $b=-1$. The first two signed scores are both $1$, so they touch opposite margin planes. The third signed score is $2$, so it is farther outside the margin. The dual solution $\alpha_1=\alpha_2=1/2$ and $\alpha_3=0$ satisfies $\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0$ and $\sum_i\alpha_i y_i\mathbf x_i=(1,0)$. The full slab width is $2/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert=2$. The third point is correctly classified, yet it does not set the boundary.{{cite:burges1998}} (§§3.1–3.2, margin geometry and KKT solution)

**Your turn.** Replace the nearest positive point $\mathbf x_2$ by $(1.5,0)$ while keeping the other two observations. Find the new $\mathbf w$, $b$, equal nonzero multipliers and full slab width. Does $\mathbf x_3$ become a support vector?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The nearest opposite-class pair is now $(0,0)$ and $(1.5,0)$, so their margin planes are $1.5$ apart. The negative point requires $b=-1$, and the positive point requires $1.5w_1-1=1$, giving $\mathbf w=(4/3,0)$. The dual equality gives $\alpha_1=\alpha_2$; the weight equation gives $1.5\alpha_2=4/3$, so each is $8/9$. The slab width is $2/(4/3)=1.5$. At $\mathbf x_3=(3,2)$, the signed score is $4-1=3>1$, hence $\alpha_3=0$: it is still outside the margin.
:::

## Common misconceptions
A support vector is not necessarily a misclassified point: in the separable case its signed score equals $1$. A point exactly on a margin can have zero multiplier when another constraint makes it redundant, so geometric contact alone does not prove influence. The multiplier is not a probability, distance or response coefficient; it is a dual weight attached to one training constraint. A bound multiplier $\alpha_i=C$ does not prove misclassification, because a point on or inside the margin may be correctly classified. Finally, support vectors identified in one scaled feature space need not remain support vectors after changing the scaling or kernel.{{cite:burges1998}} (§§3.2, 3.5, 3.7; §4, kernel space)

## Assumptions and limits
The hard-margin construction fails if two identical feature vectors have opposite labels: no hyperplane can give both signed margins at least $1$. Soft margins keep optimisation feasible, but the two conflicting observations necessarily incur violations, and their multipliers do not turn contradictory labels into reliable predictions. With many nearly duplicated margin points, the same separating normal can admit different allocations of dual weight. A fitted model may then have a stable decision boundary but unstable individual $\alpha_i$ values. If a solver stops before satisfying KKT tolerances, interpreting tiny positive multipliers as exact support-vector status is misleading. These cases call for checking primal margins, dual constraints and out-of-sample behaviour together.{{cite:burges1998}} (§§3.5, 3.7, nonseparable data and nonuniqueness; §5, optimisation checks)

## History
The maximum-margin construction for separable data was developed before soft-margin SVMs. Cortes and Vapnik’s 1995 support-vector networks paper extended the approach to nonseparable training data and emphasised that only observations with nonzero dual weights enter the decision expansion. Burges’s 1998 tutorial made the KKT interpretation and the linear-slack bound $0\le\alpha_i\le C$ explicit for a broad statistical-learning audience. Those results explain the modern use of “support vector” as a fitted optimisation role rather than a fixed property of an observation.{{cite:cortes_vapnik1995}} (pp. 273–280, Introduction and §2.1){{cite:burges1998}} (§§3.2, 3.5)
