## Motivating example

Repeated heart-rate measurements can help predict oxygen intake, but a fitted line alone does not say how much a new measurement may vary around it. The normal linear model adds a probability law for the unexplained part, making uncertainty in slopes, means and new responses calculable from the same regression fit. Its central assumption is that independent errors have one common variance and a normal distribution after the mean pattern has been accounted for.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 3, §§3.1–3.3, pp. 61–101)

## Intuition

Think of the columns of the design matrix $A$ as possible mean patterns. They span a $p$-dimensional subspace of all $n$ response vectors. [Ordinary least squares](ordinary-least-squares.qmd) projects the observed vector $Y$ onto that subspace: the projected part gives fitted values $\widehat Y=A\widehat\beta$ and the perpendicular part gives residuals $e=Y-\widehat Y$. Under independent equal-variance normal errors, an orthogonal rotation makes the two parts independent and shows why estimating $p$ coefficients leaves $n-p$ independent residual directions for estimating variance. That geometry is the source of exact $t$ and $F$ reference distributions, rather than a visual impression that a scatterplot looks roughly straight.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 3, §§3.1–3.3, pp. 61–101)

## Definition

The design $A$ is treated as fixed, or inference is conducted conditionally on an observed design. Full column rank ensures that each coordinate of $\beta$ is separately identifiable; when $A$ has a column of ones and one predictor column, the specification reduces to [simple linear regression](simple-linear-regression.qmd), and with several predictors to [multiple linear regression](multiple-linear-regression.qmd). “Linear” means linear in $\beta$: a column of squared predictor values represents a curved mean in the raw input while keeping $A\beta$ linear in $\beta$, and normality applies to $Y$ conditional on $A$ rather than to the predictor values.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 3, §3.3.2, pp. 86–92, transformed predictors)

In this fixed-design regression setting, **normal linear model** and **[Gaussian linear model](gaussian-linear-model.qmd)** are synonymous names for $Y=A\beta+\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon\sim N_n(0,\sigma^2 I_n)$: the normal linear model serves as the general matrix and orthogonal-projection reference, whereas the [Gaussian linear model](gaussian-linear-model.qmd) develops the worked small-sample Student-$t$ inference application.{{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 3, §3.2, p. 44; Ch. 20, §20.3, pp. 347–349)

## Interpretation

The fitted line estimates the conditional mean of oxygen intake at a given heart rate; a fresh oxygen measurement carries an additional independent error around that mean, making a prediction interval wider than a confidence interval for the mean at the same design point. Each regression coefficient represents a difference in modelled conditional mean with other design columns held fixed, not automatically the effect of intervening on heart rate: confounding and reverse association remain possible even when normal-theory calculations are exact. The heart-rate series illustrates conditional association across one subject's repeated runs rather than a universal physiological law.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 3, §§3.1.2 and 3.3.3, pp. 63–68, 92–101, interpretation and inference)

## Properties

With $H=A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top$, fitted values are $HY$ and residuals are $(I-H)Y$. Because $H$ is symmetric and idempotent of rank $p$, $I-H$ is the complementary orthogonal projector of rank $n-p$. Under $\varepsilon\sim N_n(0,\sigma^2 I_n)$, $\widehat\beta\sim N_p\{\beta,\sigma^2(A^\top A)^{-1}\}$ and $\mathrm{RSS}/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-p}$ independently, as recorded in the [sampling distribution of the regression estimators](sampling-distribution-of-the-regression-estimators.qmd). Consequently $s^2=\mathrm{RSS}/(n-p)$ is unbiased for $\sigma^2$ when $n>p$, whereas the Gaussian maximum likelihood estimator $\mathrm{RSS}/n$ is biased downward in finite samples.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 3, §3.3.3, pp. 92–101)

For a fixed contrast vector $c\neq 0$, the standardized ratio $\{c^\top\widehat\beta-c^\top\beta\}/\{s\sqrt{c^\top(A^\top A)^{-1}c}\}$ follows $t_{n-p}$, and testing a [general linear hypothesis](general-linear-hypothesis.qmd) that removes $r$ independent coefficient directions compares restricted and full residual sums of squares through $F_{r,n-p}$. Both finite-sample reference laws rely on normal, independent, homoscedastic errors: unbiasedness of $\widehat\beta$ holds under $\E(\varepsilon\mid A)=0$ alone, whereas moment assumptions by themselves do not yield exact $t$ or $F$ distributions.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 3, §§3.1–3.3, pp. 61–101){{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 20, §§20.1–20.3, pp. 342–349)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Differentiate $\|Y-Ab\|^2$ with respect to $b$ to obtain the normal equations $A^\top Ab=A^\top Y$. Full column rank makes $A^\top A$ positive definite, so the unique minimizer is $\widehat\beta=(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top Y$. Substituting $Y=A\beta+\varepsilon$ gives $\widehat\beta-\beta=(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\varepsilon$. Its expectation is zero, covariance is $\sigma^2(A^\top A)^{-1}$, and normality follows because it is a linear image of a normal vector. The normal likelihood is proportional to $(\sigma^2)^{-n/2}\exp\{-\|Y-Ab\|^2/(2\sigma^2)\}$; minimizing the norm gives the same coefficient MLE, while differentiating in $\sigma^2$ gives $\widehat\sigma^2_{\rm ML}=\mathrm{RSS}/n$.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 3, §§3.1–3.3, pp. 61–101)

The projection identities $H^2=H=H^\top$ and $HA=A$ imply $e=(I-H)\varepsilon$. Choose an orthonormal basis with its first $p$ vectors spanning the columns of $A$. Rotating $\varepsilon/\sigma$ into this basis gives $n$ independent standard normals. The first $p$ coordinates determine $\widehat\beta-\beta$; the remaining $n-p$ give $\mathrm{RSS}/\sigma^2$ as a sum of squares. Independence and the $\chi^2_{n-p}$ law follow. Therefore $\E(\mathrm{RSS})=(n-p)\sigma^2$, proving the unbiased divisor and the downward bias of the ML divisor. For any nonzero $c$, the standardized linear contrast is normal and independent of $\mathrm{RSS}/\sigma^2$, so their ratio has the stated $t$ law. For nested full and restricted design subspaces, the difference of their orthogonal projections has rank $r$ and is perpendicular to the full residual subspace. The difference in residual sums of squares divided by $\sigma^2$ is then independent $\chi^2_r$, giving the stated $F$ ratio.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 3, §3.3.3, pp. 92–101){{cite:shalizi2025_truth_regression}} (Ch. 20, §20.3, pp. 347–349)

## Statistical use — Intervals and tests under normal errors

The model answers three distinct inferential questions. A coefficient interval quantifies sampling uncertainty in a partial slope. A confidence interval for the conditional mean $a_0^\top\beta$ at design row $a_0^\top$ uses $\operatorname{Var}(a_0^\top\widehat\beta)=\sigma^2a_0^\top(A^\top A)^{-1}a_0$. A prediction interval for one new independent response at $a_0^\top$ adds the new observation's error variance $\sigma^2$, giving $\sigma^2\{1+a_0^\top(A^\top A)^{-1}a_0\}$; larger samples shrink mean uncertainty toward zero without removing the irreducible spread $\sigma^2$ of an individual draw. Residual plots against fitted values and covariates screen for curvature and heteroskedasticity, whereas independence must be judged from the data-collection design.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 3, §3.3.3, pp. 92–101)

## Worked example

Take four design points $x=(0,1,2,3)$ and responses $y=(1,2,2,4)$. Their means are $\bar x=1.5$ and $\bar y=2.25$, with $S_{xx}=5$ and $S_{xy}=4.5$. The fitted line is $\widehat y=0.9+0.9x$, giving fitted values $(0.9,1.8,2.7,3.6)$ and residuals $(0.1,0.2,-0.7,0.4)$. The residuals sum to zero and have zero inner product with $x$, as projection requires. Their squared sum is $\mathrm{RSS}=0.7$. With an intercept and slope, $p=2$, so $n-p=2$ and the unbiased variance estimate is $s^2=0.7/2=0.35$; maximum likelihood instead reports $0.7/4=0.175$. The estimated slope standard error is $\sqrt{0.35/5}\approx0.2646$. The line rises, but four points leave considerable uncertainty: using $t_{2;0.975}\approx4.303$, the slope's $95\%$ interval is about $0.9\pm4.303(0.2646)=(-0.238,2.038)$. It includes zero.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 3, §§3.1–3.3, pp. 61–101)

**Your turn.** For $x=(0,1,2)$ and $y=(1,3,2)$, fit an intercept and slope. Find the residual sum of squares, residual degrees of freedom, unbiased variance estimate and normal-likelihood variance MLE.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The means are $\bar x=1$ and $\bar y=2$, so $S_{xx}=2$ and $S_{xy}=1$. The slope is $1/2$ and intercept is $3/2$, giving fitted values $(1.5,2,2.5)$ and residuals $(-0.5,1,-0.5)$. Hence $\mathrm{RSS}=0.25+1+0.25=1.5$. There are $3-2=1$ residual degrees of freedom. The unbiased estimate is $1.5/1=1.5$, while the ML estimate is $1.5/3=0.5$. The large gap comes from estimating two mean coefficients using only three responses.
:::

## Common misconceptions

Normal errors do not require normally distributed predictors: the distributional assumption governs $Y$ conditional on $A$. A straight fitted line does not verify linearity of $\E(Y\mid A)$ or independence of $\varepsilon$. The ML divisor $n$ and the unbiased divisor $n-p$ share the same $\mathrm{RSS}$ while targeting likelihood maximization and unbiasedness respectively. Computing a $t$ ratio does not make its Student-$t$ reference law exact without independent homoscedastic normal errors, and a narrow confidence interval for $a_0^\top\beta$ never implies a narrow prediction interval for a single future observation.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 3, §§3.1.2 and 3.3.3, pp. 63–68, 92–101)

## Assumptions and limits — When normal-model inference fails

If a predictor column of $A$ is duplicated, $A^\top A$ is singular and the two individual slopes on the duplicated predictor are aliased, although the projected mean vector, the intercept, and the sum of those two slopes remain estimable on $n-\operatorname{rank}(A)$ residual degrees of freedom. If error spread grows with the mean, $\sigma^2(A^\top A)^{-1}$ misstates sampling variance; if repeated sensor readings are serially correlated, effective sample information is smaller than $n$ independent draws; and if a curved conditional mean such as $\E(Y\mid x)=x^2$ is fitted with only an intercept and $x$, systematic bias invalidates intervals centred on the wrong line. These correspond to rank, homoscedasticity, independence, and mean-specification failures.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 3, §3.3.3, pp. 92–101){{cite:sas2012_glm}} (Ch. 4, pp. 89–90, “Models Not of Full Rank”; Ch. 15, pp. 251–252, “Estimability”)

## Connections

[Ordinary least squares](ordinary-least-squares.qmd) supplies the orthogonal projection, while the normal linear model supplies the exact joint law recorded in the [sampling distribution of the regression estimators](sampling-distribution-of-the-regression-estimators.qmd) and applied at small degrees of freedom in the [Gaussian linear model](gaussian-linear-model.qmd). Nested subspace projections generate the [ANOVA sum-of-squares decomposition](anova-sum-of-squares-decomposition.qmd) and $F$ tests of the [general linear hypothesis](general-linear-hypothesis.qmd). A [generalised linear model](generalised-linear-model.qmd) retains a linear predictor while replacing the identity link and Gaussian response law, and Bayesian linear regression adjoins a prior on $(\beta,\sigma^2)$ to the same Gaussian likelihood.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 3, §§3.1–3.3, pp. 61–101)

## History

Legendre published the least-squares method in his 1805 work on cometary orbits, turning an overdetermined system of observations into a single fitted trajectory whose algebraic optimality is expressed by the normal equations. Attaching Gauss's normal error law to the disturbances later yielded exact finite-sample variance, $t$, and $F$ inference for the linear model; the algebraic fitting rule and the normal sampling model remain distinct historical and logical steps.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 304–307, least-squares method and Legendre)
