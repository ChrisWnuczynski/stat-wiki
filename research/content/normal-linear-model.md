## Motivating example
Repeated heart-rate measurements can help predict oxygen intake, but a fitted line alone does not say how much a new measurement may vary around it. The normal linear model adds a probability law for the unexplained part, making uncertainty in slopes, means and new responses calculable from the same regression fit. Its central assumption is that independent errors have one common variance and a normal distribution after the mean pattern has been accounted for.{{cite:kulich2022_regression}} (§§1.1–1.3, pp. 6–8){{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 3, §§3.1–3.3, pp. 61–101)

## Intuition
Think of the columns of the design matrix $A$ as possible mean patterns. They span a $p$-dimensional subspace of all $n$ response vectors. Least squares projects the observed vector $Y$ onto that subspace. The projected part gives fitted values $\widehat Y=A\widehat\beta$; the perpendicular part gives residuals $e=Y-\widehat Y$. Under independent equal-variance normal errors, an orthogonal rotation makes the two parts independent. It also shows why estimating $p$ coefficients leaves $n-p$ independent residual directions for estimating variance. This geometry is the source of exact $t$ and $F$ reference distributions, rather than a visual impression that a scatterplot looks roughly straight.{{cite:kulich2022_regression}} (§§1.2–1.3, pp. 7–8)

## Definition
The design $A$ is treated as fixed, or inference is conditional on an observed design to which the model applies. Its full column rank ensures that each coordinate of $\beta$ is separately identifiable. When $A$ has a column of ones and a column of predictor values, the model is simple linear regression. “Linear” means linear in the coefficients: a column of squared predictor values can represent a curved mean as a function of the original predictor while retaining a linear model in $\beta$. The normality assumption concerns the conditional response about that mean, not the distribution of the predictor values themselves.{{cite:kulich2022_regression}} (§§1.1–1.3, pp. 6–8){{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 3, §3.3.2, pp. 86–92, transformed predictors)

## Interpretation
The fitted line estimates the conditional mean of oxygen intake at a given heart rate; a fresh oxygen measurement has additional error around that mean. This distinction makes a prediction interval wider than a confidence interval for the mean at the same design point. The regression coefficient is a change in modelled conditional mean holding other columns fixed. It is not automatically the result of changing a person's heart rate through an intervention: confounding and reverse association remain possible even when the normal-error calculations are exact. The heart-rate table illustrates a strong conditional association in one person's repeated observations, not a universal physiological law.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 3, §§3.1.2 and 3.3.3, pp. 63–68, 92–101, interpretation and inference)

## Properties
With $H=A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top$, fitted values are $HY$ and residuals are $(I-H)Y$. The matrix $H$ is symmetric and idempotent, hence an orthogonal projection of rank $p$; $I-H$ is the complementary projection of rank $n-p$. The normal model gives $\widehat\beta\sim N_p\{\beta,\sigma^2(A^\top A)^{-1}\}$ and $\mathrm{RSS}/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-p}$ independently. Thus $s^2=\mathrm{RSS}/(n-p)$ is unbiased for $\sigma^2$, whereas maximum likelihood uses $\mathrm{RSS}/n$ and is biased downward in finite samples. The formulas need $n>p$ to estimate a positive residual variance.{{cite:kulich2022_regression}} (§§1.2–1.3, pp. 7–8)

For a fixed contrast vector $c$, the standardized difference $\{c^\top\widehat\beta-c^\top\beta\}/\{s\sqrt{c^\top(A^\top A)^{-1}c}\}$ has a $t_{n-p}$ law. A restriction that removes $r$ independent coefficient directions compares a restricted and a full residual sum of squares through an $F_{r,n-p}$ ratio. These exact statements use normality as well as the specified mean and covariance. The coefficient estimate can remain unbiased under a correctly specified linear mean with nonnormal errors, but the finite-sample $t$ law does not follow just from that unbiasedness.{{cite:kulich2022_regression}} (§§1.2–1.4, pp. 7–9)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Differentiate $\|Y-Ab\|^2$ with respect to $b$ to obtain the normal equations $A^\top Ab=A^\top Y$. Full column rank makes $A^\top A$ positive definite, so the unique minimizer is $\widehat\beta=(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top Y$. Substituting $Y=A\beta+\varepsilon$ gives $\widehat\beta-\beta=(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\varepsilon$. Its expectation is zero, covariance is $\sigma^2(A^\top A)^{-1}$, and normality follows because it is a linear image of a normal vector. The normal likelihood is proportional to $(\sigma^2)^{-n/2}\exp\{-\|Y-Ab\|^2/(2\sigma^2)\}$; minimizing the norm gives the same coefficient MLE, while differentiating in $\sigma^2$ gives $\widehat\sigma^2_{\rm ML}=\mathrm{RSS}/n$.{{cite:kulich2022_regression}} (§§1.2–1.3, pp. 7–8)

The projection identities $H^2=H=H^\top$ and $HA=A$ imply $e=(I-H)\varepsilon$. Choose an orthonormal basis with its first $p$ vectors spanning the columns of $A$. Rotating $\varepsilon/\sigma$ into this basis gives $n$ independent standard normals. The first $p$ coordinates determine $\widehat\beta-\beta$; the remaining $n-p$ give $\mathrm{RSS}/\sigma^2$ as a sum of squares. Independence and the $\chi^2_{n-p}$ law follow. Therefore $\operatorname E(\mathrm{RSS})=(n-p)\sigma^2$, proving the unbiased divisor and the downward bias of the ML divisor. For any $c$, the standardized linear contrast is normal and independent of $\mathrm{RSS}/\sigma^2$, so their ratio has the stated $t$ law. For nested full and restricted design subspaces, the difference of their orthogonal projections has rank $r$ and is perpendicular to the full residual subspace. The difference in residual sums of squares divided by $\sigma^2$ is then independent $\chi^2_r$, giving the stated $F$ ratio.{{cite:kulich2022_regression}} (§1.3, pp. 8–9, normal-regression results)

:::

## Statistical use
The model answers three related questions. An interval for a coefficient quantifies uncertainty in an estimated association. An interval for the mean response at a design row $a_0^\top$ reflects $\operatorname{Var}(a_0^\top\widehat\beta)=\sigma^2a_0^\top(A^\top A)^{-1}a_0$. A prediction interval for a new independent response at that row adds its own noise variance $\sigma^2$, becoming $\sigma^2\{1+a_0^\top(A^\top A)^{-1}a_0\}$. More observations can shrink uncertainty about the mean, but cannot remove the intrinsic spread of one new response. Residual plots against fitted values and predictors are a first check for curvature and unequal spread; study design is needed to judge independence.{{cite:kulich2022_regression}} (§§1.3–1.5, pp. 8–10){{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 3, §3.3.3, pp. 92–101)

## Worked example
Take four design points $x=(0,1,2,3)$ and responses $y=(1,2,2,4)$. Their means are $\bar x=1.5$ and $\bar y=2.25$, with $S_{xx}=5$ and $S_{xy}=4.5$. The fitted line is $\widehat y=0.9+0.9x$, giving fitted values $(0.9,1.8,2.7,3.6)$ and residuals $(0.1,0.2,-0.7,0.4)$. The residuals sum to zero and have zero inner product with $x$, as projection requires. Their squared sum is $\mathrm{RSS}=0.7$. With an intercept and slope, $p=2$, so $n-p=2$ and the unbiased variance estimate is $s^2=0.7/2=0.35$; maximum likelihood instead reports $0.7/4=0.175$. The estimated slope standard error is $\sqrt{0.35/5}\approx0.2646$. The line rises, but four points leave considerable uncertainty: using $t_{2;0.975}\approx4.303$, the slope's $95\%$ interval is about $0.9\pm4.303(0.2646)=(-0.238,2.038)$. It includes zero.{{cite:kulich2022_regression}} (§1.3, p. 8, coefficient and variance inference)

**Your turn.** For $x=(0,1,2)$ and $y=(1,3,2)$, fit an intercept and slope. Find the residual sum of squares, residual degrees of freedom, unbiased variance estimate and normal-likelihood variance MLE.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The means are $\bar x=1$ and $\bar y=2$, so $S_{xx}=2$ and $S_{xy}=1$. The slope is $1/2$ and intercept is $3/2$, giving fitted values $(1.5,2,2.5)$ and residuals $(-0.5,1,-0.5)$. Hence $\mathrm{RSS}=0.25+1+0.25=1.5$. There are $3-2=1$ residual degrees of freedom. The unbiased estimate is $1.5/1=1.5$, while the ML estimate is $1.5/3=0.5$. The large gap comes from estimating two mean coefficients using only three responses.{{cite:kulich2022_regression}} (§§1.2–1.3, pp. 7–8)
:::

## Common misconceptions
Normal errors do not require normal predictor values: the distributional statement is about $Y$ conditional on the design. A straight fitted line is not proof that the conditional mean is linear or that the errors are independent. The ML variance estimate and the unbiased residual estimate use different divisors; they answer different estimation conventions even though they share the same RSS. A coefficient's $t$ statistic is not exact merely because least squares produced a coefficient; the independent normal-error assumptions make the finite-sample reference law. A narrow confidence interval for the *mean* response is not a narrow prediction interval for one new observation.{{cite:kulich2022_regression}} (§§1.1–1.5, pp. 6–10){{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 3, §§3.1.2 and 3.3.3, pp. 63–68, 92–101)

## Assumptions and limits — When normal-model inference fails
If two columns of $A$ duplicate one another, $A^\top A$ is singular and the two individual coefficients cannot be identified, even though a fitted mean vector can still be projected onto their common column space. If errors become more variable as the fitted mean grows, the conventional $\sigma^2(A^\top A)^{-1}$ covariance and resulting intervals are miscalibrated; weighted or robust-variance methods address different assumptions. If a sensor records repeated measurements with serially correlated errors, there are fewer independent pieces of information than the model presumes. A curved mean such as $E(Y\mid x)=x^2$ fitted with only an intercept and $x$ yields systematic residual curvature, so normal errors around the wrong line do not rescue inference about that mean. These are rank, variance, independence and mean-shape failures respectively.{{cite:kulich2022_regression}} (§§1.1–1.5, pp. 6–10){{cite:james_islr2013}} (Ch. 3, §3.3.3, pp. 92–101)

## Connections
Ordinary least squares supplies the projection; the normal linear model supplies its exact sampling distribution. The chi-squared residual sum is the denominator behind Student $t$ coefficient pivots and nested-model $F$ tests. Analysis of variance is another design-matrix arrangement of the same sum-of-squares geometry. Generalized linear models keep a linear predictor but change the response distribution and link, so their finite-sample error laws differ. A Bayesian linear model retains the likelihood and adds a prior on coefficients and variance, changing the inferential target rather than the observed design.{{cite:kulich2022_regression}} (§§1.2–1.3 and 2.2, pp. 7–8, 18–23)

## History
Legendre published the least-squares method in his 1805 work on cometary orbits, turning many imperfect observations into a single fitted trajectory. The normal equations in modern matrix notation are the algebraic expression of minimizing that squared discrepancy. The later normal-error model attached a probability law to the residuals, enabling the exact variance, $t$ and $F$ calculations developed above. The historical publication and the modern probability model are related steps, not identical claims about what the earliest orbital analysis assumed.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 304–307, least-squares method and Legendre){{cite:kulich2022_regression}} (§§1.2–1.3, pp. 7–8, normal-model results)
