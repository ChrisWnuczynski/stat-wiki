## Lead context

The exact distribution of an estimator is often unknown, or known only under assumptions too narrow for the data. Asymptotic analysis replaces that distribution by a limit taken as the sample size grows, and then uses the limit as an approximation at the sample size actually in hand. In Bayesian work the same name covers the limiting behaviour of a posterior.

Gelman and coauthors describe asymptotic theory as the collection of properties that hold in the limit as $n$ becomes large, useful as approximations and as a way to see when a prior fades, and not as a promise that every large sample looks normal.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 4, p. 83)

## Definition

Asymptotic analysis, in statistics, is the study of those limits and the use of the limits as working approximations. For a posterior, the basic large-sample tool is asymptotic normality. As more observations arrive from the same process, the posterior distribution of the parameter vector approaches a multivariate normal, even when the distribution that generated the data is not inside the parametric family. The observations to which the theorems apply most directly are independent draws $y_1,\ldots,y_n$ from a common distribution. If that distribution is inside the family, at a parameter value $\omega_0$, consistency holds as well: the posterior converges to a point mass at $\omega_0$. If the true distribution is outside the family, the point that plays the role of $\omega_0$ is the parameter that makes the model closest to the true distribution in Kullback–Leibler divergence.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 4, §4.2, pp. 87–88)

Under regularity conditions, notably a likelihood continuous in the parameter and a limit point that is not on the boundary of the parameter space, the posterior approaches normality with mean $\omega_0$ and variance $(n J(\omega_0))^{-1}$, where $J$ is the Fisher information for one observation. The curvature of the log posterior is one prior term plus a sum of $n$ likelihood terms. For large $n$ the sum dominates, the curvature is about $n J$, and the likelihood alone determines the mode and the curvature used in the normal approximation. The practical summary is equation (4.2) of Gelman and coauthors: the posterior is approximated by a normal centred at the posterior mode, with covariance the inverse of the observed information at that mode.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 4, §4.1–§4.2, pp. 85–88)

A parallel limit for sample proportions is the classical central limit theorem. When observations are independent and the sample is large enough that $np\ge 10$ and $n(1-p)\ge 10$, the sample proportion $\hat p$ is approximately normal with mean $p$ and standard error $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$. The de Moivre–Laplace theorem is the binomial case of that limit: a binomial count, written as a sum of Bernoulli trials, has a standardised version whose distribution function approaches the standard normal as $n$ grows. The approximation is

$$
\mathbb{P}(X_n \ge x) \approx 1-\Phi\left(\frac{x-\tfrac12-np}{\sqrt{npq}}\right),
$$


with the continuity correction of one half, as the form that improves on the approximation without the half.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, §5.1.3){{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 356–357)

## Statistical use

The everyday product on the sampling side is a standard error. For a proportion, the standard error $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$ is largest at $p=1/2$ and shrinks like $1/\sqrt n$. Intervals and tests for a proportion are built from that normal approximation once the success-failure condition holds. The same style of approximation, a point estimate and a standard error from a normal limit, is what a large-sample posterior interval uses when the normal approximation to the posterior is adequate.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, §5.1.3){{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 4, §4.4, pp. 91–92)

A numerical check of the binomial approximation is the probability of 27 or more successes in 100 trials with success probability $0.2$, the sum Dodge writes from $k=27$ to $100$. The mean is $np=20$ and the variance is $npq=16$, so the standard deviation is $4$. Without the continuity correction the standardised point is $(27-20)/4 = 1.75$. Dodge's normal table gives $\Phi(1.75)=0.9599$, and $1-0.9599=0.0401$. With the correction of one half the point is $(27-0.5-20)/4 = 1.625$, and the same table gives approximately $0.0521$. The exact binomial probability of that sum is $0.0558$ to four decimal places. The corrected normal value is the closer of the two approximations, and both are limits used at a finite $n$ of 100.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 357)

On the posterior side, the matching statement is about coverage. When the normal approximation holds and the model contains the true distribution, a central posterior interval has, in the limit, the same repeated-sampling coverage as the corresponding interval for the posterior mode. A 95 percent central posterior interval then covers the true parameter in 95 percent of repeated samples as $n\to\infty$. That correspondence is a theorem about two readings of one approximation. In smaller samples Gelman and coauthors prefer to report the posterior itself, for example by 50 percent and 95 percent central intervals, rather than to collapse it to a point.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 4, §4.4, pp. 91–92)

The same style of limit is used to compare procedures that are not estimators of a single parameter. An asymptotic comparison of local polynomial smoothers is used to explain why odd-degree fits dominate even-degree fits in mean squared error when boundary behaviour dominates the error, and why a local linear fit is the more reliable default when the goal is extrapolation near the edge of the data. The comparison is a limit statement about bias and variance.{{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (Ch. 6, §6.1.2, p. 198)

## Common misconceptions

A large sample does not put every posterior under a normal curve. The theorems require the limit point to lie off the boundary. If the true value sits on the boundary, the Taylor expansion is cut off in some directions and the limiting shape need not be normal. Gelman and coauthors give the model $y_i \sim N(\omega, 1)$ with the restriction $\omega\ge 0$ and true value $\omega=0$: the limiting posterior is half a normal, centred at 0 and truncated to the positive side. If the true value is $-1$, outside the parameter space, the limiting posterior spikes at 0 and does not look normal at all.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 4, §4.3, pp. 89–90)

An unidentified parameter is a second failure. If the likelihood is constant across a range of parameter values, there is no single point for the posterior to converge to. A prior that puts zero density on the limit point, or on a neighbourhood of it, also blocks the argument that the likelihood dominates the prior. The normal approximation can be accurate for most of the posterior mass and still fail in the tails: a parameter constrained to be positive is given negative values by a normal approximation at any finite $n$, and that error only recedes further into the tail as $n$ grows.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 4, §4.3, pp. 89–91)

The success-failure condition is the corresponding warning for a sample proportion. Independence and a large $n$ are not enough when $p$ is so close to 0 or 1 that $np<10$ or $n(1-p)<10$. The standard error formula remains defined, and the normal approximation is then the wrong shape. The de Moivre–Laplace calculation at $n=100$ and $p=0.2$ is inside the condition, since $np=20$ and $n(1-p)=80$, and even there the uncorrected normal probability $0.0401$ is visibly short of the exact probability $0.0558$ of 27 or more successes.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, §5.1.3){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 357)

## History

The inferential tradition that treats limits of averages as data analysis traces its origins to Bayes's essay of 1763, de Moivre's *Doctrine of Chances* of 1718, Gauss's theory of errors of 1809, and Laplace's analytic theory of 1812. De Moivre's book is also where the normal density appears in probability calculations for games of chance; Gauss took that density into the theory of measurement. The de Moivre–Laplace theorem is the binomial form of the normal limit, and it is a particular case of the central limit theorem, obtained by writing the binomial count as a sum of Bernoulli random variables. Pearson's chi-squared criterion of 1900, and the later sampling theory of Neyman, Egon Pearson, and Fisher, then needed limit distributions whenever an exact law was unavailable.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 263–264, 356–357)

The modern organisation of those limits, for posteriors, is the large-sample theory of consistency and asymptotic normality, with an explicit list of cases in which the normal limit does not arrive: a parameter on the boundary, a likelihood that does not identify the parameter, a prior that excludes the limit point, and tails that the normal approximation misrepresents at every finite $n$. Those cases are why an asymptotic approximation is reported together with the condition under which it was derived.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 4, §4.2–§4.3, pp. 87–91)
