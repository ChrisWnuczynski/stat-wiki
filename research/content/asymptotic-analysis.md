## Lead context

The exact distribution of an estimator is often unknown, or known only under assumptions too narrow for the data. Asymptotic analysis replaces that distribution by a limit taken as the sample size grows, and then uses the limit as an approximation at the sample size actually in hand. The approximation is a theorem with conditions, not a promise that every large sample looks normal.{{cite:hansen2010}} (Ch. 5, §5.1, p. 65){{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 4, opening, p. 83)

## Definition — Limits as the sample size grows

Asymptotic analysis, in statistics, is the study of properties that hold in the limit as the number of observations becomes large, together with the use of those limits as working approximations. In the sampling theory of estimators the standard name for the same project is asymptotic theory. It approximates a sampling distribution by the limit of the finite-sample distribution as $n$ tends to infinity. The primary tools are the weak law of large numbers, the central limit theorem, and the continuous mapping theorem.{{cite:hansen2010}} (Ch. 5, §5.1, p. 65){{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 4, opening, p. 83)

The scope covers consistency, asymptotic normality, and the delta method for smooth functions of an estimator. Consistency is convergence in probability of the estimator to the parameter: for every fixed tolerance, the probability of missing by more than that tolerance goes to zero. For an average of independent observations with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$, the variance of the sample mean is $\sigma^2/n$, which tends to zero, and Chebyshev’s inequality turns that variance into the probability bound. Asymptotic normality is the further claim that, after centring and scaling by $\sqrt n$, the estimator’s distribution approaches a normal law, so that a standard error and a normal critical value become usable.{{cite:hansen2010}} (Ch. 5, §5.2, p. 66; §5.1, p. 65)

A parallel development applies to posterior distributions. Under regularity conditions, as independent observations accumulate from a fixed model, the posterior concentrates at the value that makes the model closest to the distribution generating the data, and it approaches a normal shape whose variance shrinks like one over $n$ times the Fisher information. The prior’s influence fades in that limit. The likelihood then determines both the centre and the curvature used in the normal approximation.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 4, §4.2, pp. 87–88)

## Statistical use

The everyday product is a standard error. For a sample proportion with independent observations and a large enough sample, the central limit theorem supplies a normal sampling distribution with mean equal to the population proportion and standard error $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$. Intervals and tests for means and regression coefficients are built the same way once an estimator is known to be approximately normal. In regression, the asymptotic covariance of least squares often has a sandwich form, which stays valid when the variance of the error depends on the covariates, provided the moment conditions hold.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, §5.1.3, p. 172){{cite:hansen2010}} (Ch. 5, §5.1, p. 65)

The same style of argument compares procedures. An asymptotic comparison of local polynomial smoothers, for example, is used to explain why odd-degree fits dominate even-degree fits in mean squared error when boundary behaviour matters, and why a local linear fit is the more reliable default when the goal is extrapolation near the edge of the data. The comparison is a limit statement about bias and variance, not a finite-sample ranking for every design.{{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (Ch. 6, §6.1.2, p. 198)

Asymptotic results are also the bridge between a posterior interval and a repeated-sampling coverage statement. When the normal approximation to the posterior holds and the model contains the true distribution, a 95% central posterior interval covers the true parameter in 95% of repeated samples in the limit. That correspondence is a theorem about two readings of one approximation.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 4, §4.4, pp. 91–92)

## History

Large-sample reasoning grew out of the probability results that already described averages. The inferential tradition traces its origins to de Moivre’s doctrine of chances, Bayes’s essay, Gauss’s theory of errors, and Laplace’s analytic theory, the setting in which laws of large numbers and normal approximations to sums became tools for data. Pearson’s chi-squared criterion and the later sampling theory of estimators then needed limit distributions whenever an exact law was unavailable.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 263–264)

Twentieth-century asymptotic theory organised those limits into a method: consistency from the weak law, normality from the central limit theorem, and transformations from the continuous mapping and delta-method arguments. Bayesian large-sample theory developed the matching normal approximation for the posterior, with explicit counterexamples when the parameter sits on a boundary, when a parameter is unidentified, or when the number of parameters grows with the sample. Those counterexamples are part of the subject, because they show which limits are not free.{{cite:hansen2010}} (Ch. 5, §5.1, p. 65){{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 4, §4.2, pp. 87–88; §4.3, p. 89)
