## Motivating example
A reliability analyst may know survival probabilities more easily than a lifetime density. For a nonnegative lifetime $T$, each realisation is the length of the interval lying below it: $T=\int_0^\infty\mathbf1_{\{T>t\}}\,dt$. Averaging those lengths should give the area under the survival curve. The exchange is valid because the integrand is nonnegative, including when that area is infinite. A finite mean is therefore a conclusion to check after the exchange, rather than a prerequisite for writing the identity.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.7, thm. 1.7.2 and ex. 1.7.2)

## Definition — Two sufficient conditions for exchanging integrals
Let $(X,\mathcal A,\mu)$ and $(Y,\mathcal B,\nu)$ be sigma-finite measure spaces, and let $f$ be measurable for their product sigma-algebra. For $f\ge0$, Tonelli's theorem identifies the product integral with either iterated integral in $[0,\infty]$. For real-valued $f$ satisfying $\int|f|\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)<\infty$, Fubini's theorem gives the same equality with a finite signed value:
$$\int f\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)=\int_X\!\left(\int_Y f(x,y)\,d\nu(y)\right)d\mu(x)=\int_Y\!\left(\int_X f(x,y)\,d\mu(x)\right)d\nu(y).$$
In the integrable case, sections are integrable for almost every outer coordinate. Exceptional sections of measure zero do not change the outer integral. A probability measure is finite and hence sigma-finite; a product of a probability measure and Lebesgue measure on $[0,\infty)$ is also within these hypotheses.{{cite:tao2011_measure}} (§1.7, thms. 1.7.20–1.7.21 and cor. 1.7.23)

## Properties — From rectangles to signed functions
The mechanism is a sequence of extensions. For a rectangle $A\times B$, both iterated integrals of its indicator are $\mu(A)\nu(B)$. First consider finite measures. The measurable sets for which the section-integral identity holds form a lambda-system: the whole product belongs, complements preserve the identity by subtracting from the finite total, and disjoint countable unions preserve it by monotone convergence. Rectangles form a generating pi-system, so the pi–lambda theorem extends the identity to every product-measurable set. For sigma-finite measures, restrict to increasing rectangles of finite measure and take limits.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.7, lems. 1.7.3–1.7.4)

Linearity then proves the claim for nonnegative simple functions. Approximate a nonnegative measurable $f$ by increasing simple functions and apply monotone convergence to the inner and outer integrals. Finally write a real integrable function as $f^+-f^-$. Absolute integrability makes both nonnegative integrals finite, so their difference is defined in either order. This last subtraction explains why allowing two infinite signed contributions would break the argument.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.7, proof of thm. 1.7.2)

## Statistical use — Tail probabilities and random sums
Applying Tonelli to the lifetime indicator yields $\mathbb ET=\int_0^\infty\Pr(T>t)\,dt$. For an integer-valued $N\ge0$, use counting measure to obtain $\mathbb EN=\sum_{k\ge1}\Pr(N\ge k)$. More generally, for $r>0$, the pathwise identity $T^r=\int_0^T r t^{r-1}\,dt$ gives $\mathbb ET^r=\int_0^\infty r t^{r-1}\Pr(T>t)\,dt$, with infinity permitted. These statements require neither a continuous distribution nor a density. An atom at zero contributes no lifetime, while other atoms contribute through their tail intervals.{{cite:tao2011_measure}} (§1.7, ex. 1.7.25)

The same principle justifies exchanging an expectation and an infinite nonnegative sum. For signed random contributions $Z_k$, a sufficient condition is $\sum_k\mathbb E|Z_k|<\infty$. It makes the absolute sum integrable and permits $\mathbb E\sum_k Z_k=\sum_k\mathbb EZ_k$. A finite number of terms needs only integrability of those terms; an infinite sequence requires a further argument.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.7, corollary following thm. 1.7.2)

## Worked example — Integrating a triangular region
On $0<x<y<1$, let $f(x,y)=6x$, and set it to zero elsewhere. Integrating first over $x$ gives $\int_0^1\int_0^y6x\,dx\,dy=\int_0^1 3y^2\,dy=1$. Reversing the order gives $\int_0^1\int_x^1 6x\,dy\,dx=\int_0^1 6x(1-x)\,dx=1$. Thus $f$ is a joint density. Its marginal density for $Y$ is $3y^2$ and its mean is $\mathbb EY=\int_0^1 3y^3\,dy=3/4$. The geometry determines the limits: fixing $y$ leaves $0<x<y$, while fixing $x$ leaves $x<y<1$.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The marginal density of $X$ is $6x(1-x)$. Hence $\mathbb EX=\int_0^1 6x^2(1-x)\,dx=1/2$.

## Assumptions and limits — Having two answers is a warning
A signed double series can have convergent row and column sums yet disagree between orders. Put $a_{mn}=1$ when $m=n$, $a_{mn}=-1$ when $m=n+1$, and zero otherwise, with indices starting at one. Each column sums to zero. The first row sums to one and every later row sums to zero, so summing the row totals gives one. The absolute double sum is infinite. Neither absolute summability nor nonnegativity holds, so the theorem supplies no order-invariance. A convenient iterated calculation alone does not establish the hypotheses that make it legitimate.{{cite:tao2011_measure}} (§1.7, ex. 1.7.23; rem. 0.0.3)

## Interpretation — The limits describe the region, in either order

A triangular support forces the limits to change when the order of integration changes. The value does not, once the hypotheses hold. For the nonnegative function that equals $2$ on $0<y<x<1$ and equals $0$ elsewhere, integrating $x$ first means that each fixed $y\in(0,1)$ contributes an inner integral from $x=y$ to $x=1$. The inner integral equals $2(1-y)$, and the outer integral equals $1$. Integrating $y$ first runs $y$ from $0$ to the fixed $x$. Both descriptions are the same set of pairs. Writing the limits of the unit square instead would integrate a different region.

Tonelli's nonnegative form is the one used for a lifetime mean. If the integral of the survival function diverges, the identity still holds and the mean is infinite. Absolute integrability is the extra condition that lets a signed integrand be split into a positive part and a negative part without producing an indeterminate difference of infinities. Two finite answers that disagree have left that condition, or they have integrated two different regions. A convenient order is a computational choice. It is not evidence that the interchange was free.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.7, thm. 1.7.2 and ex. 1.7.2){{cite:tao2011_measure}} (§1.7, thms. 1.7.20–1.7.21 and cor. 1.7.23)

## Common misconceptions
Two finite iterated integrals that agree are the expected outcome when the hypotheses hold. Two finite iterated integrals that disagree are a signal that the hypotheses have failed, or that the two integrals describe different regions. The signed array with $a_{nn}=1$, $a_{n,n+1}=-1$ and zeros elsewhere has column sums equal to zero and row sums equal to one in the first row and zero afterwards. Summing columns first gives zero. Summing rows first gives one. The sum of absolute values is infinite, so neither absolute convergence nor nonnegativity is available, and the two orders are free to differ.{{cite:tao2011_measure}} (§1.7, ex. 1.7.23; rem. 0.0.3)

A nonnegative integrand is the case Tonelli's theorem covers, including the case where the integral is infinite. The expectation of a nonnegative random variable equals the integral of its survival function, $\int_0^\infty P(X>t)\,dt$. If that integral diverges, the identity still holds and the mean is infinite. Absolute integrability is the extra condition that lets a signed integrand be split into a positive part and a negative part whose integrals are both finite. An indeterminate $\infty-\infty$ is the outcome the extra condition is there to avoid.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.7, thm. 1.7.2 and ex. 1.7.2){{cite:tao2011_measure}} (§1.7, thms. 1.7.20–1.7.21 and cor. 1.7.23)

## Connections
The order of integration is a description of the same set when the limits are rewritten with the region. For the density that equals $6x$ on $0<x<y<1$, integrating $x$ first uses $0<x<y$ for each fixed $y$ and returns $\int_0^1 3y^2\,dy=1$. Integrating $y$ first uses $x<y<1$ for each fixed $x$ and returns $\int_0^1 6x(1-x)\,dx=1$. The marginal density of $Y$ is $3y^2$ and $EY=3/4$. The marginal density of $X$ is $6x(1-x)$ and $EX=1/2$. Both orders integrate the triangle. Integrating over the whole unit square instead would add the region $y<x$, where this function was defined to be zero, and a different function that is nonzero there would be a different integral.{{cite:tao2011_measure}} (§1.7, thms. 1.7.20–1.7.21 and cor. 1.7.23)

The same bookkeeping applies to a random sum. If $N$ is a nonnegative integer-valued random variable and the $X_i$ are conditionally independent given $N$ with a common conditional mean, the iterated expectation $E(X_1+\cdots+X_N)=E(E(X_1+\cdots+X_N\mid N))$ is an integral over the product of the two laws. Nonnegativity lets the sum and the expectation change order even when the mean is infinite. A signed sum needs the absolute values to have finite expectation before the same rearrangement is justified. Writing the iterated integral in the convenient order is a computational choice after that justification, and the value does not depend on the order once the justification is in place.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.7, thm. 1.7.2 and ex. 1.7.2){{cite:tao2011_measure}} (§1.7, thms. 1.7.20–1.7.21 and cor. 1.7.23)

## History
Fubini's theorem and Tonelli's theorem are the two sufficient conditions used for these integrals. Tonelli applies to nonnegative measurable functions and allows the value $+\infty$. Fubini applies to integrable signed functions, those whose absolute value has a finite integral, and returns a finite value. Both conclude that the two iterated integrals exist and equal the double integral. The triangle density $6x$ is nonnegative and integrates to one, so Tonelli applies and the two orders are a check of the limits. The signed counterexample has an infinite absolute sum, so it sits outside both theorems and the two orders are allowed to differ.{{cite:tao2011_measure}} (§1.7, thms. 1.7.20–1.7.21 and cor. 1.7.23){{cite:durrett2019}} (§1.7, thm. 1.7.2 and ex. 1.7.2)
