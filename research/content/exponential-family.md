## Lead context
A density in the class is a base factor, free of the parameter, times an exponential tilt. The tilt is a natural parameter times a statistic, minus a log-normaliser that makes the probabilities sum or integrate to one.

## Motivating example — Poisson, binomial, and normal share a log-likelihood shape
A [Poisson](poisson-distribution.qmd) count, a [binomial](binomial-distribution.qmd) success total and a [normal](normal-distribution.qmd) measurement have different possible values, yet each can be written as a parameter multiplying an observation statistic, minus a normalising term. In the Poisson case the coefficient is the log mean; in the binomial case it is the log odds. The same derivative and factorisation arguments apply to each likelihood because each is an exponential tilt of a base factor.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§§1.1, 3.1–3.3, pp. 371, 376–380){{cite:geyer2022_exp_family}} (§§7.1–7.2, 9.1, “More About Examples” and “Independent and Identically Distributed Sampling”)

## Definition — Natural parameter, log-normaliser, and base factor
When the statistic is the observation itself, the unscaled form is $f(y\mid\eta)=h(y)\exp\{\eta y-A(\eta)\}$. The natural parameter $\eta$ multiplies $y$, the log-normaliser $A$ is chosen so that the density sums or integrates to one, and the base factor $h$ does not depend on $\eta$. For a vector statistic $T(x)$, the product in the exponent is $\eta^{\mathsf T}T(x)$, with $\eta$ and $T(x)$ of the same dimension.{{cite:geyer2022_exp_family}} (§§3.1, 3.7, “Definitions” and “Probability Mass-Density Functions”)

The dispersion-scaled GLM writing has log density $[y\theta+c(\theta)]/e(\phi)+d(y;\phi)$, where $\theta$ is the canonical parameter, $e(\phi)>0$ is the dispersion factor, and $c(\theta)$ is the term that does not depend on $y$. Holding $e(\phi)$ fixed and differentiating the normaliser in $\theta$ gives the conditional mean $-c'(\theta)$. When the observation statistic, written $a(y)$, equals $y$ and $\phi$ is fixed, the two writings describe the same density under $\eta=\theta/e(\phi)$, $A(\eta)=-c(\theta)/e(\phi)$ and $h(y)=\exp\{d(y;\phi)\}$. This writing calls $\theta$ canonical, and the unscaled writing calls $\eta$ natural. The two parameters agree when $e(\phi)=1$.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§1.1, p. 371)

For the fixed-variance normal example, $\theta=\mu$ and $e(\phi)=\sigma^2$, so $\eta=\mu/\sigma^2$ in the unscaled form. For Poisson, $e(\phi)=1$, $\eta=\log\lambda$ and $A(\eta)=e^\eta$. For a binomial total of $n$ trials, $\eta=\log\{p/(1-p)\}$ and $A(\eta)=n\log(1+e^\eta)$. In each case $Y$ denotes a random response and $y$ its realised value.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§§3.1–3.3, pp. 376–380){{cite:geyer2022_exp_family}} (§§7.1–7.2, 7.5, “More About Examples”)

## Interpretation — Tilting the base measure, and why the sum is sufficient
The base factor $h$ accounts for features that do not change with the parameter; $A$ makes the probabilities sum or integrate to one. Changing $\eta$ tilts the relative weights of possible observations according to $T(x)$. For an iid sample of size $n$, the likelihood depends on the data through $S(\boldsymbol x)=\sum_{i=1}^n T(x_i)$, where $\boldsymbol x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$ records the realised observations. Thus $S$ is sufficient under the model, although the data can still be needed to assess whether the model fits.{{cite:geyer2022_exp_family}} (§§3.7, 8.3, 9.1, “Probability Mass-Density Functions,” “The Neyman-Fisher Factorization Criterion,” and “Independent and Identically Distributed Sampling”)

## Properties — Moments from A, and the conjugate hyperparameter update
For the unscaled form, $\nabla_\eta A(\eta)=\mathbb E_\eta T(X)$ and $\nabla_\eta^2A(\eta)=\operatorname{Cov}_\eta\{T(X)\}$ when differentiation can pass under the normalising integral or sum. The Hessian is therefore positive semidefinite; it is also the one-observation Fisher information in $\eta$ under the score regularity conditions. The dispersion-scaled mean $\mathbb E(Y)=-c'(\theta)$ and variance $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=-e(\phi)c''(\theta)$ follow from the same normalisation argument with the dispersion scale held fixed.{{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§2, eqs. 2.1–2.2, p. 271){{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§1.1, p. 371)

With a proper conjugate prior on $\eta$ proportional to $\exp\{\eta^{\mathsf T}\alpha-\nu A(\eta)\}$, the posterior replaces $\alpha$ by $\alpha+S(\boldsymbol x)$ and $\nu$ by $\nu+n$. The vector $\alpha$ and scalar $\nu$ are prior hyperparameters, distinct from any regression coefficients. This is a kernel identity; whether it defines probability distributions requires finite normalising integrals.{{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§2, eq. 2.3 and Theorem 1, pp. 271–272){{cite:geyer2025_conjugate}} (§§2.1–2.2, eqs. 2.3–2.5)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Normalisation gives $1=\int h(x)\exp\{\eta^{\mathsf T}T(x)-A(\eta)\}\,dx$, with a sum in the discrete case. Differentiating in $\eta$ gives $0=\mathbb E_\eta T(X)-\nabla_\eta A(\eta)$. Differentiating once more gives $0=\operatorname{Cov}_\eta\{T(X)\}-\nabla_\eta^2A(\eta)$. Every covariance matrix is positive semidefinite. The score for one observation is $T(X)-\nabla_\eta A(\eta)$, whose covariance is the same Hessian; this is the Fisher information under the stated regularity conditions. For the scalar dispersion-scaled form, differentiating the normalisation in $\theta$ gives $\mathbb EY=-c'(\theta)$, and a second derivative gives $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=-e(\phi)c''(\theta)$ when $e(\phi)$ is held fixed.{{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§2, eqs. 2.1–2.2, p. 271){{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§1.1, p. 371)

For independent observations, multiplying their densities yields $\{\prod_i h(x_i)\}\exp\{\eta^{\mathsf T}S(\boldsymbol x)-nA(\eta)\}$. The parameter-dependent part contains the data only through $S$, so the factorisation criterion makes $S$ sufficient. Multiplying that likelihood by the displayed conjugate-prior kernel gives $\exp\{\eta^{\mathsf T}[\alpha+S(\boldsymbol x)]-(\nu+n)A(\eta)\}$, proving the update whenever the posterior is normalisable.{{cite:geyer2022_exp_family}} (§§8.3, 9.1, “The Neyman-Fisher Factorization Criterion” and “Independent and Identically Distributed Sampling”){{cite:geyer2025_conjugate}} (§2.2, eqs. 2.4–2.5)
:::

## Statistical use — A family does not excuse a bad link or dependence
In a GLM, choose a response family whose support matches the observed variable and specify a link for its conditional mean. For a Poisson count the natural log parameter gives a log link; for a Bernoulli response the log odds give a logit link. The likelihood can then be fitted using scores based on the sufficient statistics, while the model's mean–variance relation guides assessment of its spread. An exponential-family expression alone does not guarantee that a chosen link, independence assumption or variance model fits the data.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§§1.1–2.4, 3.2–3.3, pp. 371–376, 377–380)

In Bayesian work, a conjugate prior permits the hyperparameter update by addition, which can simplify repeated analysis. It does not require a prior to be conjugate: other priors can be combined with the same likelihood, though their posteriors may need numerical calculation. Check that the proposed prior and posterior are proper before using their moments.{{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§§1–2, pp. 269–272){{cite:geyer2025_conjugate}} (§§2.1–2.4, “Conjugate Priors” through “Proper Priors and Posteriors”)

## Worked example — Four Poisson counts, and a Gamma prior on the mean
Suppose four independent Poisson counts are $1,0,2,1$. Their total is $S=1+0+2+1=4$, so the likelihood for their common mean $\lambda$ is proportional to $\lambda^4e^{-4\lambda}$ and its maximum occurs at $\hat\lambda=S/4=1$. On the natural scale $\eta=\log\lambda$, the sufficient statistic is the count itself and $A(\eta)=e^\eta$. Thus the same four numbers enter the likelihood only through their total.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§3.2, pp. 377–378){{cite:geyer2022_exp_family}} (§§7.2, 9.1, “The Poisson Distribution” and “Independent and Identically Distributed Sampling”)

Give $\lambda$ a Gamma prior with shape $2$ and rate $1$, whose density is proportional to $\lambda^{2-1}e^{-\lambda}$. Multiplication by the likelihood gives a Gamma posterior with shape $2+4=6$ and rate $1+4=5$, hence posterior mean $6/5=1.2$. In the natural parameter $\eta$, the prior density includes the Jacobian $d\lambda/d\eta=\lambda$ and has kernel $\exp\{2\eta-e^\eta\}$: its hyperparameters are $\alpha=2$ and $\nu=1$. The general update gives $\alpha'=2+4=6$ and $\nu'=1+4=5$, agreeing with the Gamma calculation.{{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§§1–2, pp. 269–272){{cite:geyer2025_conjugate}} (§§2.1–2.2, eqs. 2.3–2.5)

**Your turn.** Four independent Poisson counts are $0,1,1,0$. Find the maximum-likelihood estimate of their common mean $\lambda$, then update a Gamma prior with shape $2$ and rate $1$.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The total is $S=2$, so $\widehat\lambda=S/4=1/2$. The likelihood contributes $\lambda^2e^{-4\lambda}$; the posterior is Gamma with shape $2+2=4$ and rate $1+4=5$, giving posterior mean $4/5$.
:::

## Assumptions and limits — When support depends on the parameter
The displayed densities use a common support for parameter values in the regular family. If support moves with the parameter, the usual derivative interchange and the Pitman–Koopman–Darmois characterisation can fail: a sample maximum remains sufficient for a $\operatorname{Uniform}(0,\theta)$ model even though its support depends on $\theta$. The converse characterisation requires additional regularity conditions on the distribution of the sufficient statistic.{{cite:geyer2022_exp_family}} (§§3.7, 9.4, “Probability Mass-Density Functions” and “The Pitman–Koopman–Darmois Theorem”)

## Connections — Natural parameter, canonical link, and sufficiency
The [natural parameter](natural-parameter.qmd) is the coefficient of the statistic in the exponent. A [canonical link](canonical-link.qmd) sets the linear predictor equal to that coefficient, which is why the GLM score reduces to $X^\top(y-\mu)$ and $X^\top y$ is [sufficient](sufficient-statistic.qmd) for $\beta$.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§§1.1–1.3, 2.2, pp. 371–374) Independently of any link, the sum of the statistics is sufficient for $\eta$ in an iid sample.{{cite:geyer2022_exp_family}} (§§8.3, 9.1, “The Neyman-Fisher Factorization Criterion” and “Independent and Identically Distributed Sampling”) The [conjugate update](generic-exponential-family-conjugate-update.qmd) adds that sum to the prior hyperparameters, because the likelihood depends on the sample only through it.{{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§2, eq. 2.3, pp. 271–272){{cite:geyer2025_conjugate}} (§§2.1–2.2, eqs. 2.3–2.5)

## History — Conjugate priors on the natural parameter
Nelder and Wedderburn's 1972 paper used this exponential-family form for the response in a generalised linear model.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (Summary; Introduction; §§1–3, pp. 370–380)

Diaconis and Ylvisaker's 1979 study of conjugate priors for exponential families investigated when such priors are proper and when posterior expectations of the mean parameter are linear in the observed statistic. Their general treatment connected familiar beta–binomial and gamma–Poisson updates with a common natural-parameter construction. A later account by Geyer sets out the additive hyperparameter update and its propriety conditions.{{cite:diaconis_ylvisaker1979}} (§§1–2, pp. 269–272){{cite:geyer2025_conjugate}} (§§2.1–2.4, “Theory”)
