## Lead context
Universities now award degrees in data science, and the courses overlap statistics without always sitting inside statistics departments. Data science, in the sense argued for by people who work on the analysis of data, is the technical work of learning from data: models, computing, theory, and the evaluation of tools, judged by whether the analyst learns more.{{cite:cleveland2001}} (pp. 21–22) {{cite:donoho2015}} (§8.1)

## Intuition
A department that only proves theorems about estimators will still send its graduates to sit with subject-matter data. Cleveland's premise is that the technical areas should be judged by whether they help that person learn from the data. A tool helps directly when the analyst uses it. A theory helps indirectly when it is the basis for such a tool. The Bayesian account of estimation is his example of a theory with that kind of reach: it affects methods of estimation and of distribution generally, not one data set. The name "data science" is the name he gives the enlarged field, because the change he wants is large enough that the old name would understate it.{{cite:cleveland2001}} (p. 21)

Donoho's later survey distinguishes a narrower public meaning from that programme. The public meaning is often a superset of statistics and machine learning plus the technology for scaling to large data, motivated by commercial demand. His alternative, which he calls greater data science, follows Tukey, Cleveland, Chambers, and Breiman: the science of learning from data, organised so that the activity itself can be studied. The six divisions he writes down are a way of seeing the whole job, not a list of software products.{{cite:donoho2015}} (§8.1)

## Definition
William Cleveland used the name for an enlargement of statistics organised around the practising analyst. Six areas share the effort, with shares of the resources that remain after the existing curriculum is taught, and the same shares as a guide to the courses. Multidisciplinary investigations, 25 percent, are collaborations on subject-matter data. Models and methods, 20 percent, cover statistical models, model building, and probabilistic estimation. Computing with data, 15 percent, covers hardware, software, and algorithms. Pedagogy is 15 percent. Tool evaluation is 5 percent: surveys of tools in use, of needs, and of how tools are developed. Theory is 20 percent: foundations, and mathematical investigation of the other areas. A tool counts if it helps the analyst directly. A theory counts if it helps build such tools.{{cite:cleveland2001}} (pp. 21–22)

David Donoho groups the work of greater data science into six divisions that restate and widen that plan. Data exploration and preparation covers the detective work Tukey called exploratory analysis, and the cleaning, recoding, grouping, and subsetting that make a file ready. Data representation and transformation covers the many formats an analyst meets and the work of moving among them. Computing with data is its own division. Data modelling is the statistical and predictive core. Data visualisation and presentation is separated from exploration so that the display of a result is not confused with the search that produced it. Science about data science is the sixth: evidence about how analyses are done, and about what changes when the workflow changes.{{cite:donoho2015}} (§8.1)

The model shelf inside the modelling division is the statistical one. [Linear regression](multiple-linear-regression.qmd), [generalised linear models](generalised-linear-model.qmd), and [regularisation](regularisation.qmd) such as [ridge regression](ridge-regression.qmd) are standard when the number of predictors is large. [Cross-validation](cross-validation.qmd) and a held-out test set estimate how a fitted rule behaves on new cases. [Classification](classification.qmd) and [regression trees](regression-tree.qmd), including [random forests](random-forest.qmd), are used when the goal is prediction. [Principal components](principal-component-directions.qmd) and other low-dimensional summaries handle high-dimensional measurements. Those methods are the content of Cleveland's "models and methods" area and of Donoho's modelling division. They are not the whole of either plan.{{cite:cleveland2001}} (p. 21) {{cite:donoho2015}} (§8.1)

## Statistical use
Exploration is not a preface to be skipped. Donoho's first division says that a large share of the effort goes into learning what a messy file contains so that it can be used, and he identifies two subactivities. Exploration is the sanity check and the search for unexpected features, in the line of Tukey's exploratory data analysis. Preparation is the response to anomalies and artifacts: reformatting, recoding, grouping, smoothing, subsetting. He notes the common claim that most of the effort in a data-science project is this work, and he treats the claim as a reason to teach the work rather than as a complaint about it.{{cite:donoho2015}} (§8.1)

Computing is one of the six areas in both plans, and Cleveland gives it a share that a classical theory department did not have. He argues that computing with data had been recognised by computer scientists and still needed more resources inside statistics. Donoho's computing division is the same claim in the later survey: languages, workflows, and the ability to carry a step from one project to the next. A model that cannot be refit by someone else has not finished the computing area, whatever its coefficients.{{cite:cleveland2001}} (pp. 21–22) {{cite:donoho2015}} (§8.1)

Modelling splits, in the history Donoho reviews, into a generative culture aimed at the mechanism and a predictive culture aimed at accuracy on future inputs. Breiman's 2001 essay is the source he uses for that split. Cleveland's models-and-methods area is where both cultures sit when the department is organised his way: probabilistic estimation is named explicitly, and so is model building. The predictive culture does not leave the plan. It is one use of the models area, judged by whether the analyst learns from the data, which for a prediction problem includes whether the rule works on cases it did not fit.{{cite:donoho2015}} (§5; §8.1) {{cite:cleveland2001}} (p. 21)

Multidisciplinary projects are the largest share in Cleveland's allocation, and he gives the historical reason. Fisher on experimental design came from agricultural data, Tukey on the spectrum from physical and engineering data, Box on response surfaces from chemical process data. The plan's 25 percent is the claim that new tools and theories are stimulated by subject-matter problems, and that students should analyse data as a required part of the programme. Donoho's sixth division, science about data science, is the later addition: not only do the projects, but study how the analysis is carried out, so that a proposal to change a workflow can be judged by its effect on the validity of the result.{{cite:cleveland2001}} (p. 22) {{cite:donoho2015}} (§8.1)

## Assumptions and limits
The public meaning Donoho criticises — statistics plus machine learning plus technology for large data, adopted because employers ask for it — can miss the intellectual programme. He argues that choosing the field that way is likely to miss the important development, which is the study of data analysis across science, not only the scaling of one company's files. A degree that adds information-technology courses to a statistics core has not, by that addition, adopted the six divisions. The curriculum he inspects at one new master's programme is mostly material a statistics department could teach, with storage and retrieval as the piece that looks new, and with machine learning in the title as a signal that prediction is weighted more heavily than inference.{{cite:donoho2015}} (§2.4; §7; §8.1)

Cleveland's percentages are a proposal for a department, not a theorem about every project. A single analysis can be almost entirely modelling, or almost entirely preparation. The percentages are shares of a department's effort and of its courses. Tool evaluation, at 5 percent, is the area he thinks statisticians already preach to other fields and rarely apply to their own software. Pedagogy, at 15 percent, is there because many graduates teach and almost none were taught how. Leaving those areas out of the definition shrinks data science back to model fitting.{{cite:cleveland2001}} (pp. 21–22)

The modelling division inherits the limits of the models. A regression fit on a file that was not explored can be a precise summary of an artifact. Donoho's ordering puts exploration and preparation before modelling for that reason. A predictive success on a held-out slice of the same file does not answer a causal question the file was not designed to answer. The enlargement of the field does not repeal the difference between association and a contrast under an intervention.{{cite:donoho2015}} (§8.1)

## History
John Tukey's 1962 paper "The Future of Data Analysis" is the usual intellectual start. Donoho's section on that paper records Tukey's statement that his central interest had become data analysis, and that he treated the activity as a science rather than as a branch of mathematics. Later appeals pushed on different walls. John Chambers argued for more attention to preparing and presenting data. Bill Cleveland, in 2001, published the six-part plan and the name "data science," with computing and multidisciplinary projects given shares that a classical theory department did not have. Leo Breiman's 2001 essay described a generative culture, aimed at how inputs produce responses, and a predictive culture, aimed at future responses. Around 2015, campus initiatives and new master's degrees made the name an administrative fact, often led from outside statistics departments, which is the tension Donoho's survey records. The survey itself was presented at the Tukey centennial workshop in September 2015 and published in 2017 as "50 Years of Data Science."{{cite:cleveland2001}} (pp. 21–22) {{cite:donoho2015}} (§3; §5; §8.1)

Statistics remains the parent discipline for the probability models, the [sampling distribution](sampling-distribution.qmd), and the [design of experiments](designed-experiment.qmd). Machine learning is the predictive culture Breiman located largely outside statistics departments. [Econometrics](econometrics.qmd), [biostatistics](biostatistics.qmd), and [epidemiology](epidemiology.qmd) are subject-matter fields that already did multidisciplinary data analysis under other names. Cleveland's argument for a separate name is organisational as much as mathematical: computing, data preparation, pedagogy, and the evaluation of tools have to be taught and staffed, or the modelling core has nothing reliable to model.{{cite:cleveland2001}} (pp. 21–22) {{cite:donoho2015}} (§8.1)
