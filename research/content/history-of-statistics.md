Probability and statistics took shape as a sequence of practical problems that acquired mathematical form. A game left unfinished had to be divided fairly. Astronomers had to combine discordant observations of the same quantity. A population had to be described by a few regularities. A small sample had to be judged without pretending that it was large. Later, a probability statement had to rest on stated axioms, and a calculation that could not be done by hand had to be done by repeating a random experiment inside a machine. The timeline collects the dated steps. The sections take those steps in the same order. Where a concept now has a treatment of its own, the sentence names it.

## Early probability {#early-probability}

Games of chance are older than any theorem about them. Dice and astragals appear in graves and in classical histories, and card play is recorded in European cities by the end of the fourteenth century. For a long time the roll of a die was a question put to a god, or a piece of bone too irregular to suggest equal faces. The mathematical step was to treat the faces as equally likely and to compute a fair share of a stake from that assumption.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 431)

The first extended writings of that kind in the West come from Italy. Cardano’s *Liber de Ludo Aleae* was printed in 1663, after his death. Galileo’s notes on dice already use the principle that the faces are equally likely.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 431) Those pages are the start of a calculus of chances, rather than a list of lucky throws.

In France the subject took a sharper form. Blaise Pascal (1623–1662) was asked by Antoine Gombauld, the chevalier de Méré, how to divide a stake when a game of several plays stops before either player has won. Pascal wrote to Pierre de Fermat (1601–1665). Fermat produced a solution, and Pascal found a recurrence that gives the same division.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 431–432) The problem is the division of an unfinished game. The answer is a probability: each player’s share is the chance that he would have won had the game gone on.

Christiaan Huygens, working in the Netherlands, published *De Ratiociniis in Ludo Aleae* in 1657. The tract values a position in a game by averaging the fortunes that the remaining chances can produce, and it carries the same questions into settings other than the gaming table. Jakob Bernoulli and Abraham de Moivre both took the tract as a starting point.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 431) The fair value of a chance is the idea that later becomes an [expectation](articles/expectation.qmd).

Jakob Bernoulli’s *Ars Conjectandi* appeared in 1713. The book is in four parts. The first reprints Huygens, with substantial changes. The second treats combinations. The third works examples from games of chance and a revised notion of expected value. The fourth, left unfinished, turns probability toward moral and economic questions and distinguishes an exact classical measure of probability from an approximate one. In that development Bernoulli recast the calculation of an expectation as a probability calculus and formulated the weak [law of large numbers](articles/law-of-large-numbers.qmd), the stability of a relative frequency when the number of trials grows.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 37–38) A single trial with two outcomes, success or failure, is a [Bernoulli trial](articles/bernoulli-distribution.qmd). The count of successes in a fixed number of independent trials is the [binomial distribution](articles/binomial-distribution.qmd).

Abraham de Moivre (1667–1754) studied in France, left after the revocation of the Edict of Nantes in 1685, and was elected to the Royal Society in 1697. *The Doctrine of Chances* (1718) treats questions that remain part of the elementary theory of probability. *Miscellanea Analytica* (1730) contains the density of the normal curve in a calculation of chances for a game.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 356) In 1733 he was the first to obtain that curve as an approximation to the binomial; an English version followed in 1738. He called the approximation a curve, and he found it while computing the probabilities of gain.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 379) That approximation is the classical [central limit theorem](articles/central-limit-theorem.qmd) in its first special case, and the curve is the [normal distribution](articles/normal-distribution.qmd).

## Bayes and Laplace {#bayes-and-laplace}

Thomas Bayes (1702–1761) was a Presbyterian minister at Tunbridge Wells and, from 1742, a Fellow of the Royal Society. He published no technical memoir in his lifetime. After his death Richard Price went through his papers and sent the principal one to the Royal Society. Price presented *An essay towards solving a problem in the doctrine of chances* on 23 December 1763. The essay then appeared in the *Philosophical Transactions*.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 30, 32)

The theorem that carries Bayes’s name answers a conditional question: among the reasons that might have produced an event, what is the probability of each reason once the event has been seen. It is a rule for reversing a conditional probability.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 30) In the form used now, a prior distribution for an unknown quantity is multiplied by the [likelihood](articles/likelihood-function.qmd) of the observations, and the product is the posterior, up to a constant. The unknown is treated as random because it is unknown, even when the quantity itself is a fixed number. With no special information the prior is chosen to add as little as possible.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 32) The same product is [Bayes’ rule](articles/bayes-rule.qmd).

Pierre-Simon Laplace was born in 1749 and died in Paris in 1827. His first publications are from the early 1770s. He entered the Academy of Sciences in 1785, directed the Bureau of Longitudes, served briefly as Minister of the Interior in 1799, and was made a count in 1806.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 295) The 1774 memoir on the probability of causes given the events is the early statement of inverse reasoning: an observation updates the probabilities of the causes that might have produced it.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 295) In the same year he obtained the normal distribution as an approximation to the hypergeometric. After de Moivre, he gave the binomial approximation a more formal and more general shape.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 379) The *Théorie analytique des probabilités* of 1812 gathers that work into a treatise.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 295)

Laplace’s generation also met the problem of combining observations. Roger Boscovich, around 1755–1757, fitted a line to measured meridian arcs by two conditions: the signed deviations sum to zero, and the sum of their absolute values is as small as possible. He solved the conditions geometrically. In 1789 Laplace took the same two conditions and replaced the geometry with an algebraic algorithm.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 451) Absolute deviations were a fitting principle before squared deviations became the standard one. The squared criterion is [least squares](articles/least-squares.qmd).

## Least squares and the normal law {#least-squares-and-the-normal-law}

The method of least squares minimises the sum of squared differences between observed values and the values given by a linear function of the unknowns. Adrien-Marie Legendre, born in Paris in 1752, published the method in 1805 in *Nouvelles méthodes pour la détermination des orbites des comètes*, in an appendix on the method of least squares.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 304, 306, 451) The published criterion is the one still used as [ordinary least squares](articles/ordinary-least-squares.qmd): choose the coefficients that make the residual sum of squares as small as possible. For a line, the fitted line passes through the point of averages, and the slope is the ratio of a sum of products of centered variables to a sum of squared centered predictors.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 306)

Carl Friedrich Gauss was born in Brunswick in 1777, took his doctorate in 1799, and from 1807 worked at Göttingen, where he died in 1855.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 216–217) In statistics his name is attached to estimation, to least squares, and to the use of the normal distribution for errors of measurement.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 217) His *Theoria motus corporum coelestium* (1809) and the 1816 paper on the accuracy of observations made normal-theory techniques into standard methods of the nineteenth century.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 217, 379) The 1821 part of *Theoria combinationis observationum erroribus minimis obnoxiae* contains his proof of the result later called the Gauss–Markov theorem: when the errors have mean zero and a common unknown variance, the least-squares estimator has the smallest variance among linear unbiased estimators. Markov published a rediscovery in 1900.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 217)

A dispute over priority followed the two publications. Gauss had been using least squares, on the later account, since 1795, and in 1809 he printed the method with references to that earlier work. Legendre’s 1805 appendix remains the first publication. Robert Adrian in America was fitting by least squares in the same years, without contact with either of them.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 451) The [normal distribution](articles/normal-distribution.qmd) entered this story from two directions at once. De Moivre had the curve in 1733 as a binomial approximation. Gauss studied it as the law of astronomical error, and the distribution came to be called by his name and by Laplace’s.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 356, 379) Galileo had already noticed that observational errors fall symmetrically and gather near the true value.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 379)

The eighteenth-century motive for all of this fitting was concrete. The motion of the Moon had to be represented mathematically. The inequality in the motions of Jupiter and Saturn had to be explained. The shape of the Earth had to be determined from meridian arcs. Least squares grew up as the way to turn those overdetermined measurements into a single estimate.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 451)

## Quetelet, Galton, and Pearson {#quetelet-galton-pearson}

Adolphe Quetelet was born in Ghent in 1796 and died in 1874.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 379, 442–443) Sent to Paris to prepare an observatory for Brussels, he met Poisson and Laplace, and Laplace pressed on him the importance of probabilistic calculation. In 1828 he published *Instructions populaires sur le calcul des probabilités* and became astronomer, then director, of the Brussels observatory. In 1846 his lessons to the princes Ernest and Albert appeared as letters on the theory of probability applied to the moral and political sciences.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 442–443) For the error curve he used the names “curve of possibilities” and “distribution of possibilities.”{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 379) A probability that had been a device for games and for the sky was being used to describe people.

Francis Galton’s seed experiments are the next change of subject. In 1875 he sorted peas into seven groups by weight, took ten seeds from each group, and asked seven friends to grow them, 490 seeds in all. Each group of offspring was spread in a normal curve. The curves were centred at different weights, with a common variability, and the centres lay nearer the overall mean than the parent weights had been. He called the pull “reversion.”{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 451) The memoir of 1886 dates the lecture in which he set those seed results out: 9 February 1877, at the Royal Institution. The offspring of large seeds were smaller than their parents, and the offspring of very small seeds were larger. The point they converged toward sat below the average size of the bag he had bought. He did not then fix a numerical ratio. The plantings, made for him from Nairn to Cornwall and carried through one, two, or three generations, were enough to convince him of the direction. They were not enough, in his own judgement, to pin the fraction down.{{cite:galton1886}} (pp. 246–247)

He wanted the same pattern in people, and he collected family records of stature without telling the families which comparison he intended to make. The analysis of those records confirmed the seed conclusion and supplied a number. Table I of the memoir arrays the adult children of 205 mid-parents. A mid-parent is a couple’s heights combined, with each woman’s height multiplied by 1.08 so that the two sexes sit on one scale.{{cite:galton1886}} (pp. 246–248) The law he states for stature is that the height-deviate of the offspring is, on the average, two-thirds of the height-deviate of the mid-parent. The level of mediocrity in the population he used is 68¼ inches without shoes. Reading the same table down the columns, the most probable mid-parent of a man deviates only one-third as much as the man does.{{cite:galton1886}} (pp. 252–254) From 1885 he used the word regression for this linear return, and *Natural Inheritance* (1889) is the book-length account.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 451) The pattern is the [regression effect](articles/regression-effect.qmd): an extreme parent is followed, on average, by a less extreme child. A line fitted to the pairs is a [least-squares line](articles/least-squares-line.qmd), and several predictors at once are [multiple linear regression](articles/multiple-linear-regression.qmd).

Correlation grew out of the same heredity work. Galton had been occupied with heredity since 1860. In an 1888 paper to the Royal Statistical Society, “Co-relations and their measurement, chiefly from anthropometric data,” he used the word correlation, still alternating with “co-relation,” and he spoke of a co-relation index. The paper also treats a negative association.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 116) Pearson wrote in 1920 that Galton had discovered correlation, and that *Natural Inheritance* had pushed Pearson, Weldon, and Edgeworth to study it. Weldon wanted the coefficient called the Galton function. Edgeworth’s name, correlation coefficient, is the one that stayed. Pearson systematised the algebra, including the case of three variables, and Yule at University College carried it to several variables at once. Spearman’s rank correlation followed in 1904.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 116) The ordinary product-moment measure is the [correlation](articles/correlation.qmd) coefficient. Galton’s own names for the error curve were “frequency of error distribution” and “distribution of deviations from a mean.”{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 379)

Karl Pearson was born in London in 1857 and died in 1936. After Cambridge he was appointed in 1885 to the chair of applied mathematics at University College London, and in 1911 to the chair of eugenics there. In 1901, with Galton’s help, he founded *Biometrika* and edited it until his death. In 1906 he had Gosset in the laboratory for a year, working on small samples. He retired in 1933. The department was then divided: eugenics went to Fisher, and statistics to Pearson’s son Egon.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 418–419) Among the papers of the 1890s is the 1896 memoir on regression, heredity, and panmixia.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 419) The comparison of observed and expected counts in a classification, scaled and summed, is the [Pearson statistic](articles/pearson-statistic.qmd).

## Gosset, Fisher, Neyman, and Pearson {#gosset-fisher-neyman-pearson}

William Sealy Gosset was born in Canterbury in 1876. He studied mathematics and chemistry at New College, Oxford, and in 1899 became a brewer at Guinness in Dublin. The brewery kept a research laboratory. Colleagues brought him mathematical questions about barley and hops, the samples were small, and the usual large-sample theory of errors did not fit them. He met Karl Pearson in July 1905, and in 1906–1907 spent a year in Pearson’s laboratory on the probable error of a mean. Guinness would not let him publish under his own name. He chose the pseudonym Student. He died in 1937. For years the table he had computed was used at the brewery and at Rothamsted, and little elsewhere.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 234)

In 1908 Student published the density of a ratio: the difference between a sample mean and the population mean, divided by the sample standard deviation, together with the first table of that distribution. Further tables, longer and finer, followed in 1925.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 526) Fisher wrote to him in 1912 with a geometric derivation and with the notion of degrees of freedom. In 1925 Fisher defined the ratio $t = Z\sqrt{v/X}$, with $X$ a chi-squared variable on $v$ degrees of freedom, independent of the numerator.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 526) That ratio is the [Student $t$ distribution](articles/student-t-distribution.qmd). A test of one mean that estimates the standard error from the sample is the [one-sample $t$ test](articles/one-sample-t-test.qmd).

Ronald Fisher was born in 1890 in East Finchley. He studied mathematics at Cambridge, and from 1919 to 1933 he worked at the Rothamsted experimental station. In 1933 he became professor of eugenics at University College London, the chair created by the split of Pearson’s department. From 1943 to 1957 he held the Balfour chair of genetics at Cambridge. He died in Adelaide in 1962.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 203, 419) The Rothamsted years produced the theory of [maximum likelihood](articles/maximum-likelihood.qmd), the [design of experiments](articles/designed-experiment.qmd) for agricultural trials, and the [analysis of variance](articles/anova-sum-of-squares-decomposition.qmd). Randomisation, randomised blocks, Latin squares, and factorial arrangements are his.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 203) The 1922 paper on the mathematical foundations of theoretical statistics and the 1925 *Statistical Methods for Research Workers* are the books and papers through which that theory travelled.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 203) The sharpness of a parametric model, the quantity that sets a bound on how well a parameter can be estimated, is [Fisher information](articles/fisher-information.qmd). A test that uses the exact permutation distribution of a table, rather than a chi-squared approximation, is [Fisher’s exact test](articles/fisher-s-exact-test.qmd).

Jerzy Neyman (1894–1981) studied physics and mathematics at Kharkov, worked as a statistician at the agricultural institute in Bydgoszcz, and in 1924 studied at University College under Karl Pearson. There he met Egon Pearson, Gosset, and Fisher. In 1937 he became professor at Berkeley and built the department of statistics there. With Egon Pearson he set out the basis of the theory of testing hypotheses. Their 1928 papers in *Biometrika* examine test criteria for inference, and the 1933 paper in the *Proceedings of the Cambridge Philosophical Society* treats the testing of hypotheses in relation to prior probabilities.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 372) The criterion that a test of a given size should maximise power against the alternative is the [Neyman–Pearson lemma](articles/neyman-pearson-lemma.qmd). The pieces of such a test — a null specification, a statistic, a reference distribution, and a rule — are the [ingredients of a hypothesis test](articles/ingredients-of-a-hypothesis-test.qmd). The tail probability under the null is a [p-value](articles/p-value.qmd). An interval procedure with a stated long-run coverage rate is a [confidence interval](articles/confidence-interval.qmd). For a simple hypothesis against a simple alternative, the [likelihood-ratio test](articles/likelihood-ratio-test.qmd) is the most powerful test of its size.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 310)

The three programmes sit next to one another and do different jobs. Gosset’s ratio is a reference distribution for a mean when the scale is estimated. Fisher’s likelihood is a principle for estimation and for the design of the experiment that produces the data. Neyman and Pearson’s lemma is a principle for choosing a test once the hypotheses and the error rates are stated. A later reader uses all three, often in one analysis: a designed experiment, a likelihood or a least-squares fit, and a test or an interval whose error rate can be stated.

## Kolmogorov’s axioms {#kolmogorov}

Andrey Kolmogorov was born in Tambov in 1903. He entered Moscow State University in 1920, published in 1925 on inequalities for partial sums of random variables, took his doctorate in 1929, and became professor in 1931. In 1933 he published the monograph on the foundations of probability.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 282) The book is *Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung*.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 283)

The monograph states probability as a measure on a family of events. Non-negativity, the probability of the whole space, and countable additivity are enough to carry the classical calculations and the limit theorems that Bernoulli, de Moivre, and Laplace had reached by special arguments. A [probability measure](articles/probability-measure.qmd) is that assignment. The [axioms of probability](articles/axioms-of-probability.qmd) are the short list the measure has to satisfy. The [Kolmogorov extension theorem](articles/kolmogorov-extension-theorem.qmd) is the later existence result that builds a process from consistent finite-dimensional distributions. The 1933 monograph is the point at which those ideas become the ordinary language of the subject, rather than a collection of separate theorems about games, errors, and frequencies.

The same year, 1933, saw a second Kolmogorov paper, on the empirical distribution function.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 283) The distance between an empirical distribution and a fully specified theoretical distribution is the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic, one of the goodness-of-fit comparisons that sit beside the chi-squared comparison Pearson had introduced. A probability calculus with axioms and a statistic that compares a sample with a named distribution are two faces of the same decade.

## The mid-twentieth century {#mid-century}

Abraham Wald, in 1941, extended the likelihood-ratio comparison beyond the simple case. Under regularity conditions the asymptotic distribution of the general statistic was then given by Wilks.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 310) A test that uses an estimate and its standard error, rather than a ratio of maximised likelihoods, is a [Wald test](articles/wald-test.qmd). Decision theory in Wald’s later book-length form is a further development of the same programme of stating an action, a loss, and a risk. The 1941 extension is the step that is fixed by the account used here.

Paired comparisons acquired a rank form that does not assume a normal difference. Frank Wilcoxon’s signed procedure of 1945 ranks the absolute paired differences and restores the signs. In 1949 he treated the pairs whose difference is zero.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 571) A procedure that keeps only the sign of each difference, and not its rank, is a [sign test](articles/sign-test.qmd). A two-sample comparison that pools the observations and ranks them is the [Wilcoxon rank-sum test](articles/wilcoxon-rank-sum-test.qmd). The signed procedure and the rank-sum procedure answer different designs. Both belong to the nonparametric turn of the 1940s, in which a test is derived from ranks or signs so that a normal error law is not required.

John Tukey was born in Bedford, Massachusetts, in 1915, took a Princeton doctorate in mathematics in 1939, and became professor of mathematics there at thirty-five. He helped form Princeton’s department of statistics in 1966 and chaired it until 1969. He died in New Jersey in 2000. *Exploratory Data Analysis* appeared in 1977.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 542) The approach itself is dated from 1970: look at the data before committing them to a probability model, the way a detective looks for clues. The clues are numerical and, very often, graphical. The box-and-whisker plot is from 1972. The stem-and-leaf diagram, close in spirit to a [histogram](articles/histogram.qmd), is from 1977.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 192) A compact numerical companion of the box is the [five-number summary](articles/five-number-summary-and-boxplot.qmd). The whole habit of plotting and tabulating before fitting is [exploratory data analysis](articles/exploratory-data-analysis.qmd).

Resampling answered a different difficulty: the standard error of a complicated statistic, when no formula is available and the shape of the population is not known. The [jackknife](articles/jackknife.qmd) omits observations systematically. The bootstrap replaces the unknown distribution by an estimate $\widehat F$ built from the sample, draws new samples from $\widehat F$, and recomputes the statistic. The name is the baron who lifted himself by his own bootstraps. The statistical method, in the form that spread, begins with Efron’s 1979 paper, which set off a large literature of theory and of applications. The calculations are computer calculations. Automatic repetition takes the place of an analytical standard-error formula, and it remains available when one will not assume a parametric shape for the data.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 51) The article on the [bootstrap](articles/bootstrap.qmd) is that method.

A random number used as a computing device is older than the bootstrap, and it has a different target. Any numerical method that solves a problem by random or pseudorandom numbers, because the problem has no usable closed form, is a Monte Carlo method. The name is the city of the casino. The method is credited to von Neumann, Ulam, and Metropolis, and it was developed around 1949. It became practical when computers could repeat the draw.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 359–360) Estimating an area by throwing points into a square and counting the hits is the textbook picture: the proportion of hits, times the area of the square, estimates the area of the region.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 360) That device is the [Monte Carlo method](articles/monte-carlo-method.qmd). Drawing from a probability distribution that is known only up to a constant, by proposing a move and accepting or rejecting it, is the [Metropolis–Hastings algorithm](articles/metropolis-hastings-algorithm.qmd). Updating each coordinate from its conditional distribution is [Gibbs sampling](articles/gibbs-sampling.qmd). Both are Monte Carlo methods aimed at a posterior or at another distribution that cannot be written in closed form.

## Computation and machine learning {#computation-and-learning}

Once a draw could be repeated thousands of times, several older ideas changed their role. Least squares, likelihood, and the normal theory of errors remained the language of a designed experiment and of a linear model. Alongside them, a standard error could be computed by resampling, a posterior could be computed by a Markov chain, and a data set could be examined graphically before any model was chosen. Nonlinear regression is a clear case of the delay. The models go back to Fisher and Mackenzie in the 1920s, and their routine use waited until automatic calculation was ordinary, in the 1970s.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 372)

The same machines changed what could be asked of a fit. A statistician could hold back part of the sample and see whether a rule fitted on the rest still predicted, which is [cross-validation](articles/cross-validation.qmd). A regression with many coefficients could be asked to set some of them to zero, which is the [lasso](articles/lasso.qmd). A probability of a yes-or-no outcome could be linked to predictors through a log-odds, which is [binary logistic regression](articles/binary-logistic-regression.qmd). A cloud of variables could be summarised by the directions of greatest scatter, which are [principal component directions](articles/principal-component-directions.qmd). A classifier could cut the space with a sequence of questions, which is a [decision tree](articles/decision-tree.qmd), or it could place a linear boundary so that the gap between two separable classes is as wide as possible, which is a [hard-margin support vector machine](articles/hard-margin-support-vector-machine.qmd). A weighted sum passed through a nonlinear function is an [artificial neuron](articles/artificial-neuron.qmd), and a stack of such units is the start of a network.

Those procedures inherit the earlier centuries more than a list of names suggests. A logistic regression is still a likelihood. A support-vector boundary is still a geometric fit, cousin to the line Legendre and Gauss computed from squared errors. A tree and a neuron are still functions from predictors to a response, which is the relation Galton wanted between parent and child and which regression analysis defines as estimating one variable from others.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 451) Cross-validation and the bootstrap are both answers to the question Gosset met in the brewery: the sample in hand is not the population, and a method is needed for the uncertainty that remains. The axioms Kolmogorov wrote down are what make a Monte Carlo average a probability statement rather than a pile of random numbers.

The chronology is one subject seen at several scales. Huygens prices a chance. Bernoulli watches a frequency settle. De Moivre, Laplace, and Gauss obtain a curve for a sum and for an error. Legendre prints a fitting criterion. Quetelet and Galton turn the curve and the fit toward people, and Pearson, Gosset, Fisher, Neyman, and Egon Pearson build the inference that a sample can support. Kolmogorov writes the axioms. Wald, Wilcoxon, Tukey, and Efron, and the Monte Carlo of 1949, supply the mid-century tools for decisions, ranks, pictures, and resampling. The computational methods that followed are those tools used on larger collections of variables, with the same demand that a fit be estimable, checkable, and honest about the sample that produced it.
