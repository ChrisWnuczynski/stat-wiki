## Lead context
The log link makes a positive response mean depend multiplicatively on predictors while keeping every fitted mean above zero.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (p. 544, link and linear predictor)

## Motivating example
A clinic records infection counts for wards observed for unequal lengths of time. Four infections in two ward-years and four in eight ward-years are equal counts but observed rates of $2$ and $0.5$ per ward-year. A count regression that treats those observations as having the same exposure gives a misleading comparison. With a log link and an offset for log exposure, the fitted mean count is the exposure times a positive fitted rate. Differences in log rate then become ratios of rates, the form in which a ward comparison is often meaningful.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (p. 544, regression and link formulation)

## Intuition
A linear predictor can be any real number, including a large negative one, whereas an expected count or positive reaction time cannot be negative. Exponentiating the predictor solves the range mismatch: an additive step on the log scale becomes a multiplication on the mean scale. That is why a log-link coefficient describes a *ratio*, even when the model is fitted through an ordinary linear combination of covariates. It is also why changing the units of a covariate changes the numerical size of its coefficient without changing its fitted predictions.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (p. 544, linear predictor and link)

## Definition
For a conditional mean $\mu_i=\mathbb E(Y_i\mid\mathbf x_i)>0$, the log link is $g(\mu_i)=\log\mu_i=\eta_i$, with inverse $g^{-1}(\eta_i)=e^{\eta_i}$. In a regression, $\eta_i=\beta_0+\sum_j\beta_jx_{ij}$, perhaps plus a specified offset. The response distribution is a separate part of the model: a Poisson count, a Gamma positive continuous response, or a quasi-likelihood can share the same log link while making different variance and likelihood assumptions. In R, `poisson()` defaults to `link = "log"`; `Gamma(link = "log")` is available, while bare `Gamma()` defaults to the inverse link.{{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Sec. Usage; Arguments, Poisson and Gamma links; Value, linkfun and linkinv){{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (pp. 543–544, components of a GLM)

## Properties
For a one-unit change in $x_j$ with the other predictors and any offset fixed, $\mu(x_j+1)/\mu(x_j)=e^{\beta_j}$. The exact percentage change is $100(e^{\beta_j}-1)\%$, which differs from the approximation $100\beta_j\%$ when the coefficient is not close to zero. A two-unit change multiplies the mean by $e^{2\beta_j}$. The examples below show both a doubling coefficient and how a small per-kilometre slope accumulates over a hundred kilometres.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (p. 544, link applied to linear predictor)

Because $d\mu/d\eta=e^\eta=\mu$, the same absolute change in the predictor causes a larger absolute change in the mean where the fitted mean is already large. The relative rate of change, $(1/\mu)d\mu/dx_j=\beta_j$, is constant in the simple additive predictor. This describes the conditional mean, not the variance: for a Poisson GLM $\operatorname{Var}(Y_i\mid\mathbf x_i)=\mu_i$, whereas for a Gamma GLM with dispersion $\phi$ it is $\phi\mu_i^2$. The link does not by itself choose between those relationships.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (pp. 543–544, distribution, link and predictor as distinct components)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
If $\eta(x_j)=a+\beta_jx_j$ for everything held fixed in $a$, then $\mu(x_j+\Delta)=\exp\{a+\beta_jx_j+\beta_j\Delta\}=\mu(x_j)e^{\beta_j\Delta}$. Setting $\Delta=1$ proves the coefficient ratio; subtracting one and multiplying by $100$ gives the percentage change. Differentiation gives $d\mu/dx_j=\beta_j\mu$, hence $(1/\mu)d\mu/dx_j=\beta_j$.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (p. 544, link and linear predictor)

If exposure $t_i>0$ enters as an offset, $\eta_i=\log t_i+\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta$, so $\mu_i=t_ie^{\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta}$ and $\mu_i/t_i=e^{\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta}$. Finally, a Poisson log likelihood for one count is $\ell(\mu;y)=y\log\mu-\mu-\log(y!)$. With $\eta=\log\mu$, this becomes $y\eta-e^\eta-\log(y!)$, so $\eta$ is its natural parameter and the log link is canonical for the Poisson. This calculation does not make it canonical for the Gamma, whose usual inverse canonical link differs.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (pp. 543–544, exponential-family and link formulation)

:::

## Statistical use
For counts, specify the count distribution and whether the observed units have equal exposure. If not, include $\log t_i$ as an offset with coefficient fixed at one. This gives a rate comparison while retaining a count response and its appropriate likelihood. A model of raw rates with the same weights is not automatically equivalent. If the count variance substantially exceeds its mean, changing the link does not repair the Poisson variance assumption; a quasi-Poisson or negative-binomial model may be needed.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§§1.3, 3.3–3.4, pp. 372, 378–380)

For positive continuous measurements, a Gamma response with log link models $\mathbb E(Y_i\mid x_i)$ multiplicatively and allows variance proportional to its square. It is different from taking $\log Y_i$ and fitting a normal linear model: the latter describes the mean of the transformed response, and retransformation generally requires an adjustment. The clotting-time comparison below puts the log link beside inverse and inverse-square links; choosing among them changes both the predictor scale and what straight-line effects mean. R's default Gamma inverse link is a software default, not a rule that every Gamma analysis must use it.{{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Sec. Usage; Arguments, Gamma links and variance)

## Worked example
Suppose a Poisson model for event counts has log expected count $\log\mu=\log t+\log 2+(\log 1.5)x$, where $t$ is years of observation and $x$ indicates the exposed group. At $t=5$ and $x=0$, the fitted rate is $2$ events per year and the fitted count is $\mu=5\times2=10$. At $t=10$ and $x=1$, the rate is $2\times1.5=3$ per year and the fitted count is $\mu=10\times3=30$. The second expected count is three times the first because exposure doubled *and* the rate multiplied by $1.5$; the group coefficient alone describes a $50\%$ higher fitted rate. If both groups were observed for five years, their expected counts would be $10$ and $15$, showing the rate ratio directly.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (p. 544, GLM predictor and link)

**Your turn.** Let $\log\mu=\log t+\log4+(\log1.25)x$. Find the fitted rate and expected count for $x=2$, $t=3$. What happens to the rate if $x$ increases to $3$ at the same exposure?{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (p. 544, link and predictor)

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The fitted rate is $4(1.25)^2=6.25$ per exposure unit, so $\mu=3\times6.25=18.75$. At $x=3$ the rate is $4(1.25)^3=7.8125$ and the expected count is $3\times7.8125=23.4375$. The rate ratio is $7.8125/6.25=1.25$: each one-unit increase in $x$ raises the mean rate by $25\%$ when the other terms are fixed.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (p. 544, link and predictor)
:::

## Common misconceptions
The log link models $\log\mathbb E(Y\mid x)$, not $\mathbb E(\log Y\mid x)$. For a positive variable equally likely to be $1$ or $9$, the first is $\log5\approx1.609$, while the second is $\log3\approx1.099$. A coefficient of $0.6$ on $\log A$ is an elasticity, not a $60\%$ increase for one extra unit of $A$; the lake-area question makes that distinction explicit. A fitted mean of $4.48$ is an expectation for a count, not a prediction that a fractional event will be observed. Finally, positivity of the mean does not prove a Gamma or Poisson distribution fits the response.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (pp. 543–544, separate model components)

## Assumptions and limits
The link requires $\mu>0$; a structural mean of exactly zero cannot be represented by a finite log predictor. In a count study with ten times as much exposure in one group, omitting the offset can create an apparent group effect even when rates are identical. In a Poisson model with fitted mean $2$ but observed variance near $10$ across comparable units, the log link's positive mean does nothing to correct underestimated sampling variation. An additive log-scale effect also imposes a constant rate ratio; if a treatment's rate ratio changes sharply with age, include an interaction or another flexible term rather than interpret one coefficient as universal. Extreme positive predictors can overflow a naive numerical exponential, so computation also needs stable implementation.{{cite:nelder_wedderburn1972}} (§§1.3, 3.3–3.4, pp. 372, 378–380)

## History
Nelder and Wedderburn's 1972 generalized-linear-model formulation brought a distributional family, a linear predictor and a link into one framework. McCullagh's introduction to that paper emphasizes the transformation between the expected response and the linear predictor and lists the exponential family and link as separate ingredients. This history explains why the logarithmic transformation belongs to the *mean* side of the model. The R family documentation reflects the later software distinction: log is the Poisson default and an available Gamma option, whose default is inverse.{{cite:mccullagh1992_glm_intro}} (pp. 543–544){{cite:rstats_family2026}} (Sec. Usage; Arguments)
