## Lead context

Hundreds of thousands of students take a first statistics course, and many of them will never compute a variance by hand again. Statistics education is the work of deciding what those courses are for and how they are taught. The current college guidelines treat the course as training in an investigative process, not as a catalogue of formulas. They also record that the audience is not one audience. Future consumers, future analysts, engineers, life-science students, and mathematics majors do not need the same semester. In autumn 2010, about 508,000 students in United States two-year and four-year colleges were enrolled in an introductory statistics course, a rise of about a third from 2005, and advanced-placement candidacy had grown sharply beside them.{{cite:gaise2016}} (pp. 3–4)

## Definition — Scope

Statistics education is the design and teaching of statistical thinking, from school through the first university courses and beyond. The 2016 college report, revising guidelines the American Statistical Association endorsed in 2005, states six recommendations. Teach statistical thinking, as an investigative process of problem-solving and decision-making, and give students experience with several variables at once. Focus on conceptual understanding. Integrate real data that have a context and a purpose. Foster active learning. Use technology to explore concepts and to analyse data. Use assessment to improve and evaluate learning. The desired result is a statistically educated student, which means a student who can think statistically. Mastering a fixed list of techniques is not the goal the report sets in place of that ability.{{cite:gaise2016}} (pp. 3, 8)

The goals that spell this out ask a student to judge whether a result reported in public follows from the study that produced it, to recognise a question for which investigation would help, to produce and read graphical and numerical summaries, to explain the role of variability and of randomness, to meet statistical models including models with several variables, to use the basic ideas of tests and intervals, to read standard software output, and to notice ethical issues in practice. Identifying the variables, the units, and the population to which a result can be generalised is part of the first of those goals.{{cite:gaise2016}} (p. 8)

The scope covers introductory courses and, the authors argue, later undergraduate courses in practice, computing, and theory. It distinguishes literacy courses for consumers from methods courses for producers. Prerequisites differ. Most courses ask for high-school algebra, and some ask for calculus. Class size runs from a dozen students to lectures of hundreds. A single syllabus does not fit that range, which is why the recommendations are stated as aims rather than as a table of contents.{{cite:gaise2016}} (p. 7)

## Properties

What is taught, when a course follows the guidelines, is the statistical process itself. Students should recognise a question for which investigation would help, produce and read graphical and numerical summaries, and judge whether a reported result follows from the study that was done. A first course organised on those lines starts from data and the way they were collected, then summaries of shape and centre, then probability models such as the normal law, and then inference and regression. Technology is part of the method. Multivariable thinking is emphasised because the questions students meet are rarely one-predictor questions. Topics that crowd out that process are candidates for omission. Omitting a topic from a first syllabus is a curricular decision about time. It is not a statement that the omitted topic is false.{{cite:gaise2016}} (pp. 3, 8){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.3; Ch. 2, §2.1.3; Ch. 4, §4.1.4)

Sampling is one place the process becomes concrete. A random draw is used to reduce the chance that the collector's interests decide who is included. Even then, a high non-response rate can leave the returned answers describing respondents rather than the population that was named. A histogram is another. Bars that trail off to the right are a long right tail, and naming that shape is part of reading a summary rather than part of memorising a formula for skewness. A normal model for a score, with a stated mean and standard deviation, turns a cutoff into a tail probability only after the student has drawn the region and standardised the cutoff. A straight line fitted to two measurements leaves residuals, and a curve in those residuals is evidence that the line was the wrong shape. Each of these is a judgement the guidelines want a student to be able to make, not a trick of arithmetic.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.3; Ch. 2, §2.1.3; Ch. 4, §4.1.4; Ch. 8, §8.1){{cite:gaise2016}} (p. 8)

Assessment, in this framework, is evidence about whether students can carry out that process, not only about whether they can compute a test statistic from a formula sheet. The report's authors say they tried to keep the goals realistic while still asking for more than a drill. Incremental alignment with the six recommendations is available in a small class and in a large one. The constraints on instructors, of time, of preparation, and of class size, are part of the account, not an afterthought.{{cite:gaise2016}} (pp. 3, 7)

## History

The 2005 college report had six recommendations that the 2016 committee kept, with simpler wording and a change of order so that what to teach precedes how to teach it. Two emphases were added to the first recommendation: statistics as investigation, and multivariable thinking. The revision was a response to a decade in which introductory enrolments reached about 508,000 in autumn 2010, advanced-placement candidacy grew by more than 150 percent from 2005 to 2015, and data science became a neighbouring discipline described as computational thinking joined to inferential thinking. A pre-college companion report influenced school standards, including standards on interpreting data and on making inferences. Appendix material in the 2016 report sketches that longer development of introductory courses and of the resources used to teach them.{{cite:gaise2016}} (pp. 3–4)

Textbooks written for the same introductory audience are the other half of the history that a student actually meets. One widely used text makes the investigative order concrete in its early chapters: how a sample is drawn and how non-response can still bias it, how a histogram shows a long tail, how a normal model turns a score into a tail area, and how a residual plot shows that a line has left a pattern behind. Understanding those weaknesses is part of using the tools. The guidelines and that kind of book are aimed at the same first course, one from the side of recommendations and one from the side of exercises.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.3; Ch. 2, §2.1.3; Ch. 4, §4.1.3; Ch. 8, §8.1){{cite:gaise2016}} (pp. 3–4)

## Connections

Statistics education is the producer of [statistical literacy](statistical-literacy.qmd) and of the next generation of analysts. It is where students meet [sampling bias](sampling-bias.qmd), [confounding variables](confounding-variable.qmd), and the difference between a [p-value](p-value.qmd) and a practical effect, if the course follows its own goals. A course that drills procedures and never asks whether a published claim follows from its design has missed the first goal the guidelines set. A course that never touches data has missed the recommendation to integrate real data with a context and a purpose. The report's claim is that a closer alignment with the six recommendations can be made step by step, in a seminar and in a lecture of hundreds.{{cite:gaise2016}} (pp. 3, 7–8){{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 1, §1.3.3)
