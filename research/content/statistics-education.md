

## Lead context

Hundreds of thousands of students take a first statistics course, and many of them will never compute a variance by hand again. Statistics education is the work of deciding what those courses are for and how they are taught. The current college guidelines treat the course as training in an investigative process, not as a catalogue of formulas. They also record that the audience is not one audience: future consumers, future analysts, engineers, life-science students, and mathematics majors do not need the same semester.

## Definition

Statistics education is the design and teaching of statistical thinking, from school through the first university courses and beyond. The 2016 college report, revising guidelines the American Statistical Association endorsed in 2005, states six recommendations. Teach statistical thinking, as an investigative process of problem-solving and decision-making, and give students experience with several variables at once. Focus on conceptual understanding. Integrate real data that have a context and a purpose. Foster active learning. Use technology to explore concepts and to analyse data. Use assessment to improve and evaluate learning. The report's desired result is a statistically educated student, which means a student who can think statistically, not a student who has mastered a fixed list of techniques.{{cite:gaise2016}} (pp. 3, 6, 8)

The scope covers introductory courses and, the authors argue, later undergraduate courses in practice, computing, and theory. It distinguishes literacy courses for consumers from methods courses for producers. Prerequisites differ: most courses ask for high-school algebra, and some ask for calculus. Class size runs from a dozen students to lectures of hundreds. A single syllabus does not fit that range, which is why the recommendations are stated as aims rather than as a table of contents.{{cite:gaise2016}} (p. 7)

What is taught is the statistical process itself. Students should recognise a question for which investigation would help, produce and read graphical and numerical summaries, and judge whether a reported result follows from the study that was done. A first course organised on those lines starts with data structures and collection, then summaries, probability, and inference, and it warns that the tools are imperfect and the data are messy. Technology is part of the method, not a demonstration after the arithmetic. Multivariable thinking is emphasised because the questions students meet are rarely one-predictor questions. Topics that crowd out that process are candidates for omission, which is a curricular decision the guidelines discuss rather than a claim that the omitted topics are false.{{cite:gaise2016}} (pp. 3, 8){{cite:openintro2019}} (Preface)

Assessment, in this framework, is evidence about whether students can carry out that process, not only about whether they can compute a test statistic from a formula sheet.{{cite:gaise2016}} (p. 3)

## History

The 2005 college report had six recommendations that the 2016 committee kept, with simpler wording and a change of order so that what to teach precedes how to teach it. Two emphases were added to the first recommendation: statistics as investigation, and multivariable thinking. The revision was a response to a decade in which introductory enrolments in United States colleges reached about 508,000 in autumn 2010, advanced-placement candidacy grew sharply, and data science became a neighbouring discipline. A pre-college companion report influenced school standards. Appendix material in the 2016 report sketches that longer development of introductory courses and of the resources used to teach them.{{cite:gaise2016}} (pp. 3–4)

Textbooks written for the same introductory audience, and used in schools as well as in universities, are the other half of the history. They make the investigative order concrete: data, summaries, probability, inference, and an explicit statement that understanding weaknesses is part of using the tools.{{cite:openintro2019}} (Preface)



A numerical boundary keeps the definition attached to a calculation. List the integers from 1 through 15. Their total is 120, so their mean is 8.0. The first three integers have mean 2. The difference between the full-list mean and that three-integer mean is 6.0. The difference is a property of which units entered the average. It is not a property of the name of the field. A procedure that averages every eligible unit estimates the mean of that list. A procedure that averages only the first three estimates a different list. Statistics education has to say which list is the target before the average is interpreted.

The same arithmetic limits a model fitted to the observed rows. The mean 8.0 describes the integers that were listed. Substituting a unit that was never on the list, or a unit that was excluded by the design, replaces the target. A [confidence interval](confidence-interval.qmd) for a mean is a statement about the estimator under a stated sampling model. A [standard error](standard-error.qmd) is the standard deviation of that estimator. Neither object repairs a list that was the wrong list. The procedures already named here include [statistical literacy](statistical-literacy.qmd), [sampling bias](sampling-bias.qmd), [confounding variables](confounding-variable.qmd), [p-value](p-value.qmd).

Two further limits are algebraic. The sum of deviations from the mean 8.0 is zero, so the mean is the least-squares centre of these 15 numbers. A robust centre, or a median, answers a different minimisation. Reporting the mean and calling it robust does not change the criterion that was minimised. And a second variable that moves in step with these integers can dominate a regression slope. The slope then describes the joint list, which is a larger object than the one-column mean 8.0.

## Connections

Statistics education is the producer of [statistical literacy](statistical-literacy.qmd) and of the next generation of analysts. It is where students meet [sampling bias](sampling-bias.qmd), [confounding variables](confounding-variable.qmd), and the difference between a [p-value](p-value.qmd) and a practical effect, if the course follows its own goals. A course that drills procedures and never asks whether a published claim follows from its design has missed the first goal the guidelines set. A course that never touches data has missed the third recommendation. The constraints on instructors are real. The report's claim is that incremental alignment with the six recommendations is available in a small class and in a large one.{{cite:gaise2016}} (pp. 3, 7–8){{cite:openintro2019}} (Preface)
