## Motivating example
A daily balance changes by an average gain plus an unpredictable shock. Even when the shocks have no trend, their cumulative sum produces persistent movements in the balance. A random walk with drift separates the expected accumulated change from this growing uncertainty. A steady positive average does not mean that every realized step, or every finite-horizon change, is positive.{{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§5.2, Drift method; §9.1, random walk models)

## Definition — Independent increments and a deterministic start
Let $S_n=s_0+\sum_{i=1}^nY_i$, where the increments are iid with mean $\mu\ne0$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$. Equivalently, write $S_n=S_{n-1}+\mu+\varepsilon_n$, with $\E\varepsilon_n=0$. The coefficient of $S_{n-1}$ is one. A coefficient larger than one instead creates an explosive autoregression, while an intercept in a stationary autoregression produces a long-run mean rather than a linearly accumulating drift.{{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§§9.1, 9.3, autoregressive models)

## Properties — Drift, fluctuations and correlation
Linearity and independence give $\E(S_n)=s_0+n\mu$ and $\operatorname{Var}(S_n)=n\sigma^2$. For $m\le n$, the two sums share their first $m$ increments, giving $\operatorname{Cov}(S_m,S_n)=m\sigma^2$ and correlation $\sqrt{m/n}$ when $m>0,\sigma^2>0$. The process is therefore not covariance-stationary: its variance changes with time. The first difference $S_n-S_{n-1}=Y_n$ is stationary under the iid assumption.{{cite:grinstead_snell1997}} (§§6.2, 7.1, variances and sums){{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§9.1, differencing)

The weak or strong law gives $(S_n-s_0)/n\to\mu$ in the corresponding mode, while the central limit theorem gives
$$\frac{S_n-s_0-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}\Rightarrow N(0,1)\quad(\sigma^2>0).$$
Thus drift is order $n$ and standard deviation order $\sqrt n$. Their ratio $|\mu|\sqrt n/\sigma$ grows, but an event about crossing a barrier at any time depends on the whole path, rather than on a one-time normal approximation. For positive drift, the strong law does imply $S_n\to+\infty$ almost surely under integrable iid increments; it does not imply monotonicity.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§§2.4, 3.4.1)

## Worked example — Forecasting with known drift
Let $s_0=100$, $\mu=0.5$ and $\sigma=2$. After sixteen steps, the mean is $108$, variance is $64$ and standard deviation is eight. If increments are normal, the endpoint distribution is exactly $N(108,64)$ and $\mathbb{P}(S_{16}>100)=\Phi(1)\approx0.841345$. For other iid finite-variance increments this is a normal approximation whose quality depends on their distribution and the horizon.

Given observations through time $T$, a known-drift forecast is $S_T+h\mu$. Its error is the sum of $h$ independent future centered increments and has variance $h\sigma^2$. For $h=9$ and $S_T=120$, the forecast is $124.5$ with standard deviation six. These are forecast uncertainties for a future level, rather than standard errors for estimating the drift.{{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§5.5, prediction intervals; §9.1, random walk)

**Your turn.** For $s_0=0$, drift $-0.2$ and increment variance $0.64$, find the mean and standard deviation at step $25$.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The mean is $25(-0.2)=-5$. The variance is $25(0.64)=16$, so the standard deviation is four.
:::

## Statistical use — Estimating a drift from observed differences
With $T$ observed levels, the average of the $T-1$ increments is $\widehat\mu=(S_T-S_1)/(T-1)$. It gives the drift forecast $S_T+h\widehat\mu$. If the increment variance is known and future increments are independent of the observed ones, the unconditional forecast-error variance is
$$h\sigma^2+\frac{h^2\sigma^2}{T-1}.$$
The first term is future shock uncertainty and the second is drift estimation uncertainty. The cross term is zero by independence. Treating the fitted drift as known drops the second term, especially consequential at long horizons.{{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§§5.2, 5.5, drift forecasts and forecast distributions)

Seasonality, changing drift and dependent shocks require a different model. Differencing can expose their structure, but it does not prove the increments are independent. A fitted positive trend is also not a guarantee of profit or safety; these are probability models whose assumptions need to match the measurement process.{{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§9.1){{cite:durrett2019}} (§2.4, assumptions of laws of large numbers)

## Interpretation — Shared shocks and a two-term forecast error
Take $s_0=100$, $\mu=0.5$ and $\sigma^2=4$, the worked increment law. The position at step $4$ and the position at step $16$ share the first four increments, so their covariance is $4\times4=16$. Their variances are $16$ and $64$, and their correlation is $\sqrt{4/16}=1/2$. The later position is not a fresh draw around the drifted mean. Half of its correlation with the early position is the arithmetic of shared shocks, and the other ingredient is the extra twelve increments that the early position does not contain. A scatterplot of many simulated paths at those two times slopes upward for that reason. Differencing removes the shared past: $S_{16}-S_4$ is the sum of twelve new increments, with mean $6$ and variance $48$, and it is independent of $S_4$ when the increments are independent.{{cite:grinstead_snell1997}} (§§6.2, 7.1, variances and sums){{cite:durrett2019}} (§§2.4, 3.4.1)

Estimating the drift changes the forecast error. Suppose $17$ levels have been observed, so there are $16$ increments, and the next $9$ steps are to be forecast. With $\sigma^2=4$ known, the future-shock variance is $9\times4=36$ and the drift-estimation variance is $81\times4/16=20.25$. The sum is $56.25$, and the standard deviation is $7.5$. The known-drift calculation in the worked example has standard deviation $6$ because it keeps only the future shocks. The ratio $7.5/6=1.25$ is the inflation from treating $\widehat\mu$ as random. At a horizon of one step the estimation term is $\sigma^2/16$, small beside $\sigma^2$. At a horizon comparable to the length of the sample the two terms are the same order. A prediction interval that uses only $h\sigma^2$ is the interval for a known drift. Publishing it after $\widehat\mu$ was computed from the same series understates the error the forecast rule actually has.{{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§§5.2, 5.5, drift forecasts and forecast distributions){{cite:durrett2019}} (§2.4, assumptions of laws of large numbers)

## Common misconceptions
A positive drift fixes the slope of the expected path. It does not fix the sign of every increment. With $\mu=0.5$ and $\sigma=2$, a normal increment is negative with probability $\Phi(-0.5/2)=\Phi(-0.25)\approx0.401294$. Over sixteen steps the endpoint is below the start with probability $1-\Phi(1)\approx0.158655$, the complement of the worked probability $0.841345$. The path can spend long stretches below its mean and still be a walk with positive drift. A chart that rises overall is one sample path. The model also assigns probability to charts that fall.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§§2.4, 3.4.1){{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§5.2, Drift method; §9.1, random walk models)

The random-walk coefficient on the lagged level is one. An autoregression $S_n=8+0.9S_{n-1}+\varepsilon_n$ has a different structure: repeated substitution produces a long-run mean $8/(1-0.9)=80$ and shocks that are discounted by powers of $0.9$. Its variance settles if the noise variance is finite. Replacing $0.9$ by $1$ removes the discount, removes the long-run mean, and makes the variance grow like $n\sigma^2$. Estimating $0.9$ when the series is short can make a random walk look like a stationary autoregression, or the reverse. The distinction is the coefficient of the lagged level, together with the behaviour of the variance, not the presence of an intercept. An intercept beside a unit coefficient is the drift. An intercept beside a coefficient inside the unit interval is a shift of the long-run mean.{{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§§9.1, 9.3, autoregressive models)

## Assumptions and limits — What the one-step normal approximation leaves out
The central limit theorem calibrates $S_n$ at one fixed time. A question about the maximum of the path, the first time the path crosses a barrier, or the chance of ruin at any time before $n$, depends on the whole sequence. Those probabilities are not the probability that the single normal approximation at time $n$ lies above the barrier. For the worked law, $\mathbb{P}(S_{16}>100)\approx0.841345$ is an endpoint probability. The probability that the path has been above $100$ at every step, or at some step, is a different number and needs a path calculation. Finite variance is enough for the endpoint approximation to improve as $n$ grows. It is not a substitute for the path distribution.{{cite:durrett2019}} (§3.4.1, iid central limit theorem and its hypotheses){{cite:hyndman_athanasopoulos2021}} (§5.5, prediction intervals; §9.1, random walk)
