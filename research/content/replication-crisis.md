## Lead context
The replication crisis is the recognition that many published findings do not reappear when a study is repeated with new data.

A finding here means a relationship that investigators claim on the basis of formal statistical significance, typically a \(p\)-value below \(0.05\). The crisis is about the probability that a significant result is true, and about what happens when those results are actually repeated. The probability depends on how science is organised: the size of studies, the size of effects, the number of relationships tested, and the freedom to choose an analysis after seeing the data.{{cite:ioannidis2005}} (p. 0696)

## Definition
Ioannidis defines a research finding as any relationship that reaches formal statistical significance: an effective intervention, an informative predictor, a risk factor, an association. A negative finding can be useful, and the argument is aimed at the positive claims. The probability that such a claim is true depends on the prior probability that the relationship is true, on the power of the study, and on the level of statistical significance. In a field where \(R\) is the ratio of true relationships to relationships that are not true, among those tested, and where studies have power \(1-\beta\) and Type I error \(\alpha\), the positive predictive value is
\[
\mathrm{PPV}=\frac{(1-\beta)R}{R-\beta R+\alpha}.
\]
A finding is more likely false than true when this value falls below one half. The formula is a model of a field, not a measurement of one paper. It says how the field's habits move the probability.{{cite:ioannidis2005}} (p. 0696)

The conditions that push the value down are concrete. Studies are smaller. Effects are smaller. More relationships are tested, and they are less selected in advance. Designs, definitions, outcomes, and analyses are more flexible. Financial and other interests are stronger. More teams chase significance in the same field. Each of these increases the chance that a significant result is a false positive or an inflated true positive. Simulations in the paper are offered to show that, for most designs and settings considered, a research claim is more likely false than true.{{cite:ioannidis2005}} (p. 0696)

Replication is the empirical check on that model. A direct replication recreates the conditions believed sufficient for the original finding and collects new data. It can fail because the original result was a false positive, because the replication was a false negative, or because a difference between the studies, known or unknown, changes the effect. The Open Science Collaboration repeated \(100\) experimental and correlational studies from three psychology journals. That project is the clearest numerical portrait of the crisis in one field. It is not a portrait of every field, and the paper does not claim it is.{{cite:osc2015}} (Introduction and Results){{cite:ioannidis2005}} (p. 0696)

## Properties
The psychology replications were powered for the original effects. The average power for the \(97\) originally significant effects was \(0.92\), and the median was \(0.95\). If every original effect had been true and accurately estimated, about \(89\) replications would have been positive. The number observed was \(35\), or \(36.1\%\), with a \(95\%\) interval from \(26.6\%\) to \(46.2\%\). A McNemar test of the change gives \(\chi^2(1)=59.1\). The vote counts a replication as successful when it is significant at \(0.05\) in the same direction. By that vote, most of these findings did not reappear.{{cite:osc2015}} (Results)

Effect sizes tell the same story without the threshold. Original effects had mean \(0.403\) and standard deviation \(0.188\). Replication effects had mean \(0.197\) and standard deviation \(0.257\). The replication mean is about half the original mean. Wilcoxon's \(W=7137\) compares the pairs. Of \(99\) studies with both effect sizes, \(82\) were larger in the original, which is \(82.8\%\). The original and replication effect sizes were positively correlated, Spearman's \(r=0.51\). Larger originals tended to produce larger replications, and the magnitude was not preserved.{{cite:osc2015}} (Results)

The \(p\)-value threshold is a property of the summary, and it is a blunt one. A replication at \(p=0.06\) in the same direction is a failure under the vote and may be close to the original effect. The project therefore reports several indicators, not one. About \(47\%\) of original effect sizes fell inside the \(95\%\) confidence interval of the replication effect. Subjective ratings by the replication teams, asked whether the results replicated the original effect, were lower still than a generous reading of the intervals. The indicators disagree in detail and agree in direction: the published effects were larger and more often significant than the repeated ones.{{cite:osc2015}} (Results)

The original \(p\)-value predicts replication. Of original studies with \(p<0.02\), \(26\) of \(63\), about \(41\%\), reached \(p<0.05\) in the replication. Of those with \(p\) between \(0.02\) and \(0.04\), \(6\) of \(23\), about \(26\%\), did. Of those with \(p>0.04\), \(2\) of \(11\), about \(18\%\), did. Of those with \(p<0.001\), \(20\) of \(32\), about \(63\%\), did. The crisis is worse for results that barely cleared the threshold. It is not confined to them. A result with \(p<0.001\) still failed to replicate about a third of the time in this set.{{cite:osc2015}} (Results)

Field and design matter. Social-psychology effects replicated at \(25\%\) by the significance vote. Cognitive-psychology effects replicated at \(50\%\). The project ties the difference in part to weaker original effects in social psychology and to within-subject designs in cognitive psychology. Main or simple effects replicated at \(23\) of \(49\), about \(47\%\). Interactions replicated at \(8\) of \(37\), about \(22\%\). An interaction is a difference of differences. The lower rate is a property of that target as well as of the literature.{{cite:osc2015}} (Results)

## History
The theoretical statement is Ioannidis's 2005 essay in *PLoS Medicine*. The essay's claim is that it can be proved, inside the model above, that most claimed research findings are false, under conditions that the essay argues are common in modern research. The essay is explicit that research should not be summarised by \(p\)-values alone, and that a single significant study is a weak basis for a conclusive claim. The positive predictive value is the quantity the essay puts in place of the \(p\)-value.{{cite:ioannidis2005}} (p. 0696)

The empirical statement, for psychology, is the Open Science Collaboration's 2015 paper in *Science*. One hundred studies were repeated with high-powered designs and with original materials when those materials were available. The introduction of that paper treats direct replication as the way a finding becomes reproducible with new data, and it lists the reasons a replication can miss: a moderator that differs, a false positive in the original, a false negative in the replication. The results section then shows that the miss was common and that the effects shrank. Concern about replication was already widespread. The project supplied a rate.{{cite:osc2015}} (Introduction and Results)

The two papers play different roles. Ioannidis gives a mechanism: prior odds, power, multiplicity, and flexibility. The replication project gives a count in one literature. A field with larger effects, fewer tested relationships, and registered analyses would have a higher positive predictive value in the formula, and it might show a higher replication rate. The formula does not estimate that rate. The rate does not estimate \(R\). Reading either number as the other overclaims what was computed.{{cite:ioannidis2005}} (p. 0696){{cite:osc2015}} (Results)
