## Lead context
An interaction means that the effect of one predictor changes with the value of another. The effect must be measured on a stated response or model scale: a difference in mean yield, a slope in birth weight, and a change in log odds are different quantities. Interaction is common in factorial experiments because every combination of factor levels can be observed. Reporting only one average “effect” can hide the comparison that matters for a decision.{{cite:nist_interactions2002}} (§3.4.2.3){{cite:nist_two_way_anova2002}} (§7.4.3.7)

## Motivating example
Suppose a pesticide improves a crop's yield a little at a low fertiliser level but substantially at a medium level. The useful question is then not just whether pesticide raises yield *on average*; it is how much it raises yield at each fertiliser level. The crop table shows pesticide differences of $3.57$, $6.14$ and $2.46$ at low, medium and high fertiliser. These are not one constant effect. The accompanying interaction plot shows the nonparallel response lines, while the wheat example shows nearly parallel lines and little evidence of interaction.{{cite:nist_interactions2002}} (§3.4.2.3)

## Intuition
For two binary factors $A$ and $B$, write the four mean responses as $\mu_{00},\mu_{10},\mu_{01},\mu_{11}$. The effect of moving $A$ from 0 to 1 is $\mu_{10}-\mu_{00}$ when $B=0$ and $\mu_{11}-\mu_{01}$ when $B=1$. Their difference, $\delta=\mu_{11}-\mu_{01}-\mu_{10}+\mu_{00}$, is the interaction contrast on the mean-response scale. If $\delta=0$, the two effects of $A$ are equal and the two lines in an interaction plot are parallel. Their slopes need not be zero: parallel lines can represent substantial *main* effects with no interaction.{{cite:nist_interactions2002}} (§3.4.2.3){{cite:nist_two_way_anova2002}} (§7.4.3.7, interaction sum of squares)

Reversing the roles of $A$ and $B$ gives the same $\delta$. It is equally the change in the effect of $B$ between levels of $A$. Crossing lines are one conspicuous form of nonparallelism, but lines can differ in slope without crossing within the observed factor range. A plot is useful for seeing patterns, while a numerical contrast and its uncertainty are needed to distinguish a modelled interaction from visual fluctuation.{{cite:nist_interactions2002}} (§3.4.2.3)

## Definition
In a two-factor mean model, interaction is departure from an additive representation $\mu_{ij}=\mu+\alpha_i+\beta_j$. Equivalently, an additional term $\gamma_{ij}$ is needed to describe how the effect of one factor depends on the other. The values of individual coefficients depend on the coding constraints, but fitted cell means and their contrasts do not. For a balanced factorial experiment with replication, the interaction sum of squares measures the component of cell-mean variation remaining after row and column effects are fitted. It has $(a-1)(b-1)$ degrees of freedom for $a$ and $b$ factor levels.{{cite:nist_two_way_anova2002}} (§7.4.3.7, model and ANOVA table){{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 266)

For binary indicators, treatment coding writes $\mathbb E(Y\mid A,B)=\beta_0+\beta_A A+\beta_B B+\beta_{AB}AB$. Then $\beta_0=\mu_{00}$, $\beta_A=\mu_{10}-\mu_{00}$, $\beta_B=\mu_{01}-\mu_{00}$ and $\beta_{AB}=\delta$. The $A$ coefficient is its effect when $B=0$; at $B=1$, its effect is $\beta_A+\beta_{AB}$. For a quantitative predictor $x$ and a binary group $B$, the model $\mathbb E(Y\mid x,B)=\beta_0+\beta_xx+\beta_BB+\beta_{xB}xB$ has slope $\beta_x$ in group 0 and $\beta_x+\beta_{xB}$ in group 1. This is the logic of the age-by-smoking example.{{cite:nist_interactions2002}} (§3.4.2.3)

## Properties
The binary interaction contrast is a difference of differences. Adding the same constant to every cell mean leaves it unchanged, as does swapping the names of the two factors. Reversing the 0/1 coding of one factor changes its sign but not whether it is zero. These facts help separate the scientific question, “Do the effects vary?”, from a coefficient's arbitrary coding convention. Centering a quantitative predictor changes what the group main-effect coefficient means, because its reference $x$ changes, while the difference in slopes remains $\beta_{xB}$.{{cite:nist_two_way_anova2002}} (§7.4.3.7){{cite:nist_interactions2002}} (§3.4.2.3)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Evaluate the treatment-coded mean model at all four settings: $\mu_{00}=\beta_0$, $\mu_{10}=\beta_0+\beta_A$, $\mu_{01}=\beta_0+\beta_B$, and $\mu_{11}=\beta_0+\beta_A+\beta_B+\beta_{AB}$. Substitution yields $\mu_{11}-\mu_{01}-\mu_{10}+\mu_{00}=\beta_{AB}$. Thus the difference in $A$ effects is zero exactly when the binary interaction coefficient is zero. The same algebra with the roles exchanged gives the difference in $B$ effects.{{cite:nist_two_way_anova2002}} (§7.4.3.7, two-way model)

For quantitative $x$, subtract the group-0 fitted mean from the group-1 mean: $\beta_B+\beta_{xB}x$. Its change when $x$ rises by one unit is $\beta_{xB}$, so the group slopes differ by that coefficient. Replacing $x$ by $x-c$ changes the group contrast at the new reference value to $\beta_B+c\beta_{xB}$, but its derivative in $x$ and the slope difference remain $\beta_{xB}$. In the balanced two-factor ANOVA, subtracting row and column mean components leaves the interaction residual of each cell; squaring and weighting these residuals gives the NIST interaction sum of squares and its $(a-1)(b-1)$ dimensional test.{{cite:nist_two_way_anova2002}} (§7.4.3.7, sums of squares and ANOVA table)
:::
:::

## Statistical use
Start with the response scale and the comparison of interest. In a factorial experiment, show a table or plot of cell means and calculate relevant simple effects before collapsing across a factor. Fit the interaction model, then examine the interaction contrast or joint test with uncertainty. The $F$ test in the wheat and crop examples asks whether departures from additivity are large relative to residual variation; its p-value is not a measure of the interaction's size. A nonsignificant interaction test in a small or noisy study does not establish exact parallelism.{{cite:nist_interactions2002}} (§3.4.2.3){{cite:nist_two_way_anova2002}} (§7.4.3.7)

In regression, retain the main terms when interpreting a product term. The coefficient of smoking in the birth-weight model refers to age zero, while the age slope for smokers is the age coefficient plus the age-by-smoking coefficient. Recenter age at a meaningful value to make the smoking contrast interpretable near the data. If the model uses a link function, state the scale: a logistic interaction is a change in *log odds* contrast, and a log-link interaction is a change in a log mean ratio. Equal effects on one scale need not be equal on another. These are algebraic consequences of the chosen linear predictor, not new causal claims.{{cite:nist_interactions2002}} (§3.4.2.3)

## Worked example
Consider hypothetical mean yields for two binary settings. Let $\mu_{00}=10$, $\mu_{10}=14$, $\mu_{01}=12$, and $\mu_{11}=21$, where the first index is treatment $A$ and the second is setting $B$. At $B=0$, treatment $A$ changes the mean by $14-10=4$; at $B=1$, it changes the mean by $21-12=9$. The interaction contrast is $9-4=5$. Equivalently, the $B$ effect changes from $12-10=2$ at $A=0$ to $21-14=7$ at $A=1$, also a change of 5. A treatment-coded mean model that reproduces all four cells is $10+4A+2B+5AB$. Without the product term it would predict 16 rather than 21 for the joint setting $(1,1)$. These cell means alone supply an effect contrast, not a standard error or a test.{{cite:nist_two_way_anova2002}} (§7.4.3.7, model and interaction sum of squares)

**Your turn.** For another hypothetical $2\times2$ table, take $\mu_{00}=8$, $\mu_{10}=11$, $\mu_{01}=13$, and $\mu_{11}=20$. Find the $A$ effect at each level of $B$, the interaction contrast, and the treatment-coded mean model. What would the additive model predict at $(1,1)$?{{cite:nist_two_way_anova2002}} (§7.4.3.7)

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The $A$ effect is $11-8=3$ when $B=0$ and $20-13=7$ when $B=1$, so the interaction contrast is $7-3=4$. The $B$ effect at $A=0$ is $13-8=5$. The model is $\mathbb E(Y\mid A,B)=8+3A+5B+4AB$, which gives 20 at $(1,1)$. Omitting the product term would predict $8+3+5=16$ there. The contrast is on the response-mean scale.
:::

## Common misconceptions
Interaction does not require two plotted lines to cross; any change in their difference across levels is interaction. A visible crossing is not proof of a precise population effect without accounting for sample variation. Conversely, a large main effect can coexist with zero interaction when the lines are parallel. A main coefficient in a model with a product term is tied to the other variable's reference value, not necessarily its overall average effect.{{cite:nist_interactions2002}} (§3.4.2.3)

## Assumptions and limits
The mean-model contrast is a description of fitted or observed cell means. The standard two-way ANOVA $F$ reference additionally uses the model's error and design assumptions, including the specified factor structure and residual variation. Observational groups can differ for reasons other than the two recorded predictors; an interaction coefficient alone does not establish causal effect modification. With sparse or empty factor combinations, some contrasts may not be estimable without extra model assumptions. In a nonlinear-link model, an interaction statement must name its scale.{{cite:nist_two_way_anova2002}} (§7.4.3.7){{cite:nist_interactions2002}} (§3.4.2.3)

## History
Dodge's encyclopedia attributes the interaction concept in factorial design to Fisher's work in 1925 and 1935. Its entry presents the two-way model with a separate interaction term for each pair of factor levels. The contemporary NIST formulation uses the same separation of overall mean, factor effects, interaction and error, with explicit sums of squares for a balanced design.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 266){{cite:nist_two_way_anova2002}} (§7.4.3.7)
