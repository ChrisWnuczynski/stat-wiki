## Motivating example
A study compares treatments in seven hospitals. A treatment coefficient describes a contrast intended to apply across the study, but hospital baselines may differ for reasons unrelated to treatment. If the seven named hospitals are themselves the objects of comparison, estimating a separate hospital contrast answers that question directly. If the aim includes patients at other comparable hospitals, a distribution of hospital effects gives a way to describe between-hospital variation and predict a new hospital. The choice therefore changes the target of inference, even when the observed data are identical.{{cite:gelman2005_anova}} (§6, pp. 20–22){{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, pp. 2–3)

## Intuition
In a fixed-effects description, each named group receives its own coefficient. A comparison such as hospital A minus hospital B refers to precisely those groups. In a random-effects description, the group deviations are modeled as draws from a common distribution with a variance parameter. That parameter describes heterogeneity among groups, and observed groups can lend information to one another through partial pooling. A random effect is not simply an ordinary regression coefficient that happens to be uncertain: all estimated coefficients have uncertainty. The extra modeling step is a distribution for group deviations.{{cite:gelman2005_anova}} (§6, pp. 20–21){{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, pp. 2–3)

## Definition
For a normal response $Y_{ij}$ from unit $i$ in group $j$, one fixed-group model is $Y_{ij}=\beta_0+\gamma_j+\mathbf x_{ij}^{\top}\boldsymbol\beta+\varepsilon_{ij}$, with the $\gamma_j$ treated as unknown parameters and a constraint or reference category used for identification. A random-intercept model writes $Y_{ij}=\beta_0+b_j+\mathbf x_{ij}^{\top}\boldsymbol\beta+\varepsilon_{ij}$ and adds $b_j\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}N(0,\tau^2)$, independent of errors $\varepsilon_{ij}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}N(0,\sigma^2)$. In the latter model, $\tau^2$ is the between-group variance and $\sigma^2$ is the within-group variance. Both models can contain the same fixed treatment coefficient.{{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, equations for conditional response and random effects){{cite:gelman2005_anova}} (§6, pp. 20–21)

The word *fixed* also has other uses. Gelman documents definitions based on which levels are of interest, whether the levels exhaust a population, whether a distribution is assigned, and whether estimates are shrunk. They need not agree in every design. Say which estimand and group population you mean instead of deciding from a variable's name alone. For example, calendar year could be a set of named years to compare or a source of year-to-year variation, depending on the question.{{cite:gelman2005_anova}} (§6, pp. 20–22)

## Interpretation — What partial pooling changes
In a balanced random-intercept model with $m$ observations per group and known $\beta_0,\tau^2,\sigma^2$, the group mean has sampling variance $\sigma^2/m$ around $\beta_0+b_j$. Normal conditioning gives the predicted group deviation $\widehat b_j=w(\overline y_j-\beta_0)$, where $w=\tau^2/(\tau^2+\sigma^2/m)$. This is between zero and one for positive variances. A noisy small group gets a smaller weight and moves further toward the common mean; a large precise group stays closer to its own mean. The fixed-group fitted mean in an intercept-only balanced model is simply $\overline y_j$.{{cite:gelman2005_anova}} (§6, pp. 20–22, shrinkage distinction){{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, conditional normal model)

The random-effects model also predicts a group not yet observed. Its deviation has mean zero before data about that group arrive, so its predicted mean is the common fixed part, with predictive uncertainty reflecting $\tau^2$. A fixed set of named group indicators gives no analogous distribution for a new level without further assumptions. This difference matters most when the intended conclusion extends beyond the sampled groups.{{cite:gelman2005_anova}} (§6, pp. 20–22)

## Properties — Shared variation within groups
Under the random-intercept model, two observations in the same group share $b_j$. Their marginal covariance is $\tau^2$, each marginal variance is $\tau^2+\sigma^2$, and their intraclass correlation is $\tau^2/(\tau^2+\sigma^2)$. The correlation is nonnegative in this model; that is a feature of this specific shared-intercept mechanism, not a rule that all within-group associations must be positive. Independent groups have zero covariance under the stated assumptions.{{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, conditional response and random-effect covariance)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Let $Y_{ij}=\beta_0+b_j+\varepsilon_{ij}$ with independent centered $b_j$ and errors, with variances $\tau^2$ and $\sigma^2$. Variance addition gives $\operatorname{Var}(Y_{ij})=\tau^2+\sigma^2$. For different $i,i'$ in the same group, all cross-covariances involving independent errors vanish, leaving $\operatorname{Cov}(Y_{ij},Y_{i'j})=\operatorname{Var}(b_j)=\tau^2$. Dividing by the marginal variance gives the stated correlation.

For shrinkage, condition on the common mean and variances. The prior density for $b_j$ is proportional to $\exp\{-b_j^2/(2\tau^2)\}$, while the group-mean likelihood is proportional to $\exp\{-[\overline y_j-(\beta_0+b_j)]^2/(2\sigma^2/m)\}$. Completing the square makes the conditional mean of $b_j$ equal to $\{\tau^2/(\tau^2+\sigma^2/m)\}(\overline y_j-\beta_0)$. Under normality the conditional mean and mode coincide.{{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, Gaussian mixed-model specification){{cite:gelman2005_anova}} (§6, shrinkage interpretation)
:::
:::

## Statistical use — Choosing the estimand
Begin with the scientific question. If the five specified fertilizers are the only treatments of interest, their named contrasts are naturally fixed. If three land blocks were sampled to represent changing sites, a random block intercept can describe site variation. The hospital, fertilizer–species–block, and joke examples illustrate that the same design can involve both kinds of effect. A mixed model then combines fixed contrasts with a model for repeated or grouped departures.{{cite:gelman2005_anova}} (§6, pp. 20–22){{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1, mixed-effects terminology)

The random-effects assumptions deserve inspection. Group effects need a plausible common population and, in the simple model, independence from the covariates used to estimate fixed coefficients. If hospitals with high baseline risk systematically receive a particular treatment, interpreting the treatment coefficient without addressing that association is unsafe. Estimating a variance from only a few groups can also be imprecise. Restricted maximum likelihood is often used for Gaussian mixed-model variance components; it does not turn a questionable grouping story into a valid one.{{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§3, likelihood and REML criteria){{cite:gelman2005_anova}} (§6, pp. 20–22)

## Worked example
Suppose three hospitals each contribute three measurements, and their observed means are $8,10,12$. With only hospital indicators and no other predictors, a fixed-effects fit gives those three means and a hospital-three minus hospital-one contrast of $12-8=4$. For illustration, suppose a random-intercept analysis takes the common mean as $10$, between-hospital variance $\tau^2=4$, and individual residual variance $\sigma^2=12$. Each hospital mean has conditional variance $12/3=4$, so $w=4/(4+4)=1/2$. The partially pooled predictions are $10+(1/2)(8-10)=9$, $10$, and $10+(1/2)(12-10)=11$. The comparison of their predictions is two, because both deviations have moved halfway toward the common mean. These are conditional predictions for the observed hospitals, not a replacement for a fixed-effects contrast when the named hospitals themselves are the target.{{cite:gelman2005_anova}} (§6, pp. 20–22){{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1)

The same variances imply a within-hospital correlation of $4/(4+12)=0.25$. Before observing a new hospital, its mean is predicted as $10$ and a single individual's marginal variance is $4+12=16$. That prediction is more uncertain than a prediction conditional on knowing a particular hospital's intercept. The numerical variance values are chosen to expose the pooling calculation; real analyses estimate them and propagate their uncertainty.{{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1, marginal and conditional models)

**Your turn.** A new group has two observations with mean $6$. If the common mean is $10$, between-group variance is $4$, and individual residual variance is $8$, find its conditional random-intercept prediction and its within-group correlation under the normal model.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The group mean has conditional variance $8/2=4$, so $w=4/(4+4)=1/2$. Its predicted deviation is $(1/2)(6-10)=-2$, giving predicted group mean $10-2=8$. The correlation of distinct observations in this group is $4/(4+8)=1/3$. This is a model-based prediction given the assumed variance parameters.{{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1)
:::

## Common misconceptions
“Random” does not mean that the analyst chose factor levels by a physical random-number device; it denotes a distribution assigned to effects for the inferential purpose at hand. “Fixed” does not mean known without error; fixed coefficients are estimated and uncertain. A random intercept does not make all observations independent after the intercept is averaged out: it induces positive within-group covariance. And shrinkage is not evidence that observed group differences were mistakes; it balances each noisy group mean against information from the other groups under a common-population model.{{cite:gelman2005_anova}} (§6, definitions and shrinkage){{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1)

## Assumptions and limits — When the group story fails
If the three hospitals are the entire policy target, a population variance for hypothetical hospitals may answer an unnecessary question and obscure the named comparisons. If a group has only one observation, its intercept is especially hard to separate from residual noise. If a treatment is assigned mainly in high-risk hospitals, independent random effects and treatment covariates are implausible; a simple random-intercept coefficient may be biased for the intended treatment contrast. If competition creates negative within-group correlation, the shared positive-variance random intercept cannot generate it. These are limits of this model, not of all possible mixed models.{{cite:gelman2005_anova}} (§6, pp. 20–22){{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1.1)

## History
Gelman's review of the fixed–random distinction traces the variance-component terminology at least to Eisenhart's 1947 treatment and records several definitions still in use. His analysis of variance account argues that the decisive question is often whether inference concerns the existing levels or new levels of a factor. Modern mixed-model software represents the two roles explicitly as fixed and random terms in the predictor.{{cite:gelman2005_anova}} (§6, pp. 20–22){{cite:bates_lme4_2015}} (§1, p. 2)
