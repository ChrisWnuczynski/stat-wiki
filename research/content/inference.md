## Lead context

A poll, a clinical series, or a production sample is a fragment of a larger collection. The fragment is in hand. The collection is not. Inference is the step that treats the fragment as evidence about a quantity defined on the collection: a proportion, a mean, a treatment contrast, a future observation. The step is inductive. It does not follow from the arithmetic of the sample alone, and it is not a description of the sample for its own sake. {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 263) {{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Part I, p. 1)

## Definition

Inference is reasoning by induction from information collected on a sample, with the aim of saying something about the population tied to that sample. Sampling, in the same vocabulary, is the selection of part of a population, the study of characteristics of that part, and the making of inferences relative to the population. The population quantity may be a parameter that organises the sampling distribution, or a further observation that has not yet been recorded. Either way, the target is not one of the numbers already on the page. {{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 263, 468)

Statistical inference is primarily concerned with understanding and quantifying the uncertainty of parameter estimates. The equations and details change with the setting. The foundations are shared: a point estimate, an interval of plausible values for the population quantity, and a test of a claim about that quantity. Descriptive summary stays inside the observed records. Inference leaves them, and therefore has to say under what sampling story the departure is allowed. {{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, introduction preceding §5.1) {{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 263–264)

A [confidence interval](confidence-interval.qmd) reports a range from a method with a long-run coverage rate. A test compares the sample with what a stated hypothesis would tend to produce. An [estimator](estimator.qmd) is the rule that turns the sample into a number aimed at the parameter. Bayesian inference fits a probability model and summarises the result by a probability distribution on the parameters and on unobserved quantities such as a prediction. That is a different reading of the same inductive step: the unknown carries the probability, rather than the procedure. {{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Part I, p. 1; Ch. 1, §1.1, p. 3) {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 264)

## Properties

The ordinary sequence is to name the target, name the sampling model, compute a summary, and attach a statement about error. For a proportion, the sample proportion is the point estimate of the population proportion, often written \(p\) for the population value and \(\hat p\) for the sample value. The gap between them is the error of the estimate. Sampling error is the part that changes from sample to sample. Bias is a systematic tendency to overshoot or undershoot, for example when the wording of a question pushes respondents toward one answer. Much of the mathematics of inference is about the first part. Thoughtful collection is what keeps the second part small. {{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, §5.1.1)

A sampling distribution is the distribution of the estimate across repeated samples drawn the same way. It is never observed in an application, and it is still the object that gives the estimate a standard error. Consider a population proportion \(p=0.88\) and samples of size \(n=1000\). One simulated sample produced \(\hat p=0.894\), an error of \(+0.014\). Further draws produced \(0.885\), \(0.878\), and \(0.859\), with errors \(+0.005\), \(-0.002\), and \(-0.021\). In 10,000 repetitions the centre of the sample proportions was \(0.880\) and their standard deviation was \(0.010\). That standard deviation is the standard error of \(\hat p\) in this design. A sample of size 50 would be expected to have a larger standard error. The property is general: under a simple random sample, a larger sample tends to give a more precise point estimate. {{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, §5.1.2)

Hypothesis tests and intervals are two public forms of the same calculation. An interval lists parameter values the method would not find surprising at a stated rate. A test asks whether a particular value, often a null claim, sits so far in the tail of the sampling distribution that the method rejects it. In a one-proportion test the null value is also the value used to compute the standard error, because the reference distribution is the distribution under the hypothesis, not under the estimate. Both forms need the model that connects the sample to the population. Without that model the arithmetic is a description. {{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, §5.3) {{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 250–251)

Only a probability sample makes the sampling error calculable from the design. Random methods — simple random sampling, stratified sampling, cluster sampling — use a chance mechanism to choose the units, and the variables observed on the sample are random variables. A quota sample and other nonrandom selections do not support that calculation. The 1936 *Literary Digest* telephone poll is the historical picture: a large sample whose coverage of the population was tilted toward households that owned telephones, and a prediction that failed. Size is not a substitute for the mechanism. {{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 468–470)

Prediction of a new case is inference when the new case is not in the sample. The uncertainty then includes both uncertainty about the fitted parameter and the fresh noise of the new case. A probability statement about the parameter itself, rather than about the behaviour of a procedure, is developed as Bayesian updating: the posterior is proportional to the prior times the likelihood. {{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 1, §1.3, pp. 7–8) {{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 32–33)

## History

The early history of inferential statistics is the early history of probability used on data. Bayes’s essay on the doctrine of chances, published by Price in 1763, de Moivre’s work on the probability of events in play, Gauss’s theory of errors, and Laplace’s analytic theory of probabilities supplied ways to pass between observations and an unknown quantity. Later in the nineteenth century Galton’s treatment of correlation, and Pearson’s criterion for whether a system of deviations could reasonably have arisen from random sampling, made the sample-to-population step a standard scientific calculation. {{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 263–264)

Gosset’s 1908 paper on the probable error of a mean gave small samples a reference distribution of their own. Neyman and Pearson’s 1928 papers set out how test criteria were to be used and interpreted, and their 1933 paper separated the error of rejecting a true null from the error of missing a false one. Fisher’s writing on statistical methods and scientific inference tied estimation, likelihood, and experimental design into one programme. The sampling institutions matured on a parallel track. Kiaer’s 1895 plea for representative samples was resisted by a congress that preferred complete enumeration. By the Rome congress of 1925 the live question was which sampling method to use. Modern introductions still organise the subject as point estimation, intervals, and tests, with a separate development for probability statements about the parameter. {{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 250–251, 264, 468–469) {{cite:openintro2019}} (Ch. 5, introduction preceding §5.1)

## Connections

[Estimation theory](estimation-theory.qmd) is the part of the subject that produces a number or an interval for a parameter. [Frequentist inference](frequentist-inference.qmd) judges procedures by their behaviour in repeated samples. [Mathematical statistics](mathematical-statistics.qmd) is the body of proofs that makes those judgements precise. [Asymptotic analysis](asymptotic-analysis.qmd) replaces an exact sampling distribution, when one is unavailable, by a limit as the sample size grows. A [decision problem](decision-problem.qmd) asks what action the inference should support, once losses are named. [Sampling](sampling.qmd) is the design step without which the error statement does not refer to the study that was done. A [statistical model](statistical-model.qmd) is the family inside which the parameter is defined. The [foundations of statistics](foundations-of-statistics.qmd) are the set of distinctions — population and sample, description and inference, experiment and observation — that this step relies on.
