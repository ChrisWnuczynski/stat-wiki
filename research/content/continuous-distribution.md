## Motivating example
A laboratory measures a reaction time that can vary across an interval. Asking for the probability of exactly one real-number value is usually unhelpful; asking whether the time falls between two limits has a clear answer. A density describes how probability is spread over such intervals, and its area gives their probabilities. The figures contrast smooth density curves with the jumps of a discrete distribution function. They also make a modelling issue visible: recording a time after rounding it to whole seconds produces a different, discrete observed variable.{{cite:siegrist2022_continuous}} (§ Basic Theory, Definitions and Basic Properties){{cite:knill2009_probability}} (§2.12, classes of random variables)

## Intuition
For a density $f$, the height $f(x)$ describes concentration of probability near $x$, while an integral of heights over an interval gives probability. Narrowing the interval around a fixed point makes its area tend to zero even when $f(x)$ is high. The distribution function $F(x)=\mathbb P(X\le x)$ accumulates that area from the left: $F(b)-F(a)$ is the probability between $a$ and $b$. Thus the density and CDF are two views of the same *density-based* law. A CDF itself also describes laws without a density, which is why it is the more general starting point.{{cite:siegrist2022_continuous}} (§ Basic Theory, Definitions and Basic Properties, density and CDF){{cite:knill2009_probability}} (§2.12, absolutely and singularly continuous classes)

## Definition — Two uses of continuous
In a common density-based convention, $X$ has an *absolutely continuous* distribution: some nonnegative $f$ integrates to one and $F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f(u)\,du$. Then $\mathbb P(a<X\le b)=\int_a^b f(u)\,du$, and $F'=f$ almost everywhere. A broader convention calls a distribution *continuous* when it has no atoms, equivalently when its CDF has no jumps. Every density-based law meets this broader definition, but the converse fails. The Cantor distribution has a continuous CDF and gives every individual point probability zero while concentrating all its probability on a zero-length set; it has no density with respect to ordinary length. The density calculations here concern the absolutely continuous subclass unless stated otherwise.{{cite:siegrist2022_continuous}} (§ Basic Theory, Definitions and Basic Properties, atom-free laws and density exception){{cite:knill2009_probability}} (§2.12, singular continuous Cantor distribution)

## Interpretation — Areas, heights and units
If $X$ is measured in seconds, a density $f(x)$ has units of inverse seconds. Its numeric height can be greater than one without violating any probability rule. For example, a uniform law on $[0,0.25]$ has density $4$ on that interval; the probability of $(0.10,0.15)$ is $4(0.15-0.10)=0.20$. A point $x$ has probability zero, so changing $f(x)$ at one point changes no probability. The CDF, not a single density height, gives probabilities for thresholds. The examples use this: a cubic CDF yields a density by differentiation, while a uniform point in an interval yields probabilities by length.{{cite:siegrist2022_continuous}} (§ Basic Theory, density values and integrals; Examples and Applications, continuous uniform distributions)

For a density-based $X$, $\mathbb P(a<X<b)=\mathbb P(a\le X\le b)=F(b)-F(a)$ because either endpoint has probability zero. This endpoint rule fails when an atom is present. The expected value of an integrable function $h(X)$ is $\int h(x)f(x)\,dx$ for a density-based law. This replaces the sum $\sum_x h(x)\mathbb P(X=x)$ used for a discrete law. The two figures show both shapes, while the probability rule itself remains a statement about events, not about a graph’s visual height.{{cite:siegrist2022_continuous}} (§ Basic Theory, Definitions and Basic Properties, integrals and zero singleton mass){{cite:knill2009_probability}} (§2.12, discrete and absolutely continuous classes)

## Properties — What a density guarantees
A candidate density must be nonnegative and integrate to one over its support. These checks are necessary before using its formula to calculate probabilities. Once they hold, the CDF is nondecreasing and continuous, with limits zero and one at the two ends of the real line. Where the usual derivative exists, it agrees with the density, but densities that differ only on a zero-length set describe the same law. A density may be discontinuous or unbounded; the integral, rather than the value at a particular point, determines the distribution.{{cite:siegrist2022_continuous}} (§ Basic Theory, Definitions and Basic Properties; Constructing Probability Density Functions)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
Let $f\ge0$ with $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f=1$ and define $\mathbb P(X\in A)=\int_A f(x)\,dx$. Additivity of the integral gives $F(b)-F(a)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ for $a<b$. A singleton has length zero, so its integral is zero; hence the choice of strict or weak endpoints does not change an interval probability. The integral over a shrinking interval tends to zero for integrable $f$, giving continuity of $F$. For continuous $f$ at $x$, the fundamental theorem of calculus gives $F'(x)=f(x)$; the almost-everywhere version for arbitrary integrable $f$ is the standard differentiation theorem. To see why continuity alone is insufficient, form the Cantor set by removing middle thirds. At stage $n$ its remaining total length is $(2/3)^n$, so its limiting length is zero. The random ternary sum $X=\sum_{n\ge1}2B_n/3^n$, with independent fair binary $B_n$, belongs to that set with probability one. A specified point fixes every finite prefix of the ternary digits, whose probability is at most $2^{-n}$, so it has probability zero. If this law had a density $f$, the probability of the zero-length Cantor set would be $\int_C f=0$, contradicting its probability one.{{cite:siegrist2022_continuous}} (§ Basic Theory, Definitions and Basic Properties, densities and atom-free laws){{cite:knill2009_probability}} (§2.12, Cantor distribution and singular continuity)
:::
:::

## Statistical use — Likelihood and recorded measurements
For independent exact observations from a parametric density $f(x;\theta)$, likelihood multiplies density values $\prod_i f(x_i;\theta)$. Those factors are not probabilities of the exact observations; they compare how models concentrate probability near the observed values. If a device rounds a continuous reading to the nearest unit, the recorded value has a point mass equal to the integral over its rounding bin. A likelihood for the rounded data should use those bin probabilities. A model with an atom at zero, such as a device that sometimes produces a true zero and otherwise a positive continuous measurement, needs both its point mass and density component. Treating the density alone as if it integrated to one would omit part of the law.{{cite:siegrist2022_continuous}} (§ Basic Theory, density and conditional-density integrals){{cite:knill2009_probability}} (§2.12, pure-point and absolutely continuous components)

## Worked example
Suppose a continuous measurement $X$ has candidate density $f(x)=c(1+x)$ for $0<x<2$, and zero elsewhere. Normalisation requires $1=c\int_0^2(1+x)\,dx=c(2+2)$, so $c=1/4$. The CDF inside the support is $F(x)=(x+x^2/2)/4$. The probability of $0.5<X<1.5$ is $F(1.5)-F(0.5)=(2.625-0.625)/4=0.5$. Its expected value is $\mathbb EX=(1/4)\int_0^2x(1+x)\,dx=(1/4)(2+8/3)=7/6\approx1.1667$. The density at $x=1$ is $f(1)=0.5$, yet $\mathbb P(X=1)=0$: one height is not a point probability.{{cite:siegrist2022_continuous}} (§ Basic Theory, constructing densities and integral probabilities)

**Your turn.** A sensor reports zero with probability $0.2$; otherwise it reports a value drawn uniformly from $(0,1)$. Find $\mathbb P(X=0)$ and $\mathbb P(0.25<X<0.75)$. Can a density alone describe the whole law?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The atom has probability $\mathbb P(X=0)=0.2$. On $(0,1)$ the continuous component has unconditional density $0.8$, so the interval probability is $0.8(0.75-0.25)=0.4$. That density integrates to $0.8$, leaving the atom’s $0.2$ to complete total probability one. A single ordinary density cannot assign positive probability to the exact value zero.
:::

## Common misconceptions
A density value $f(x)$ is not $\mathbb P(X=x)$; an absolutely continuous law gives every singleton probability zero. A density is not restricted to heights below one; the area under it is the probability. A CDF without jumps need not be the integral of a density, as the Cantor law shows. A real-valued measurement is not automatically continuously distributed after rounding or censoring. Finally, $F'=f$ holds almost everywhere in a density model, while a density’s arbitrary value at one point is not identifiable from probabilities. These errors concern different objects: point mass, density scale, existence of a density, observation process and derivative convention.{{cite:siegrist2022_continuous}} (§ Basic Theory, Definitions and Basic Properties, density nonuniqueness){{cite:knill2009_probability}} (§2.12, continuous classes)

## Assumptions and limits
A density-only calculation fails for the mixed sensor example because it misses the $0.2$ atom. For the Cantor law, every point has probability zero yet integrating any ordinary density over its zero-length support gives zero instead of one. For rounded readings, the reported integers have positive masses even when the underlying physical quantity has a smooth density. The smoothness of a plotted curve is also no evidence that it fits data: an exponential density and a normal density may both look smooth but assign very different tail probabilities. State whether the model concerns the latent quantity or the recorded one, and check support and normalisation before calculating or fitting.{{cite:siegrist2022_continuous}} (§ Basic Theory, density conditions and existence exception){{cite:knill2009_probability}} (§2.12, singular and mixed classes)

## Connections
The [cumulative distribution function](cumulative-distribution-function.qmd) describes any real-valued law, including those without densities. An [atom](atom-of-a-distribution.qmd) makes a CDF jump; a [discrete distribution](discrete-distribution.qmd) is built from such masses. The [exponential distribution](exponential-distribution.qmd), [normal distribution](normal-distribution.qmd) and [uniform distribution](uniform-distribution.qmd) are familiar density-based examples. [Expectation of a function](expectation-of-a-function.qmd) replaces summation by integration when a density exists. The distance-to-the-centre question derives a density from a geometric CDF, the lifetime question uses a continuous tail probability, and the shifted-exponential question uses a density to calculate an expectation and a simulation rule.{{cite:siegrist2022_continuous}} (§ Basic Theory and Examples and Applications){{cite:knill2009_probability}} (§2.12, classes of laws)

## History
Classical density calculations treat probability as area, while the later axiomatic formulation treats probabilities as assignments to events. Kolmogorov’s 1933 monograph is the source of the familiar countably additive probability axioms. Knill also records earlier axiomatic proposals. In that framework the CDF and probability measure remain meaningful without a density. The distinction between absolutely continuous and singular continuous laws, illustrated by the Cantor distribution, clarifies exactly which part of continuous probability the elementary area method describes.{{cite:knill2009_probability}} (§2.1, remark on the axioms; §2.12, classes of random variables and Cantor example){{cite:siegrist2022_continuous}} (§ Basic Theory, Definitions and Basic Properties)
