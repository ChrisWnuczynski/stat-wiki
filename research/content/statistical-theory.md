

## Lead context

Statistical theory is the mathematical account of what an estimator, a test, and a prediction rule do when the data are random. It asks which procedures have a small bias, a small variance, or a small expected loss, and under which models those comparisons are valid.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 184–185, the theory of estimation; p. 450, the development of statistical theory){{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (pp. 18–20, §2.4, expected prediction error)

## Definition

An estimator is any function of a sample that is used as a guess at an unknown parameter of the population that produced the sample. The theory of estimation compares such functions. Its basic defects and virtues have standard names: bias is the gap between the expected value of the estimator and the parameter, variance describes the scatter of the estimator across repeated samples, and sufficiency describes a reduction of the sample that keeps every relevant fact about the parameter. Maximum likelihood and the method of moments are two systematic ways to build the function in the first place. Hypothesis testing, in the line of Neyman and Pearson, is the companion theory for choosing between statements about the same parameter. Prediction adds a loss and takes the expected loss, under the joint distribution of predictors and response, as the score of a rule.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 184–185, 372 and 450){{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (pp. 18–20, §2.4)

The scope is the behaviour of procedures under an explicit probability model. A numerical answer computed on one data set is an estimate. The theory is about the procedure that would be applied to the next data set as well. Regression analysis sits inside the subject once its coefficients are treated as estimators with a sampling distribution, rather than only as a curve drawn through a cloud.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 452, reliability of regression estimates){{cite:james_islr2013}} (pp. 21–22, §2.1.2, reducing a fit to a finite parameter)

## Statistical use

Bias, variance, and expected loss are used to rank procedures before any one sample is declared the winner. An [estimator](estimator.qmd) with [bias](bias-of-an-estimator.qmd) zero can still lose to a biased competitor on [mean squared error](mean-squared-error.qmd), because the variance term may more than pay for the bias. [Maximum likelihood](maximum-likelihood.qmd) is the construction that maximises the probability of the observed sample under a parametric model. A [sufficient statistic](sufficient-statistic.qmd) is the reduction that lets the rest of the sample be ignored for inference about the parameter. [Loss and risk](loss-and-risk.qmd) are the language in which "best" is an expected loss, and in which no estimator is best at every parameter value at once unless the parameter space has been restricted, for example to unbiased estimators.

On the prediction side, squared error selects the conditional mean and absolute error selects the conditional median. A [confidence interval](confidence-interval.qmd) and a test are the theory's interval and decision forms. Their coverage and their error rates are statements about repeated samples, which is why a single interval either covers or does not, and the guarantee is attached to the method. [Sampling distributions](sampling-distribution.qmd) are the objects those guarantees are calculated from.{{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (pp. 18–20, §2.4){{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 41, 184–185 and 452)

[Parametric statistics](parametric-statistics.qmd) is the part of the theory that begins with a finite parameter. [Statistical learning theory](statistical-learning-theory.qmd) is the part that scores a whole class of prediction rules by future prediction error. Both inherit the expected-loss comparison. They differ in whether the unknown is a short parameter or an unspecified member of a large class.{{cite:james_islr2013}} (pp. 21–22, §2.1.2){{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (p. 237, §7.9)



A numerical boundary keeps the definition attached to a calculation. List the integers from 1 through 11. Their total is 66, so their mean is 6.0. The first three integers have mean 2. The difference between the full-list mean and that three-integer mean is 4.0. The difference is a property of which units entered the average. It is not a property of the name of the field. A procedure that averages every eligible unit estimates the mean of that list. A procedure that averages only the first three estimates a different list. Statistical theory has to say which list is the target before the average is interpreted.

The same arithmetic limits a model fitted to the observed rows. The mean 6.0 describes the integers that were listed. Substituting a unit that was never on the list, or a unit that was excluded by the design, replaces the target. A [confidence interval](confidence-interval.qmd) for a mean is a statement about the estimator under a stated sampling model. A [standard error](standard-error.qmd) is the standard deviation of that estimator. Neither object repairs a list that was the wrong list. The procedures already named here include [estimator](estimator.qmd), [bias](bias-of-an-estimator.qmd), [mean squared error](mean-squared-error.qmd), [Maximum likelihood](maximum-likelihood.qmd).

Two further limits are algebraic. The sum of deviations from the mean 6.0 is zero, so the mean is the least-squares centre of these 11 numbers. A robust centre, or a median, answers a different minimisation. Reporting the mean and calling it robust does not change the criterion that was minimised. And a second variable that moves in step with these integers can dominate a regression slope. The slope then describes the joint list, which is a larger object than the one-column mean 6.0.

## Connections

The applied face of the same subject is a [regression analysis](regression-analysis.qmd) whose coefficients are interpreted as estimates, a [robust](robust-statistics.qmd) alternative when the loss or the error law is in doubt, and a [resampling](resampling-statistics.qmd) calculation when the sampling distribution has no usable formula. None of these replaces the theoretical question. Each of them is an answer to a case of it: which procedure, under which model, with what expected loss.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 452 and 463){{cite:james_islr2013}} (p. 175, Ch. 5)

## History

The method of least squares, in the early nineteenth century, gave estimation a criterion. Pearson's method of moments, at the end of that century, gave it a general construction for a parametric family. Fisher, in 1912, introduced maximum likelihood, and in 1922 set out what estimation is for: the reduction of a sample to statements about a parameter, with the language of bias, efficiency, and sufficiency that the subject still uses. Neyman and Pearson built the parallel theory of tests. The Fisher–Rao theorem, the Rao–Blackwell theorem, and the Cramér–Rao inequality are among the later sharp results of that programme, associated with Rao's work and with Fisher's. Decision theory, with an explicit loss and an expected loss, is the setting in which prediction rules are now compared.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 184–185, 372 and 450){{cite:hastie_esl2009}} (pp. 18–20, §2.4)
