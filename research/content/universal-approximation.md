## Motivating example
A straight line, with its two coefficients chosen as carefully as one likes, still cannot follow every curved response on an interval. A calibration curve, a dose response, or a probability that rises and then flattens can bend in a way no line contains. Adding nonlinear hidden units changes the menu of functions available to the model. An approximation theorem asks whether that menu can be brought arbitrarily close to a chosen target throughout a domain. The question is prior to any question about estimating weights from a finite noisy sample. Separating the two questions is what makes the theorem useful: it can dispose of a representational objection without standing in for a training method or a sample-size formula.{{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§6.4.1, pp. 194–195)

## Definition — The uniform theorem, stated once
A sigmoidal activation tends to $0$ as its argument tends to $-\infty$ and to $1$ as its argument tends to $+\infty$. The logistic function $\sigma(t)=(1+e^{-t})^{-1}$ is the usual example. It is continuous and strictly increasing, and it has those two limits. Continuity is a further requirement. The two limits by themselves do not force the graph to be continuous: a unit step has the same limits and a jump.{{cite:cybenko1989}} (§1, p. 303)

The uniform approximation theorem uses a continuous sigmoidal activation together with a linear readout. For every continuous real-valued $f$ on the cube $[0,1]^p$ and every tolerance $\varepsilon>0$, some finite width $m$ and some real coefficients satisfy
$$
\sup_{x\in[0,1]^p}\left|f(x)-\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_j\,\sigma(w_j^\top x+b_j)\right|<\varepsilon.
$$
Every hidden unit uses the same activation. The width, the directions $w_j$, the offsets $b_j$, and the output weights $a_j$ may all change when the target changes or when the tolerance is tightened. The claim is that the union, over all finite widths, of these networks is dense in the continuous functions on the cube, in the uniform norm. Each target and each tolerance is allowed its own width.{{cite:cybenko1989}} (thm. 2, pp. 307–308)

The linear readout is part of that class, not a cosmetic choice of notation. Each hidden activation is trapped between the two limits, but an output weight may be negative and may be large, so the network value is not trapped in $(0,1)$. Replace the linear readout by a final logistic sigmoid and every output lies in $(0,1)$. The distance from any such output to the constant function $2$ exceeds $1$ at every input, so the tolerance $0.5$ is unreachable. The theorem's unrestricted linear combination is what allows targets outside the range of the activation.

## Intuition — What the sigmoidal limits are for
A single hidden unit $\sigma(w^\top x+b)$ is constant on hyperplanes orthogonal to $w$. The direction $w$ selects the axis of change, and the offset $b$ slides the location of that change. The output weight then decides how that feature enters the sum.{{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (Chapter 6, pp. 164–166)

Multiplying $w$ by a large positive factor $\lambda$ makes the change abrupt: $\sigma(\lambda(w^\top x+b))$ moves most of the way from its lower plateau to its upper plateau inside a thin slab, and is nearly constant on either side. The limits $0$ and $1$ are the heights of those two plateaus, so a steep ridge is a continuous stand-in for the indicator of a half-space. A continuous sigmoidal function is discriminatory: if a finite signed measure on the cube integrates to zero against every ridge $x\mapsto\sigma(w^\top x+\theta)$, the measure itself is zero. The passage to the limit, in which a steep sigmoid approaches a step, is how the two horizontal asymptotes enter. Without two distinct finite limits, there is no half-space indicator to pass to, and this particular argument does not begin. The discriminatory property is the role of the activation condition.{{cite:cybenko1989}} (§2, Theorem 1 and Lemma 1, pp. 305–308)

The hypothesis is consequently stronger than a request that the graph look vaguely S-shaped. It asks for two different finite asymptotes, so that a steep ridge has two plateaus, and it asks for continuity, so that every finite network remains a continuous function of $x$. An activation that is bounded but does not approach two different limits need not support the same reasoning. A rectified linear unit, which increases without a finite upper limit, sits in a different family of approximation results. A guarantee proved for one activation stays with that activation.{{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§6.4.1, pp. 194–196, activation classes and depth)

## Properties — A jump cannot be a uniform limit
While the activation is continuous, every network in the class is continuous on the cube: it is a sum of compositions of continuous maps. A uniform limit of continuous functions on a compact set is continuous. Fix a candidate limit $g$ and a point $x$, choose one network within $\varepsilon/3$ of $g$ everywhere, and use continuity of that network to keep its own change below $\varepsilon/3$ on a small neighbourhood of $x$. The triangle inequality then keeps $g$ within $\varepsilon$ of $g(x)$ on that neighbourhood. A limit obtained uniformly from these networks cannot jump.

The gap is quantitative, not merely qualitative. Let $f$ equal $0$ on $[0,1/2)$ and equal $1$ on $[1/2,1]$. Suppose a continuous $F$ satisfied $|F(x)-f(x)|<1/2$ at every $x$ in $[0,1]$. Then $F(x)<1/2$ to the left of $1/2$, while $F(x)>1/2$ at $1/2$ and to the right. The left-hand limit of $F$ at $1/2$ would be at most $1/2$, but continuity would also require $F(1/2)>1/2$. The contradiction shows that no continuous function has uniform error strictly smaller than $1/2$. The uniform distance from this step to the continuous functions is at least $1/2$, and every continuous network is one of those functions.

Making the transition steeper does not close the gap. The logistic ridge $F(x)=\sigma(k(x-1/2))$ equals $1/2$ at the jump for every finite slope $k$, so its uniform error against the step is exactly $1/2$. Raising $k$ from $20$ to $100$ leaves that uniform error unchanged. What shrinks is a different set. The inputs at which the absolute error exceeds $0.05$ form an interval of length $2\log 19/k$, about $0.2944$ when $k=20$ and about $0.0589$ when $k=100$. A thin transition can have small length while the largest pointwise error remains $1/2$. Uniform approximation asks about that largest error. A small bad set is a different mode of approximation.

The discontinuous-target theorem uses the weaker mode. A continuous sigmoidal network can track a measurable target except on a set of arbitrarily small measure. The discontinuous-target theorem does not assert uniform approximation of every discontinuous function by continuous networks. A spike of height $1/2$ and vanishing width is small in measure and large in the uniform norm, and the two conclusions must not be read as one.{{cite:cybenko1989}} (thm. 3, pp. 309–310, approximation outside a small set)

## Worked example — A constructive curved approximation
For a concrete construction use the rectified linear unit $r(t)=\max(0,t)$. Linear interpolation of $f(x)=x^2$ at knots $0,1/4,1/2,3/4,1$ gives
$$F(x)=\tfrac14x+\tfrac12r(x-\tfrac14)+\tfrac12r(x-\tfrac12)+\tfrac12r(x-\tfrac34),\qquad0\le x\le1.$$
On this domain the first term is also $\tfrac14r(x)$, so all four summands can be hidden-unit outputs. The initial slope is $1/4$ and each hinge raises the slope by $1/2$, giving successive slopes $1/4,3/4,5/4,7/4$. On a knot interval $[a,b]$, the interpolation line minus $x^2$ is $(x-a)(b-x)$. Its maximum is $(b-a)^2/4$, reached at the midpoint. Here every interval has length $1/4$, so the exact uniform error is $1/64=0.015625$.

This is a fully specified network and an error certificate for one target. It illustrates the distinction between a constructive approximation and a general existence theorem. The rectified linear unit does not tend to a finite upper limit, so the certificate is not an instance of the sigmoidal theorem. Both constructions belong to broader families of approximation results, and a rate proved for one target does not transfer to the other family.{{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§6.4.1, pp. 194–196, activation classes and depth)

**Your turn.** Interpolate $x^2$ with equally spaced knots $0,1/2,1$. Find the interpolation value at $x=1/4$ and the maximum error. How many equal subintervals suffice to make this interpolation error at most $0.01$?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The first segment joins $(0,0)$ to $(1/2,1/4)$ and has value $1/8$ at $1/4$. The target is $1/16$, so the error is $1/16=0.0625$, also the maximum over both intervals. With $m$ equal subintervals, the maximum is $1/(4m^2)$. The condition $1/(4m^2)\le0.01$ gives $m\ge5$.

## Statistical use — Three errors, and no rate
Approximation error is the distance from the target to the best function the architecture can represent. Optimisation error is the further gap between that best function and the function a training run returns. Statistical error is the effect of seeing finitely many noisy pairs rather than the target itself. The uniform result speaks only to the first gap, and only as an existence statement. For each continuous target and each tolerance there is some finite network within the tolerance. The statement does not name the width as a function of the tolerance, does not bound the coefficients, and does not say how fast the error falls when the width is increased. A target with many steep bends can demand a much larger width, at the same tolerance, than a target that is nearly linear. The existence quantifiers allow exactly that dependence. Those quantifiers supply no sample size that would learn every continuous function on the cube: the cube contains continuous functions with arbitrarily fine oscillation, and a learning guarantee needs a restriction on that complexity together with a sampling model.{{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§6.4.1, pp. 194–195){{cite:goodfellow_bengio_courville2016}} (§6.4.1, approximation capacity, training and generalisation)

The piecewise-linear interpolation of $x^2$ has uniform error $1/(4m^2)$ when the interval is split into $m$ equal pieces. For that one target and that one construction, halving the tolerance multiplies the number of pieces by $\sqrt2$. The formula is a rate because the error on each piece was computed from $(b-a)^2/4$. It is not a rate read off the uniform theorem. That theorem does not mention $x^2$, does not use a rectified linear unit, and does not convert a modulus of continuity into a width. Quoting $1/(4m^2)$ as though it were the general speed of approximation would attach a special calculation to a theorem that does not contain it.

Large coefficients are a separate silence. A steep sigmoidal transition is produced by a large linear coefficient inside the activation. The existence result allows those coefficients to grow as the tolerance shrinks, and it gives no bound on the growth. In a numerical fit, large coefficients are an optimisation and stability question. The approximation theorem does not price that question, any more than it prices the number of observations.

## Assumptions and limits
The uniform result is for continuous targets on a compact cube, a continuous sigmoidal hidden activation, and a linear readout. The one-dimensional step shows why uniform approximation of a jump fails for every continuous network; that short argument does not prove density for continuous targets, and the density theorem does not prove the step bound. The measure statement for discontinuous targets is a different conclusion, with a different meaning of “close”. Dropping compactness, asking for uniform approximation of derivatives, or changing the activation each calls for a theorem whose hypotheses match the new question. Driving the measure of a bad set to zero is not the same achievement as driving the uniform error to zero, and neither theorem used here supplies a speed for either achievement.
