## Motivating example
Before a new clinic opens, a model for its daily arrivals should produce believable counts. Drawing a rate from a proposed prior and then a count from a Poisson distribution shows the model's *prior* range of possible days. After the first month of counts, drawing the rate from its posterior produces a *posterior* forecast for tomorrow. The second distribution uses what was learned, but still allows tomorrow's count to vary even if the rate were known. Looking at simulated days makes a prior or likelihood with implausible consequences easier to notice than looking at parameter symbols alone.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 1, §1.3, pp. 6–7; Ch. 6, §6.3, pp. 143–145)

## Intuition
Prediction requires two draws. First choose a plausible parameter value; then generate a new observation conditionally on it. The prior and posterior versions differ only in the distribution used for that first draw. Averaging over parameter uncertainty usually makes the predictive distribution wider than a sampling distribution evaluated at one point estimate. The variance decomposition below separates the variation of a fresh observation from remaining uncertainty about the parameter. As data accumulate, the latter may shrink without removing the former.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 1, §1.3, eqs. 1.3–1.4; Ch. 3, §3.2)

## Definition
Write $y$ for observed data and $\widetilde y$ for a future observation or replicate. In $p(\widetilde y)=\int p(\widetilde y\mid\theta)p(\theta)\,d\theta$, the prior predictive distribution is a property of the complete model *before conditioning on* $y$. In $p(\widetilde y\mid y)=\int p(\widetilde y\mid\theta,y)p(\theta\mid y)\,d\theta$, the posterior predictive distribution conditions on $y$. Replacing $p(\widetilde y\mid\theta,y)$ by $p(\widetilde y\mid\theta)$ requires future and observed data to be conditionally independent given $\theta$; time-series or spatial models may need the full conditional expression instead.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 1, §1.3, eqs. 1.3–1.4, pp. 6–7)

## Properties
The beta–binomial formula above is an instance of a more general point: marginalising a shared parameter can make future observations dependent even when they are independent conditional on that parameter. For two future Bernoulli outcomes, $\Pr(\widetilde Y_1=\widetilde Y_2=1\mid y)=\mathbb E(p^2\mid y)$, whereas multiplying two separate one-step predictive probabilities gives $\{\mathbb E(p\mid y)\}^2$. The difference is $\operatorname{Var}(p\mid y)$. A joint forecast must draw one $p$ for the entire future batch, not a new $p$ independently for each trial.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 1, §1.3, eq. 1.4; Ch. 2, §2.1, beta–binomial model)

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Proof"}
::: {.proof}
The law of total probability gives $p(\widetilde y)=\int p(\widetilde y,\theta)\,d\theta=\int p(\widetilde y\mid\theta)p(\theta)\,d\theta$. Conditional on $y$, the same factorisation gives $p(\widetilde y\mid y)=\int p(\widetilde y\mid\theta,y)p(\theta\mid y)\,d\theta$. Conditional independence yields the displayed posterior mixture. If $m(\theta)=\mathbb E(\widetilde Y\mid\theta)$ and $v(\theta)=\operatorname{Var}(\widetilde Y\mid\theta)$, applying the law of total variance under the posterior gives $\operatorname{Var}(\widetilde Y\mid y)=\mathbb E\{v(\theta)\mid y\}+\operatorname{Var}\{m(\theta)\mid y\}$. For two conditionally independent Bernoulli trials, $\mathbb E(p^2\mid y)-\mathbb E(p\mid y)^2=\operatorname{Var}(p\mid y)$. More generally, multiplying $\binom mk p^k(1-p)^{m-k}$ by a $\operatorname{Beta}(a,b)$ posterior density and integrating $p$ over $(0,1)$ gives $\binom mk B(k+a,m-k+b)/B(a,b)$, proving the beta–binomial formula above.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 1, §1.3, eqs. 1.3–1.4; Ch. 2, §2.1; Ch. 3, §3.2)
:::
:::

## Statistical use — Forecasting and checking a model
For a forecast, define precisely what will be new: one response at an existing covariate value, a whole replicate study, or a new group with a newly drawn group effect. These need different conditional sampling steps even if they share a posterior. For model checking, compare a feature of observed data—such as a minimum, a run length, or a group spread—with the same feature in replicated datasets. A posterior predictive check asks whether the fitted model can reproduce that feature. A prior predictive check instead asks whether the complete prior-and-likelihood model generated plausible data at all. An extreme discrepancy may expose a defect, but agreement on one feature does not certify the entire model.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 6, §§6.3–6.5, pp. 143–160){{cite:gelman_meng_stern1996}} (§§2.1, 4.1, pp. 737–739, 755–756)

With an improper flat prior, the prior predictive integral may not define a probability distribution at all, even when a proper posterior allows posterior prediction. If successive observations are serially dependent given the parameters, replacing $p(\widetilde y\mid\theta,y)$ by $p(\widetilde y\mid\theta)$ loses information about the latest state. In a hierarchical study, a prediction for a new observation in an existing school uses that school's effect; a new school requires drawing another effect from the population model. The phrase “future data” is insufficient until that replication scheme is specified.{{cite:gelman_meng_stern1996}} (§4.1, pp. 754–756){{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 1, §1.3; Ch. 6, §6.5)

## Worked example
Suppose $p\sim\operatorname{Beta}(2,2)$ and ten conditionally independent trials produce seven successes and three failures. The posterior is $p\mid y\sim\operatorname{Beta}(9,5)$. Before observing the trials, the predictive chance of one success was $\mathbb E(p)=2/4=0.5$; afterwards it is $\mathbb E(p\mid y)=9/14\approx0.64286$. For *two* future successes, however, the joint predictive chance is $\mathbb E(p^2\mid y)=9\cdot10/(14\cdot15)=3/7\approx0.42857$. Plugging the posterior mean into two independent Bernoulli probabilities gives $(9/14)^2\approx0.41327$, which is smaller because it omits the shared uncertainty about $p$. The prior joint chance was $2\cdot3/(4\cdot5)=0.3$. The data shift the forecast toward successes, and the distinction between joint and plug-in prediction remains even after that shift.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 1, §1.3; Ch. 2, §2.1, beta–binomial model)

**Your turn.** Start with a $\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)$ prior and observe three successes in four Bernoulli trials. Find the prior and posterior chances of success on one new trial, and the posterior chance of success on both of the next two trials.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true" title="Answer"}
The prior one-step chance is $1/2$. The posterior is $\operatorname{Beta}(4,2)$, so the posterior one-step chance is $4/6=2/3$. For two future successes, integrate the *same* $p$ over its posterior: $\mathbb E(p^2\mid y)=4\cdot5/(6\cdot7)=10/21\approx0.47619$. Squaring $2/3$ would give $4/9$ and would discard their dependence through the shared unknown $p$.
:::

## Common misconceptions
The posterior predictive distribution is not the posterior distribution of the parameter: its outcomes are future observations, and their variance includes fresh sampling noise. Plugging in a posterior mean is not generally the same as integrating over the posterior; it misses parameter uncertainty and, for a future batch, its induced dependence. A prior predictive distribution is not simply the likelihood with a “typical” prior parameter; it mixes the likelihood over the entire prior. Finally, posterior predictive agreement with an observed statistic is not proof of model correctness, because a model may reproduce one feature while missing another.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 1, §1.3; Ch. 6, §6.3){{cite:gelman_meng_stern1996}} (§1.1, pp. 733–735)

## Connections
The [posterior mean](posterior-mean.qmd) may be the centre of a forecast, but the [predictive-variance decomposition](predictive-variance-decomposition.qmd) explains why a new observation is more uncertain than the mean itself. The [beta–binomial posterior prediction](beta-binomial-posterior-prediction.qmd) evaluates the predictive integral exactly; simulation handles models where that integral has no convenient form. A [Bayesian credible interval](bayesian-credible-interval.qmd) covers a parameter under its posterior, while a predictive interval covers an as-yet unobserved outcome.{{cite:gelman_bda2013}} (Ch. 1, §1.3; Ch. 2, §2.1; Ch. 3, §3.2)

## History
Predictive distributions have long been part of Bayesian analysis, but routine simulation made their use for model assessment practical. Gelman, Meng and Stern's 1996 paper developed posterior predictive assessment through replicated data and discrepancies chosen for the scientific question; it explicitly compared the approach with Box's earlier prior predictive check. Their distinction between replicating with the same unknown parameter and drawing a new one remains important in hierarchical models.{{cite:gelman_meng_stern1996}} (§§1.1, 2.1, 4.1, pp. 733–739, 754–756)
