## Lead context

A telescope delivers a spectrum, a light curve, or a catalogue of galaxies, and the scientific question is a parameter of a physical model: a Keplerian orbit, a luminosity function, a clustering law. Astrostatistics is the statistical work of reducing those observations and of confronting them with the astrophysical model. Astroinformatics is the name used alongside it when the emphasis is the pipelines and the data systems that large surveys require. The pressure that makes the field its own is the combination of a strong physical model, a survey that did not observe every object equally, and a catalogue too large for a desktop analysis.

## Intuition

Fisher's statement of the statistician's task, which Feigelson quotes for the astronomical setting, is the reduction of data: a bulk of records that will not fit in the mind is replaced by a smaller set of quantities that still carry the relevant information. The astronomer already does the first part of that reduction as instrument work. A CCD image is flat-fielded, bias-removed, and tied to standard stars. A spectrum is extracted, cosmic-ray streaks are removed, and the counts are placed on a wavelength grid. Interferometric visibilities are transformed into an image. Those steps turn the instrument's record into a table.{{cite:feigelson2016}} (pp. 1–2, §1)

The next step is the science. A power-law relation between two quantities is a simple heuristic model. An orbit or a spectral line is a model with a physical derivation. Nonlinear regression links the reduced data to that model. When earlier studies or the astrophysical theory constrain a parameter before the new observations are used, the update is Bayesian: the posterior revises the prior. Astronomy is unusual in how hard that last comparison is pushed. Ecology, economics, and seismology collect data, and they do not generally have a physical model of the same tightness as Newtonian orbits or atomic lines. The statistical question is which procedure matches the measurement, the selection, and the model, not a private astronomy probability.{{cite:feigelson2016}} (p. 2, §1)

## Definition

Astronomers need statistical methods both to reduce data and to do the science, together with the computing that wide surveys require. The field is that cross-disciplinary practice. The methods in routine use are least squares, maximum likelihood, Bayesian calculation when a prior is available, bootstrap and other resampling when a sampling distribution is not known, and the goodness-of-fit and model-selection checks that go with them. Applied topics that come up repeatedly are nonparametric smoothing, clustering and classification, survival methods for nondetections, time series of light curves, and spatial statistics for points on the sky.{{cite:feigelson2016}} (pp. 1–2, §1){{cite:feigelson_babu2012}} (p. 2, §1)

A map from cosmological questions to statistical topics makes the scope concrete. Galaxy clustering is a spatial point process. Galaxy morphology is a regression or a mixture. A luminosity function can be a gamma distribution, and a power-law tail a Pareto distribution. Weak lensing uses density estimation and geostatistics. Faint-source detection is a false-discovery-rate problem. Supernova and quasar light curves are nonstationary time series. Comparing a survey with a simulation is uncertainty quantification. Cosmological parameters are a Bayesian inference and model-selection problem. None of these is a method invented for astronomy alone. Each is a standard method whose inputs are astronomical measurement error, selection, and a physical model.{{cite:feigelson2016}} (p. 3)

Bayesian astrostatistics is one strand with its own history inside that map. Bayesian calculations entered modern astronomical data analysis in the late 1970s, when Gull and Daniell framed image deconvolution by writing Bayes's theorem for the posterior probability of a candidate image and adopting an entropy-based prior, in a line of work motivated by Jeffreys and by Jaynes. By about 1990, analyses that reported posterior probabilities, rather than only a single maximum-entropy image, were appearing. The International Statistical Institute's Astrostatistics Committee, formed in 2010, and the first invited astrostatistics session at an ISI World Statistics Congress, in Dublin in 2011, mark the recognition of the field by a statistical society. Thomas Loredo's account of that session treats multilevel, or hierarchical, models of cosmic populations as a main direction for the Bayesian strand.{{cite:loredo2012}} (pp. 1–2, §1)

## Statistical use

Calibration and extraction are the first statistical-looking steps, and astronomers are generally practised at them. The wider toolkit is the next step. [Least squares](least-squares.qmd) and [maximum likelihood](maximum-likelihood.qmd) fit a parametric model. A [bootstrap](bootstrap.qmd) supplies a standard error when the analytic one is doubtful, and it is one of the calculations that pushes a moderate catalogue off a single desktop processor. [Survival](survival-function.qmd) methods keep upper limits from nondetections in the fit instead of dropping the objects that were not seen. [Time series](time-series.qmd) methods describe light curves, including series that are not stationary. Spatial point processes and [geostatistics](geostatistics.qmd) describe how galaxies or sources are arranged. The [false discovery rate](false-discovery-rate.qmd) is the error rate suited to a search for faint sources among many tests.{{cite:feigelson2016}} (pp. 2–3, §1)

A narrow classical suite is what many papers still use: least squares in the form going back to Legendre, a chi-squared comparison, a Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic, a principal-component analysis, a Fourier transform. Those procedures have conditions. Minimum chi-squared regression on arbitrarily binned data has ill-determined degrees of freedom. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic is less sensitive in the tails than an Anderson–Darling comparison. Using the familiar procedure outside those conditions is a methodological choice, and Feigelson treats the choice as part of the result the paper should be able to defend. The questions he lists after a fit are the practical checklist: how reliable the result is, whether a more suitable procedure was available, and whether the conclusion depends on the analysis path.{{cite:feigelson2016}} (pp. 3–4, §2)

Computation sits beside the choice of procedure. A survey pipeline is instrument-specific. The science analysis that follows may be a high-dimensional fit, a bootstrap, or a Markov chain Monte Carlo for a posterior. Volume, velocity, and variety of the data, and also the cost of the fit itself, move the work off a single workstation onto a parallel machine, sometimes with graphical processors, at the institution, at the observatory, or on a commercial cloud. The statistical model does not change when the machine changes. The model still has to say what was observed, what was missed, and which parameters the physical theory fixes in advance.{{cite:feigelson2016}} (p. 3, §1)

## Assumptions and limits

The reduction step assumes that the calibrated quantities still carry the information the science question needs. Flat-fielding and wavelength calibration are not neutral if the calibration error is correlated with the signal. A posterior is a probability for the parameter only inside the model that was written down: the sampling distribution, the selection, and the prior. Loredo's historical account separates a maximum-entropy image, which can also be obtained as a penalised likelihood, from a genuinely Bayesian report of posterior probabilities. Reporting only the single best image leaves the uncertainty unstated.{{cite:loredo2012}} (p. 2, §1){{cite:feigelson2016}} (p. 2, §1)

Survey selection is a design condition, not a detail to be patched after the catalogue is built. Objects that fall below a detection threshold are a selected subset. Treating the detected sample as a random draw from the population of interest answers a question about the detected objects. Upper limits and nondetections belong in the likelihood if the question is about the population that includes the faint objects. A hierarchical model of a cosmic population is one way to keep the measurement of each source and the distribution of the population in one calculation. That is the direction Loredo emphasises, and it fails when the catalogue stores only a point estimate and discards the likelihood.{{cite:loredo2012}} (p. 2, §1)

Training is a limit on practice. Astronomers typically take zero or one statistics course, against a long training in physics. A survey of about 1100 astronomers found that 90 percent write software, 8 percent had substantial training in software development, and 43 percent had none. Python and IDL dominate; about 6 percent preferred R. The methodological consequence is uneven use of procedures whose conditions are not the ones the data satisfy. Short tutorials have been the main remedy after the doctorate: nearly 2000 astronomers, many of them graduate students, had attended one- to five-day workshops in a dozen countries by the time of Feigelson's review. A workshop does not replace a sampling model.{{cite:feigelson2016}} (pp. 3–4, §2)

## History

Before the twentieth century, a good deal of statistical development was tied to astronomical measurement. Repeated, inconsistent observations of a length such as the solar year were debated by Greek, Arabic, and Renaissance astronomers. Many favoured the middle of the extreme values. The mean was promoted by Tycho Brahe and Galileo. In the twentieth century the methodology in ordinary use narrowed to a short list, and it was often applied outside the conditions the procedures assume. A few astronomers specialised in newer techniques. The rest used the short list.{{cite:feigelson_babu2012}} (pp. 1–2, §1)

Interest in astrostatistics and astroinformatics grew as surveys became wide and the datasets large. Papers tagged with the keywords "Methods: statistical" or "Methods: Numerical" more than doubled in a decade, to about 1000 studies a year, nearly 10 percent of the literature, in the count Feigelson gives. Societies followed: an International Astrostatistics Association linked to the International Statistical Institute, a commission of the International Astronomical Union, and interest groups in the American Statistical Association and the American Astronomical Society. The ISI invited session of 2011, and the committee formed the year before, are the institutional marks on the statistical side. The scientific content of the field remains the reduction of observations and the comparison with physical models, with the Bayesian hierarchical analysis of populations as one active research front.{{cite:feigelson2016}} (p. 4, §3){{cite:loredo2012}} (pp. 1–2, §1)
