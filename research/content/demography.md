## Lead context
A government that plans schools, pensions, or a response to an epidemic needs to know how many people there are, how old they are, and whether the next cohort will be larger. Demography is the study of human populations through events such as birth, death, and migration, measured with statistical tools that still work when the registers are incomplete.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 156) {{cite:moultrie2013}} (Ch. 1, pp. 9–16)

## Definition
The object is the human population, seen both as a biological collection of births and deaths and as a social collection of marriages, divorces, moves, and jobs. The quantities in routine use are fertility, mortality, migration, and the age pyramid that those three produce. Mathematics and statistics are the measuring instruments. The interpretation also uses the social sciences, because a change in the birth rate can be a change in behaviour as well as a change in biology.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 156)

Much of the technical work is estimation from limited or defective data. Censuses undercount, ages are misreported, and vital registration misses births and deaths. Before a rate is trusted, the age and sex counts themselves are examined. An age ratio that spikes, or a sex ratio that swings with age, is evidence about the enumeration. Cohort survival between two censuses then asks whether the people who were counted at one date are still there, older, at the next. The aim is a coherent account of fertility, mortality, and migration, not a single corrected total.{{cite:moultrie2013}} (Ch. 1, pp. 9–16) {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 156)

A [census](census.qmd) is the frame. Age and sex tabulations are checked before any rate is trusted. An age ratio compares twice the population in an age group with the sum of the populations in the neighbouring groups. For five-year groups the ratio is $100 \times 2\,{}_{n}N_x / ({}_{n}N_{x-5}+{}_{n}N_{x+5})$. If population change across age is roughly linear, and nothing unusual has happened to that cohort, the ratio should lie near 100. A spike or a hole, without a historical reason such as migration or a past calamity, is evidence about the count itself: undercount, or people moved into a neighbouring age. An error in one group disturbs the ratios on either side.{{cite:moultrie2013}} (Ch. 1, pp. 9–12)

## Properties
The sex ratio is the number of males per 100 females, overall and at each age. Because female mortality is typically lower than male mortality, the ratio should reflect that difference: it declines slowly from birth through middle age unless migration is large, and it falls faster at older ages as male mortality pulls ahead. A sharp drop among young adults, followed by a recovery, is the pattern associated with sex-selective labour migration. The overall ratio also depends on the age structure, lower in an older population and higher in a younger one. These are checks on the enumeration, not yet estimates of a demographic rate.{{cite:moultrie2013}} (Ch. 1, pp. 9–12)

Cohort survival uses two censuses. A group aged $x$ to $x+n$ at the first census is a cohort. If the second census is taken $a$ years later, the survivors are $a$ years older. The cohort survival ratio is the later count of that cohort divided by the earlier count. Computed for one cohort it often says little. Compared across ages and between the sexes, the pattern of the ratios is informative. Deaths reduce the cohort. Migration can add or remove people, and a population that includes or excludes the military at one census and not the other cannot be compared without an adjustment. Where migration is small, the ratio is a mortality summary, and it can be set beside the survival a [life table](survival-function.qmd) would have predicted. A ten-year span is the typical one when censuses follow the interval the United Nations recommended.{{cite:moultrie2013}} (Ch. 1, pp. 14–16)

Intercensal growth is a coarser check on the same counts. If $N(t_1)$ and $N(t_2)$ are the total populations at two dates, the growth rate is $\ln(N(t_2)/N(t_1))/(t_2-t_1)$. A rate that approaches or exceeds the range explained by the country's fertility and mortality, without large migration, raises the question whether a census was in error. An average annual growth above about 3.5 percent would require a very high birth rate and a very low death rate, and is unlikely from natural increase alone. Successive censuses make the comparison sharper, because the growth rates themselves can be compared.{{cite:moultrie2013}} (Ch. 1, p. 14)

Fertility, mortality, and migration are the three processes Dodge names as the ordinary subject matter, together with the age pyramid they produce. A rate built on a distorted age pyramid inherits the distortion, which is why the age-ratio and sex-ratio checks come first. Cohort survival then ties two censuses together: the change in a cohort's size is a mixture of deaths and net migration, and only the comparison across ages separates a mortality pattern from a single bad count. These are the properties of the field when the registers are incomplete. Each summary has to be consistent with the others, and a projection that pushes the rates forward is only as good as that consistency and as the assumption that the rates continue.{{cite:moultrie2013}} (Ch. 1, pp. 9–16) {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 156)

[Sampling](stratified-and-cluster-sampling.qmd) is the alternative when a full census is impossible. The same mean, proportion, and life table then carry [standard errors](standard-error.qmd). A population projection pushes the rates forward. It is only as good as the rates and the assumption that they continue. Dodge's definition includes that forward look: once the data are in hand, predictions become possible. The prediction is a demographic statement, not a promise.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 156)

## History
John Graunt's 1662 study of the London bills of mortality is the usual starting point. He estimated family size, the importance of migration, and the structure of the population from a list of deaths by cause, working with William Petty. Through the eighteenth century those calculations were refined. In the nineteenth century European states and the United States took national censuses and kept continuous registers of births, marriages, and deaths. Differences in survival between regions became visible in those registers and were used as arguments for better work and sanitation. Using the gathered data, predictions became possible, and the first specialist journals appeared around the end of that century, among them *Demography*, *Population*, and *Population Studies*. Around the middle of the twentieth century the growth of populations in poorer countries pulled the subject toward global totals and toward methods that do not require a complete register.{{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 156)

The later technical response to incomplete registers is to treat the census as something to be checked before it is used. Age ratios near 100, sex ratios that follow the mortality difference between women and men, and cohort survival ratios that make sense across age are the checks Chapter 1 writes down. They are the first tools, because every later rate depends on the counts.{{cite:moultrie2013}} (Ch. 1, pp. 9–16) {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 156)

## Connections
[Epidemiology](epidemiology.qmd) inherited the bills of mortality and still shares the life table, but it asks about a particular illness and its causes. Dodge dates the start of biostatistics to the same Graunt and Petty analyses, applied to death rates, birth rates, and censuses. [Actuarial science](actuarial-science.qmd) uses the same mortality schedule to price a pension or a life annuity. [Biostatistics](biostatistics.qmd) shares the survival model when the population is a group of patients rather than a nation. Official statistics is the producer of the census and the vital register on which all three depend.{{cite:dodge2008}} (pp. 49, 156)

A cohort survival ratio is a demographic use of the idea that the same people are observed twice. It is not a [cohort study](cohort-study.qmd) of an exposure and a later disease, though the arithmetic of a closed group ageing forward is shared. The demographic ratio is a check on counts and a summary of mortality and migration. The epidemiological cohort is a design for a contrast. Keeping the two apart prevents a census comparison from being read as if an exposure had been assigned.{{cite:moultrie2013}} (Ch. 1, pp. 14–16) {{cite:dodge2008}} (p. 156)
